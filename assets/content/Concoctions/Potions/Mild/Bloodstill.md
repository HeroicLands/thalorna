A thick, rusty-brown paste with the consistency of honey, this coagulant smells of iron and herbs—specifically of dried yarrow and powdered iron filings mixed with rendered fat. The texture is deliberately sticky, designed to adhere to a wound without running or washing away. When pressed against an open injury, the paste warms slightly, as though responding to the body's own heat, and the wound itself seems to relax around the application, the tissues accepting the medicine rather than rejecting it.

Applied as a poultice to a single bleeding wound, the mild coagulant grants an SV bonus to the injury's Bleeding Rolls. The effectiveness lasts for approximately ten minutes before the paste loses its potency and begins to dry—a window during which the body's own clotting mechanisms are significantly enhanced. Physicians and field herbalists prize this preparation for its accessibility and reliability; it works quietly and without drama, simply aiding the body to do what it already knows how to do. For a deep cut on an arm, a leg laceration, or a puncture wound that would otherwise bleed the victim toward dangerous weakness, the mild coagulant is often the critical difference between survival and slow death.

Coagulants are standard stock in any well-maintained caravan's medical supplies, in the kits of traveling healers, and in the careful stores of soldiers and adventurers who have learned the value of stem a wound early. The cost is modest enough that replacing a used batch is no great hardship for anyone who can afford to adventure at all.
