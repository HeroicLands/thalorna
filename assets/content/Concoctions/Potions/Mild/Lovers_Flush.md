This reddish tincture swirls with flecks of gold when held to the light, the glass warm to the touch even when freshly poured. The scent is heady – rose petals steeped in wine, with undertones of cardamom and clove that linger long after the stopper is drawn. On the tongue, it tastes both sweet and slightly bitter, coating the mouth with a warmth that spreads downward to the chest. The preparation carries a subtle glamour, the kind that makes those who hold it feel momentarily more confident, more luminous.

For four hours after drinking the mild aphrodisiac, the imbiber finds their seductive overtures met with diminished resistance. Where ordinary Charm might founder, the potion grants its drinker a penalty to their target's ability to refuse – a straightforward SV reduction in the strength of Charm resistance tests. The effect is neither magical compulsion nor violation of will, merely a subtle warming of the blood and clarification of desire, as though a fog had lifted from judgment. Those who benefit from the preparation report a delightful feeling of looseness, a confidence that can translate convincingly to charm and persuasion.

Mild aphrodisiacs are bought by courtesans, merchants seeking to finalize deals through favorable personal terms, and lovers of modest means seeking to rekindle passion in their relationships. Its cost is low enough that it appears in many apothecary stalls and tavern proprietors' private stocks. The potion walks a thin line between folk medicine and moral concern in many regions, treated variously as a legitimate enhancement or a suspect indulgence depending on local custom.
