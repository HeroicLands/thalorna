Like distilled midnight mixed with fire, this preparation is nearly black, tinged with deep purple at the edges of the vial when held before candlelight. The merest hint of the stopper releases a fragrance so overwhelming that it seems to fill an entire room—flowers that only bloom at midnight, musk and civet, the faint burned-honey warmth of amber that costs more than common soldiers earn in a year. Even breathing the air near the vial affects the body; the drinker's fingers tremble slightly as they raise the cup to their lips, their senses already primed before the liquid ever touches their tongue. The few who taste it report that the sensation borders on rapture, every nerve alive with urgent hunger.

For the full four hours of the great aphrodisiac's presence in the bloodstream, its quadrupled penalty—SV ×4—renders resistance nearly impossible. Targets of the drinker's seductive overtures find their will and judgment simply absent, their bodies and emotions responding to stimuli that ordinary people might deflect without difficulty. The great aphrodisiac is less a persuasion and more a comprehensive rewiring of desire itself; those who experience its effects often struggle to explain afterward why they acted against their own presumed interests. The potion does not create love or even genuine affection, but it creates something far more mechanically powerful: an utter inability to refuse.

The great aphrodisiac is forbidden in most organized societies, treated alongside the blackest poisons and the most vicious curses as a weapon against human autonomy. It is nonetheless available to those with extraordinary wealth, through the networks of hedge-herbalists and unscrupulous alchemists who ask no questions about the ethics of their work. Its existence is treated as an open secret, whispered of with mingled revulsion and fascination.
