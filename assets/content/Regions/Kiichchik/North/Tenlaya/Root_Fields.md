## Overview

Come over the western passes in late spring and the first thing you see below you is blue: whole meadows of small blue flowers, acres of them, in the wet hollows of a sagebrush plateau, with women and children bent over them digging with sticks. The flowers grow from a bulb the size of a thumb, sweet when it is baked in a pit for two days, and the meadows are why the western bands call their country Potonta Yunto, the Root Fields. It is the dry country of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Tenlaya]], the Grass People, beyond the mountains: plateaus of sage and bunchgrass, river gorges a thousand feet deep, and basins to the south where the rivers run into salt lakes and vanish. About 60,000 people live in it, all in bands.

## The Land

The plateaus lie between the western mountains of the [[place-wehioshtergn|Cairn Plain]] and the coast range, high, dry and open, with the snow-peaks standing above them on both sides. Rivers cross them in deep gorges of dark rock, and the greatest of them runs west through a canyon to the sea. The weather is harder than on the plains: hot and dusty in summer, bitterly cold in winter, with little rain in any season, and what grows is sage, bitterbrush, bunchgrass and, wherever there is water in spring, the root meadows.

South of the plateaus lie the basins, wide flats between bare ranges, where the rivers end in shallow lakes that are fresh in spring and salt by autumn. The bands of the basins are fewer and poorer than those of the plateau. They live on roots, seeds, waterfowl, rabbits taken in great nets, and the fish of the lakes, and they come north to the falls in good years.

## The Western Bands

The western bands speak the Tenlaya tongue with a drawl of their own and a few words for fish and roots that the plains do not know. They hunt bison only where small herds come through the passes, and they live on three harvests instead: roots in spring, salmon in summer and autumn, and game in winter. In winter they live in round lodges dug half into the ground and roofed with poles and earth, close to the rivers, which the plains bands find strange, since these are earth lodges and the western bands are not villagers.

The western bands come east to the summer gathering on the Cairn Plain once in a few years and are made welcome. The bands of the plains count them as kin, if odd ones. A plains hunter who winters with them comes home talking about fish.

## The Falls

The richest place in the Root Fields, and in the whole west of the Grass People's country, is [[place-hiyespito|Hiye Spito]], Falling Water, where the great river drops over a ledge of black rock into a narrow channel. Every summer the salmon run up the river by the hundreds of thousands, and at the falls they bunch below the ledge and leap. The bands build wooden platforms out over the water and take the fish with dip nets on long poles, and for three months the camp beside the falls is the largest gathering of people anywhere west of the mountains.

## Borders and Neighbors

West, beyond the coast range and its passes, lies the cedar coast of the [[affiliation-kelwanuk|Cedar People]], whose traders come over the passes every summer for robes, roots and dried meat, and bring shell, dried salmon of the coast and carved horn. East, the western mountains and their passes lead back to the plains. South, past the basins, lies the mesa country of the [[affiliation-teyumi|Turquoise People]]. North, the plateau climbs into forested mountains where few bands go.

## What Moves Through It

The Root Fields are the meeting place of the plains and the coast. Robes and bison meat come over the passes from the east, shell and coastal goods over the coast range from the west, and both meet at the falls, where the western bands trade them on with their own dried salmon and roots. Obsidian from the volcanic country to the south goes east to the plains as arrowheads. A traveler going from the Grass People to the Cedar People or back goes through the falls in summer or not at all, because the passes close in winter.
