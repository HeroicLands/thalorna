## Overview

Walk west across the open grass for a week and the ground drops away without warning into a valley two or three miles wide, full of cottonwood, willow and ash, with a broad brown river wandering through it. Above the floodplain on both sides, on the terraces where the floods never reach, stand the villages: low domes of earth inside ditches and palisades, smoke rising from their tops, and fields of corn, beans, squash and sunflowers below them on the bottomland. This is the Village River, Hesun Shewan, the great river of the plains, and the farming heart of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Tenlaya]], the Grass People. About 70,000 people live along it, some 30,000 in its villages and the rest in the bands that winter in its bottoms.

## The River

The river comes down out of the north-west, from the far mountains, and runs south-east through the middle of the [[place-tenlayargn|Tenlaya Region]] for a month's walk before it joins the great river of the [[affiliation-tokchiaska|One Fire]]. It is wide, shallow, full of sandbars and shifting channels, and the color of the plains it drains. In spring the ice breaks up with a noise like thunder and comes down in a flood of floes and drowned bison, and the villages turn out on the banks to haul the carcasses in with poles. In summer the river drops and the sandbars stand up out of it. In winter it freezes hard enough to walk on.

The valley is the only timber on the plains for days in any direction, and everything the villages build comes out of it: the four great posts of an earth lodge, the palisade logs, the bull boats of willow and bison hide that carry a family across the river, and the firewood for the winter. The bands come down off the grass into its bottoms every winter for the shelter and the wood, and the cottonwood bark feeds their dogs when the meat runs short.

## The Villages

Some forty villages stand along the river and its larger tributaries, each on its own terrace, each a day or two from the next. The largest is [[place-tansheyawa|Tanshe Yawa]], Bison Ford, in the middle reach, where the herds cross and the bands come in each autumn for the trading under the [Corn Pipe]{.name data-type="miscgear"}. Upstream, where the river comes out of rougher country, [[place-kopenmokin|Kopen Mokin]], Flint Hill, digs the best flint on the plains out of the bluffs above it. Downstream, where the eastern river joins it and the One Fire's country begins, [[place-henushewan|Henu Shewan]], the Forks, keeps the deepest ditch on the river and takes the counter-raids first.

A traveler going downstream sees the villages change. Upstream they are open, with shallow ditches and lodges scattered along the terraces. Toward Tanshe Yawa the ditches deepen and the palisades double. Below it, every village is a fort, with bastions on its corners and a second ditch outside the first, and the fields are worked by women with men standing watch on the bluffs. The difference is the raids, and the villagers know exactly how far down the river the One Fire's war parties come.

Half a day below Tanshe Yawa, on a terrace nobody farms, lie the ruins of the [Burned Village]{.name data-type="place/site"}. Its ditch still shows in the grass, and nobody camps inside it. Travelers going down the river pass it in silence and do not stop.

## Living on the River

A village family eats from three sources: its fields, the river and the bands. The women plant in spring with bone hoes, hoe through the early summer, and harvest from the green corn to the first frost, then dry and shell the corn and store it in the cache pits under their lodges. The men hunt along the valley, fish with traps of willow, and take what the river brings them. In midsummer most of a village goes out onto the grass to a hunting camp and lives like a band for a month. In autumn the bands come in with their meat and robes, and the villages trade corn for them.

A good year fills the cache pits for two winters. A bad one, a dry summer or a hailstorm, leaves a village depending on what it stored the year before and on what it can buy from the bands. The villages remember the bad years better than the good, and every lodge owner keeps a second year's seed corn at the bottom of her deepest pit, which nobody touches except to plant.

## What Moves Along It

The river is the plains' road. Bull boats carry corn and robes from village to village, and the villages trade with each other by marriage and gift more than by bargaining. Flint goes downstream from Flint Hill, and at Tanshe Yawa it meets the robes and hides of the bands, the tobacco grown in every village, and the occasional southern good come up the trails from the [[affiliation-teyumi|Turquoise People]]. Robes, corn and flint go on down the river to the One Fire's western towns, in trade in a good year and as plunder in a bad one. In the far north-west, where the river comes out of the parkland, the northern bands go on to trade at the edge of the forest with the [Spruce People]{.name data-type="lore/culture"}, and furs and birch-bark canoes come back down to the villages.

## The Present Trouble

Last autumn a war party of the Coyote band came home from the One Fire's western towns with robes and captives, and in midwinter the One Fire answered by burning a third of the lodges of the Forks. The lodge owners of the Forks have said that they will not open their cache pits to the Coyote band at this autumn's trading, Corn Pipe or no Corn Pipe, and every village on the river is waiting to see what the bundle keeper of Tanshe Yawa says about it.
