## Overview {#overview}

The Written Wall, Sh'ulak P'ilam, is the southern cliff of a lone mesa in the western badlands of [[place-chakultzklrgn|Ch'akul Tsa'kul Region]]. From a day away it looks like any red cliff with a pale band across it. Closer, the band is writing: the whole face of the cliff, a quarter of a mile long and taller than a temple, is cut with signs in straight rows from the scree at its foot to the rim. A stair cut into the rock climbs the cliff at its eastern end, and on the mesa top stands a roofless hall of fallen slabs.

## The Signs

Each sign sits in its own square hollow cut a hand deep into the rock, and the hollows stand in rows as even as a scribe's ruled lines, with a deeper groove between every few dozen. The signs are made of short straight cuts and small round drillings, some no more than a few strokes, some crowded, and many of them come again and again along a row. No sign is a picture. The scribes of the south who have copied them say they are not glyphs of any kind they know, and that they cannot tell where a word begins or which way a row is read.

The signs near the foot of the cliff are worn almost away by blown sand. Those high on the face are as sharp as the day they were cut, and the rows near the rim are cut smaller and closer, as if the cutters were running out of cliff.

## The Stair and the Hall

The stair is the only way up. It climbs the eastern end of the cliff in a straight line, too steep for a laden porter, its treads cut shallow and sloping out, worn smooth and crumbling at the edges. At the top, the mesa is flat and bare, and the hall stands at its southern edge, above the writing: four long walls of standing slabs, most of them fallen inward, each slab cut on both faces with more rows of the same signs. Among the fallen slabs lie broken pots painted with black and white bands.

## Dangers

The hollows are the dens of the desert's scorpions, pale ones as long as a finger, whose sting brings fever and sometimes death. There are thousands of them, one or more in nearly every hollow, and they come out at dusk and cover the cliff. A traveler who copies the signs by touch, or climbs the face to reach the high rows, puts his hands into their dens.

The stair is the second danger, and a fall from it is a fall of the whole height of the cliff. There is no water on the mesa or within a day of it, and no shade but what the fallen slabs give.

## What Is Found There

The signs themselves are the treasure. Scribes and priests of the south pay well for careful copies on bark paper, and a copy of a whole row is worth a porter's load of cacao in the markets of the south. The painted pots in the hall are whole often enough to be worth carrying. Some of the guides of the badlands say a party should copy only from the ground, by day, and touch nothing.

# The Truth of the Written Wall {#truth .secret}

The signs are writing, and no one in the setting can read them. They follow rules a careful copyist can find: a sign stands alone or in fixed pairs, certain signs come only at the end of a group, and the deep grooves fall where one passage ends and the next begins. That is as far as study goes, and no scholar, priest or spell gets any further.

The scorpions are ordinary desert scorpions in extraordinary numbers. The rock holds the day's heat into the night, and they live on the insects the warm cliff draws.

> [!TIP]
> A party that comes in the cold months finds the scorpions slow and sheltered deep in their hollows, and the climb and the copying are far safer. A party in the hot months meets them by the thousand.
