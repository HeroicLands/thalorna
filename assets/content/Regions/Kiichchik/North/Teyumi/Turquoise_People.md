## Overview

The Teyumi are the Turquoise People, the nation of the desert's northern rim, about 150,000 people in towns of stone great houses across the mesas and canyons of the [[place-teyumirgn|Teyumi Region]]. They take their name from turquoise (_teyu_), which they hold to be rain made stone. The south calls them _Ishtem Tsul_, the Bear People, after the bears painted on the walls of their chambers, where the bear (_matsau_) is a healer and a guardian. Their ways are set out in the [[lore-teyumiclt|Teyumi]] culture note, and their tongue is the [[skill-teyumilng|Teyumi Language]].

A great house (_hosa_) is a terraced block of stone and adobe, four or five stories high at the back and stepping down to a walled plaza, with hundreds of rooms. Some are laid out so that the midwinter sunrise or the moon's farthest rising strikes a marked wall. Straight roads run between them over the tableland, and climb the cliffs by cut stairways. On the uplands the towns dry-farm corn, beans and squash; in the lowlands toward the desert they lead river water over their fields through canals (_kenuva_).

The nation holds three countries and the mesa land between them. The [[place-hosaoyalargn|Canyon of Houses]] at its heart is the mother country, where [[place-yaahosa|Yaahosa]], the Grandmother House, holds the canyon feast and [[place-hepuhosa|Hepuhosa]], the Door House, keeps the oath, with the turquoise diggings of [[place-teyupotama|Teyu Potama]] in the hills to the north. The [[place-wahaulahuvorgn|Cave Mesas]] to the north are high, wet and troubled, and their towns have moved into the alcoves of the canyon walls; the outlier of [[place-yaluhepu|Yaluhepu]], the Moon Door, watches the moon from a ridge at their eastern end. The [[place-kenuvasokorgn|Canal Plain]] to the southwest is the hot lowland of the canals, and [[place-kenuvahosa|Kenuvahosa]], the Canal House, is the largest town in the nation. Their faith is the [[lore-lumaponu|Rain Road]].

## Who Rules

The great house rules its town, and the rain rites rule the great house. Each town's great house is kept by one lineage, the great-house kin (_hosayo_), and that lineage gives the town both its rulers. The rain chief (_lumamota_) keeps the town's chief rain rites and orders its year: when it plants, when it dances, whom it expels. His sister or his mother is the house head (_hosamota_), who keeps the storerooms and decides who is fed. The two argue their disagreements inside the lineage, and the town hears the result.

Below them, every adult is an initiate (_peluyo_) of at least one chamber (_pelu_), the round rooms sunk into the plaza where the rites are held. The chamber elders (_pelumota_) teach the songs, lead the rites, and settle most quarrels inside their own chambers. A sun watcher (_waimayo_) stands at a fixed place each morning, reads where the sunrise falls along the rim, and names the days on which everything else happens. The clowns (_pukiya_) mock wrongdoers in the plaza at the summer dances, and their mockery is the first punishment a town gives.

The authority is real because the storerooms are. A great house that feeds its town in a dry year keeps its people and draws families from the canyons around it. One whose storerooms run empty loses them, and its rain chief's rites are doubted with it. Every house head swears, on taking her office, that her storerooms open before her chambers close. The oath comes from the [Year of Shut Doors]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, when the great houses of the canyon kept their corn for the rites and the hungry starved below their walls.

## How the Rulers Are Chosen

The women of the great-house kin choose both rulers, and the town does not vote. When a house head dies, the senior women of the lineage meet in her rooms and choose her successor from among her daughters and her sisters' daughters, usually the eldest who has kept a household of her own. The new house head swears her oath at the next new moon, in her own plaza or in the doorway of the Door House.

A rain chief is chosen as a boy. The house head picks a boy of the lineage, her son or her sister's son, at his initiation, and the rain chief teaches him the rain songs for twenty years. When the rain chief dies, the boy he taught succeeds him if the women of the lineage agree, and they usually do, because nobody else knows the songs. A rain chief is never deposed. A town whose rain fails tells him so through the clowns, who may mock even a rain chief by name at the summer dances, and a rain chief mocked two summers running goes down into his chamber and leaves the town's year to the boy he has trained.

## War and the Ladders

The Turquoise People keep war apart from the rest of life, because a killing dries the heart of the killer and of the town. Each great house has a war chief (_tahemota_), an initiate of the warriors' chamber named by the rain chief. He has no authority in peace. When the signal fire is lit, the rain chief hands him the town: he draws up the ladders, sets the men on the roofs and the walls, and sends the runners (_ponuyo_) to the next town. When the war party has gone, the ladders come down, the war chief gives the town back, and he and every man who killed go four days unseen in a room of the great house before they may enter any other chamber.

The fire keepers (_kowayo_) make the system work. Every high mesa within sight of another has a signal station, a ring of stone with a heap of dry wood kept under brush, and a family of the nearest town keeps it, as a duty passed from mother to daughter with the house. A fire lit at [[place-kwatuhosa|Kwatuhosa]] reaches the Canyon of Houses in an hour and the Moon Door before dark.

## The Towns Together

No town commands another. The towns of different canyons are bound by their roads, by marriages between their clans, and by the canyon feast each autumn at Hosa Oyala, the Canyon of Houses, where the first great houses of the north stand and where every town sends pilgrims. Quarrels between towns are settled at the feast, when they are settled at all. When a southern war party comes out of the desert, the towns act together because they must: signal fires on the mesa tops carry the warning north in an hour, and every great house draws up its ladders.

In the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]], the Turquoise People are represented by great-house kinsmen and a chamber elder. They are the compact's early warning, and they ask the other nations for warriors whenever the south comes. The other nations suspect them of being too close to the south, because they hold the desert trade and know the southern traders by name.

The canyon feast chooses the delegates each year, a great-house kinsman of a canyon house and one of an outlying town, with a chamber elder to keep them honest, and they walk a month to the [Red Quarry]{.name data-type="place/site"}. They come with one demand, every summer: that a southern war party is everyone's danger. Three years ago, when a war party was sighted at the desert's edge, the Turquoise People sent Pipe Bearers to every nation. The Grass People smoked and sent warriors, who arrived after the war party had turned back into the desert. The Maple People did not smoke. The towns have not forgotten either answer.

## The Northern Gate

Every trader and pilgrim who survives the crossing of [[place-chakultzklrgn|Ch'akul Tsa'kul]] comes first to [[place-kwatuhosa|Kwatuhosa]], the South House. Its Keeper of the South Road (_kwatuyo_) receives them, decides what goes north and at what price, and hears what they say of the south. The southern goods are few and precious: cacao, drunk by elders at the great feasts; scarlet macaws, kept alive in the great house plazas for the feathers the cloud dancers wear; and cast copper bells, worn at the dancers' knees. Turquoise goes south in return. The Turquoise People pass the southern goods on to the other nations at their own price.

To the west, over the mountains and a trail of desert springs, the towns' traders go to the [[affiliation-shuqash|Shell-Bead People]] of the western coast for shell beads and abalone shell, which the cloud dancers wear beside the macaw feathers. They pay in turquoise and cotton cloth, and they press the coast's chiefs to join the compact. The chiefs have not joined.

The rain dances cannot be danced without the macaw feathers, and some elders say the people are sending their rain away in turquoise to buy the masks they call it with. The word for the Keeper of the South Road means, in plain speech, the southerner.

## Drought

Drought is the Turquoise People's great fear. A town's fields, its canals and the corn in its great house all depend on the rain, and the rites of the chambers exist to call it. The towns hold that rain comes to hearts at peace, and a dry year ends with a search for the dry hearts (_pesatasi_) that are keeping it away. Those found are put out as the Dry (_pesayo_), by the rain chief, on the word of the bear doctors (_matsauyo_) after a [Dry Search]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}. The last three summers have been short of rain, and the rain chief of the Grandmother House has asked for a Dry Search at this autumn's canyon feast, the first there since the Year of Shut Doors.

The dry years are already pressing the towns against one another. Hungry families from the Cave Mesas are walking south to be fed at the Door House, the canal towns are quarreling over water turns, and a southern trader has wintered at the Canal House without ever going on to Kwatuhosa, and the Keeper of the South Road has sent a runner to ask why.
