## Overview {#overview}

Every morning a road priest climbs the pyramid of [[lore-xilanixlandty|Shilan Ish'lan]] and calls the day, and on the wharves below the boatmen listen for one word. On a day the Walker of Paths has opened, the canoes push off. On a day he has closed, nobody launches, and the cargo stays in the warehouses at a fee. Ne' P'ashel, Quetzal Water, is the market town of about 18,000 where three highland rivers meet the great river that runs down through the jungle to the coast, and it is the busiest place in [[affiliation-ixbaltzaku|Ish'bal Tsa'ku]] after the port at [[place-tzalamne|Ts'alam Ne']].

## The Town

Ne' P'ashel climbs a series of terraces above the junction, on both banks, joined by stone causeways and timber bridges built high enough to clear the flood of the rainy months. The pyramid of the Walker of Paths stands on the highest terrace and is seen in the water from every wharf.

The town is two halves, as every town of the realm is. The upper half holds the north-facing terraces and the upriver wharves, where the llama-trains come down from the mountain passes and from [[place-nekiahkul|Ne' Ki'ahkul]] with metalwork, wool and stone, and unload into the warehouses. The lower half holds the southern quarter and the downriver wharves, where the canoes come up from the jungle with cacao, feathers, pelts, rubber and medicines. The great market lies between them on the middle terraces, and the stalls there sell everything the two halves bring: textiles, dyes, preserved food, pottery, cord, and the cacao beans that small buyers use as change.

## Who Governs

Each half has its own lord, its own kin groups and its own wharves. The lord of the upper half, the Ts'ikmah, takes precedence and speaks first at the rites, and his kin groups are mostly highlanders who load and drive the trains. The lord of the lower half, the Ch'almah, leads the kin groups of the river, who crew the canoes and work the downriver wharves. The present Ch'almah is [K'ushi Ts'alam]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a broad-shouldered woman who was a canoe-steerer before she was raised, and who still takes a paddle on the river at the great festivals.

The town's wealth is the toll. Every cargo that passes the junction pays a share of itself, counted out in kind on the wharf where it lands: a share for the temples, a share for the Ts'enmah's storehouse, and a share for the half that owns the wharf. The priestly assessors stand on every wharf and value each load, and the cord-keepers knot every cargo, its owner and its toll into the town's _k'otsel_. The inspectors sent by the Ts'enmah read those cords against the storehouses, and a half whose cords and stores disagree loses its lord.

## The Wharf Quarrel

A cargo pays its wharf's half, so the two halves compete for every load. A highland merchant whose goods are bound for the coast can unload upriver, under the upper half, and carry them across the market to a downriver canoe, or he can have them taken by canoe straight to a lower-half wharf and pay there. The halves' boatmen meet the trains and canoes at the edge of town with offers, and the fights between them are the commonest trouble the town has. The lords settle most of them over a meal. A few go to the inspectors, and the inspectors settle them by counting.

## Trade and Travel

Ne' P'ashel is the most mixed town of the south. Highlanders, river people and coastal traders live side by side, many speak three or four tongues, and a merchant's skill and credit count for more on the middle terraces than his birth does. The priesthood still takes its levy from every transaction, and no large sale is made without an assessor present.

The road priests are the town's busiest clergy. They call the day, bless every canoe that leaves, and keep the shrines on the river trail down to the coast. Travelers on the rivers make offerings to Shilan Ish'lan at the wharf shrine before they push off, and canoe crews who reach Ne' P'ashel on a closed day tie up at the town's edge and wait there for the next opened one.
