## Overview {#overview}

Below the eastern wall of [[place-tzalamtzltl|Ts'alam Ts'ultel]] the mountain falls away in terraces, field below field of maize and potatoes held up by walls of fitted stone, down into the cloud. Halfway down them, on a shelf of its own, stands a shrine with no roof, and in its floor lies the Rain-Drum. The terrace families call it Ne'chan Ch'umel, the voice of the rain, and nobody in the fortress calls it anything else.

It is a disc two paces across that rises out of the shrine floor. It is not set into the floor. The gray runs on under the flagstones as part of one body, and the disc is the part of it that shows: a shallow dome with no seam and no rim, smooth and even gray, with no vein or figure in it. It is cool to the hand in the warmest month and gives a little under a palm, and comes back.

## The Note

At every planting the eldest of the terrace families climbs to the shrine at dawn and strikes the disc once with a wooden mallet. It gives one flat note with no echo, a sound more felt in the chest than heard, and the families plant that day. The note has always been the same. For the last two plantings it has not: it is duller, shorter and harder to feel, though the disc looks and feels exactly as it always has.

Nothing else touches it. It has never been marked by the mallet, cracked by frost or moved by anything, and two generations of the families have tried to lift it out to see what lies beneath. It does not come up. The shrine's walls are scratched over with the names of those who struck it and the counts of their harvests, and the scratching stops at the disc's edge.

## Who Keeps It

The shrine belongs to the kin groups of the fortress's lower half, who hold the eastern terraces in the realm's name and farm them in their labor turn. A shrine-keeper from among them sweeps it and keeps the mallet, and the terrace families decide together who strikes the disc. The fortress's priests count the Rain-Drum a custom of the terraces and send nobody to it.

The terrace families hold that the note calls the rain. In their account, the first planting on the eastern slope was made in a drought, and the rain came on the afternoon of the first stroke and has come every planting since. A dull note, to them, means a dry year, and the last two have frightened them more than any raid.

## Hooks {#hooks}

- **The steel disc.** The shrine-keeper means to have a disc of steel cast at [[place-nekiahkul|Ne' Ki'ahkul]], laid over the Rain-Drum and beaten instead, so that the note rings true again. The terrace families want nothing laid on it at all. A party carrying goods up the pass is asked to bring the steel up, and asked by the families to lose it on the way.
- **The two years.** The rains at the last two plantings were one good and one poor. Each side of the quarrel tells the party the year that proves its point, and each wants the party to say so to the lord of the lower half.
- **The slide.** A terraced slope across the valley came down in the last rains and buried a village's fields. The eastern families want the Rain-Drum struck out of season, which has never been done, and the shrine-keeper refuses.

# The Truth of the Rain-Drum {#truth .secret}

The Rain-Drum is working badly. It is gray and gives under the hand all over, so it is not dead, and the change is in the note alone. Nothing about the disc has altered, and nothing anyone does to it changes the note.

The fortress's cords record one more thing, which nobody in the fortress has set beside the note. Every terraced slope within a day's walk of the pass has slid at least once in the four centuries the cords cover. The eastern slope below the shrine never has. Whether the disc has anything to do with that cannot be established. The cords of the rains show no pattern that follows the note.

The steel disc would change nothing about the Rain-Drum. It cannot be fixed to it, and the note of the steel is only the note of steel.

> [!TIP]
> The quarrel is the scene, and the party never learns what the disc does. A party that reads the cords for itself and finds the eastern slope's record holds a fact that neither side of the quarrel can use, because neither will believe a stranger who says it.
