## Overview {#overview}

The eastern stair of the westernmost pyramid of [[place-nechakalkul|Ne' Ch'akalkul]] does not begin on the plaza. It begins on the Smooth Step, Pu'talal Ts'elun: a low gray swelling, waist-high at its edge and chest-high at its crown, that comes up out of the limestone at the pyramid's foot. The first cut step of the stair is laid against its far side, and the pyramid's lowest course bends around it. Every other building in the city is square, and the curve in that course is the first thing a visitor's eye catches.

The Step is one piece, with no seam, no joint and no crack. Its top swells and hollows in long true runs, like the back of a great shell, and there is no straight edge anywhere on it. It is a smooth, even gray, unlike the pale limestone around it, and cool to the hand at noon. It gives a little under a palm and comes back. Nothing is written on it.

## The Reckoners' Place

The court reckoners of the city take the dawn sight of the bright star [K'inish'ilan]{.name data-type="place/celestial"} standing on the crown of the Smooth Step. From there the star clears the horizon above a carved post on the far side of the field of pools, and the reckoner calls the moment to a scribe on the stair behind her. The present court reckoner, [[being-neishqemul|Ne' Ish Q'emul]], has stood there on every dawn of the star's season for thirty years.

The stone of the city is laid to the Step's shape because there was no choice. The city's own record says that the builders of the pyramid meant to take the swelling up and set the stair on bare rock, and blunted every tool they had on it without raising a chip. It cannot be cut, shifted or marked, and it has not weathered in the time the city has stood. The plaza's flagstones around it are scratched with the names of reckoners and the counts of their dawns, and every scratch stops at its edge.

## What the City Holds

The court reckoners hold that the Smooth Step is the star's own threshold, laid down for K'inish'ilan to be watched from, and that a sight taken on it cannot err. No reckoner of the city takes the star's dawn sight from anywhere else, and none would let a stranger take one there. The city's other priests honor the Step as part of the pyramid and leave it to the reckoners.

## Hooks {#hooks}

- **The Warden's peg.** [[affiliation-ishilanshulum|The Star House]]'s Warden of the Sighting Stones checks every observing platform of the realm true at each solstice, by setting a bronze peg and sighting along it. He has sent a sighter to check the Smooth Step. No peg will go into it, the court reckoner will not let the sighter stand on it, and the Warden wants a party to carry his complaint to the Ts'enmah and come back with permission.
- **One dawn.** A reckoner of [[place-lishepikum|Lishep'ikum]] has come with gifts to take a single dawn sight from the Step and is willing to pay a party to get her onto it before the court reckoner wakes.
- **The scraper.** A pilgrim was caught at night on the Step with a flint, trying to scrape away a little of it to take home. He took nothing, and he says a priest of the city sent him. The court reckoner wants that priest's name.

# The Truth of the Smooth Step {#truth .secret}

The Smooth Step was on the plain before the city. The lowest course of the pyramid is laid to its curve, and nothing in the city is older than that course.

It is gray and gives, so it is not dead. Whether it has any bearing on the reckoners' count cannot be established, and there is nothing to set beside it: every dawn sight in the city's record was taken on the Step, so no record exists of the same dawns watched from cut stone. The court reckoners' belief that a sight taken there cannot err has never been tested, and they do not intend to test it.

The scraper's priest exists, and is one of the city's own. He wants something of the Step to offer to the Star House, so that its sighters can say they have examined it. He has nothing, because nothing comes off it.

> [!TIP]
> The quarrel over who may stand on the Step is the scene. A party that takes a sight from the crown and another from the stair beside it finds the two agree as well as any two sights by an untrained eye, which proves nothing either way.
