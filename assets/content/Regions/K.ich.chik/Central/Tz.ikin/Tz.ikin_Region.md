## Overview

Tz'ikin Region is the land of [[affiliation-tzikin|Tz'ikin]], and lies within [[place-cntrlkchchk|Central K'ich'chik]] on the [[place-kchchkcntnnt|K'ich'chik Continent]].

Tz'ikin, the "Land of Birds," is the eastern frontier of the central [[place-kchchkcntnnt|K'ich'chik]] heartland—a vast region of tropical rainforest, immense river systems, and tepui (ancient flat-topped mountains) that rise like islands from the jungle canopy. Where [[affiliation-balamkul|Balamkul]] is built on volcanic stone and [[affiliation-kankul|K'ankul]] on limestone, Tz'ikin is built on water. The mighty rivers that carve through its landscape—the Orinoco and its tributary system, the Essequibo, and a thousand smaller waterways—are more important than roads, more important than temples.

The quetzal bird—that manifestation of K'in'ul Tq'an (god of order) on the material plane—dwells in the cloud forests of Tz'ikin in greater abundance than anywhere in [[place-kchchkcntnnt|K'ich'chik]]. Its feathers, along with those of other exotic birds (macaws, parrots, hummingbirds of remarkable colors), command prices that only the wealthiest nobles can afford. Jaguar hides, harvested from the apex predator that rules Tz'ikin's jungle, are the mark of military rank and divine favor. Medicinal herbs—plants whose properties are understood through centuries of herbal lore—grow in Tz'ikin's interior and are gathered by healers and herbalists whose knowledge is so jealously guarded that some have been protected by oath and blood oath to guard their secrets even unto death.

## Geography

Tz'ikin encompasses the vast riverine plains and forested highlands of the eastern frontier, a region roughly equivalent to the modern territories of Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, and the northernmost regions of Brazil. The great Orinoco River and its numerous tributaries form a network of waterways that snake through the landscape for thousands of kilometers, creating zones of periodically flooded forest and navigable channels that connect distant communities. The Essequibo River to the east flows through equally impressive terrain. These rivers are the primary highways; to navigate Tz'ikin's interior is to navigate by water.

The landscape rises from the coastal lowlands and riverine plains eastward toward the Guiana Highlands, where ancient plateaus and tepui create zones of isolated flora and fauna. These high plateaus are topped with vegetation found nowhere else in the known world, having evolved in isolation for millions of years. The flora and fauna of Tz'ikin overall is notably distinct from that of the central heartland—the jaguar, the tapir, the caiman, the anaconda, various species of poison frogs, and bird species of remarkable diversity create an ecosystem that seems almost alien to the sensibilities of highland K'ich'chik visitors.

The climate is intensely hot and humid throughout the year, with heavy rainfall generating the great rivers' seasonal floods and maintaining the luxuriant vegetation. The seasonal flooding—which expands the navigable waterways and creates temporary islands—is a natural rhythm that has shaped Tz'ikin settlement patterns since ancient times. Settlements are built on higher ground, and agricultural zones are arranged to exploit the flooding cycles rather than resist them. The jungle itself is regenerative and resilient, and the K'ich'chik inhabitants of Tz'ikin have learned to harvest from it sustainably, taking what they need without depleting the extraordinary bounty of flora and fauna.

## City-States and Realms

The greatest of these include Orinoquía (the "City of Rivers"), which straddles several tributaries and serves as a collecting point for jungle products; Kamarata (the "House of Stone"), located at the base of a magnificent tepui and serving as a center for the most exotic trade goods; and several coastal settlements that connect to maritime trade networks.

## Trade

Tz'ikin's trade is inseparable from its ecology. The great river systems are the trade routes, and the goods that flow along them are largely products of the jungle and the river. Jaguar hides and other precious furs are collected by hunters and exchanged through intermediary merchants to eventually reach the great markets of Balamkul and K'ankul, where they command prices equivalent to entire estates. Feathers—particularly those of quetzals, macaws, and other colorful species—are gathered, dried, and prepared for shipment northward. The finest feathers for ceremonial use come from Tz'ikin, and the merchant families that monopolize their collection have become fabulously wealthy.

Medicinal and psychoactive plants are harvested according to ancient protocols known only to initiated herbalists. The ayahuasca vine, used in shamanic rituals throughout Tz'ikin and increasingly sought by adventurous priests in the north, flows northward in carefully sealed containers. Other plant preparations—digestive aids, pain relievers, treatments for parasitic infections common in tropical regions—are traded in smaller quantities but generate steady income for the families and communities that gather them. The knowledge of these plants, closely guarded and transmitted through apprenticeship and initiation, is as valuable as the plants themselves.

Cacao grown in Tz'ikin's highland regions is of exceptional quality, and the chocolate prepared from Tz'ikin beans is prized by the wealthy and noble throughout the northern city-states. Exotic woods—hardwoods for tool-making, aromatic woods for incense and perfume production—are harvested from the jungle and shipped northward in logs or carved into finished items. Rubber, a substance wholly unknown in the central heartland until Tz'ikin traders began importing it, is slowly becoming appreciated for waterproofing and other applications, though its true potential remains largely unexplored.
