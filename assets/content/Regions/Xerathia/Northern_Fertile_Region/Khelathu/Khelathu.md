{{< cartouche slug="aukhelathu" height="10rem" align="right" >}}

**Aû'Khelâthu** has been conquered several times, and it has outlived every one of its conquerors. Hill-nomads have taken its throne, and sea-raiders, and a [[lore-vylarianclt|Vylarian]] army at the height of **Vylaria**'s reach; each time the empire handed the new master a scribe, a temple appointment and a throne name, and within a generation or two the conquerors were [[lore-khelathiclt|Khelâthi]]. That is the empire in a sentence: the oldest continuously existing civilization in [[place-worldthlrn|Thalorna]], kept whole for millennia by a river, a priesthood and a written record that absorb whoever arrives.

The Khelâthi take their survival as proof of divine favor. Their civilization endures, they say, because it is built on eternal truths, and the evidence they offer is that it has survived invasions, dynastic collapses and the rise and fall of every rival on the continent without losing its language, its gods or its funerary rites. A foreigner remembers it for the monuments and the dead. A Khelâthi will tell you the thing to remember is the archive.

"Everything here is written down, and everything written down is somebody's business," says [[being-akherethu2|Akherethu]], an Overseer of Scribes in the bureaus of [[place-garmedjatozo|Gar-Medjatozo]], to a foreign envoy's new secretary on the first morning of an embassy. "The god-king rules. The governors govern. We write down what they did, and in a hundred years, what we wrote is what happened."

## Geography

The empire is the valley of [[place-zumeleshrvr|the Zumélesh]], a vast, slow river that runs north through the [[place-nrthrnfrtlrgn|Northern Fertile Region]] of [[place-xerathia|Xerathia]] to the southern shore of the [[place-vylarianse|Vylarian Sea]]. Khelâthi rule extends along both banks for hundreds of leagues, from the delta on the Vylarian coast to the cataracts and uplands far to the south.

_The flood sets the year._ Each year the river rises, lays rich black silt across the floodplain and goes down again, and the economic, religious and administrative calendar is built around that cycle: the **Season of Inundation**, the **Season of Emergence** when the fields are planted, and the **Season of Harvest**. The land is drowned and reborn every year, and that cycle of death and renewal is the central image of Khelâthi theology.

The valley is lush to the point of near-tropical abundance. Its soil is deep and black; palm, papyrus and fig grow thick along the banks; cranes, herons and kingfishers nest in the marshes; and hippopotami, crocodiles and catfish longer than a man thrive in the warm, slow water. A humid summer evening on the bank feels nothing like the dry country a day's ride away. That concentrated fertility makes the valley the most densely populated land in the known world.

Away from the river the empire falls into two other kinds of country:

- The coastal strip. The delta and the lowlands flanking it east and west are wet-coastal. Citrus, grape, olive, date-palm and fig thrive on the sea air and the winter rains, and the coast is the empire's garden: it fills Khelâthi tables and the foreign markets of [[place-midhalnrgn|Mídhalión]] with luxury produce, and the coastal villas of the delta Halzi'a and merchant-princes cluster along it.
- The dry country beyond the flood. Rainfall is sparse away from both river and coast, and the land beyond the valley is high desert and chaparral, with sand dunes on the approaches to the river. A millennia-old network of canals, stone aqueducts, qanats and cisterns, maintained by tens of thousands of workers year-round, carries river water out to the valley's edges, and where it reaches, the fields are dense with wheat, barley and millet. Where it cannot reach, the chaparral pastures sheep, cattle and the small, sturdy horses bred for the chariot corps. The Khelâthi are proud of the network, and with reason: it turns country that would otherwise be thinly grazed into farmland.

The west broadens into open, dry plains that shade at the far frontier into herding country, where hill-peoples pasture their flocks. The east is steeper and more rugged, rising into hills that give the empire gold, copper, turquoise, iron and fine building stone.

## History

Khelâthi history is counted in dynasties—ruling houses that rise, flourish and give way to successors. The empire has passed through unity and fragmentation many times, and the Khelâthi regard the cycle with an equanimity that baffles younger civilizations: every collapse has ended in reunification under a new dynasty claiming the unbroken mandate of the gods. The reigning dynasty, the house of **Zu'Mequ**, has held the throne for two and a half centuries, and it is the latest in a line that runs back past reliable record.

The empire survives by absorbing its conquerors. Foreign peoples have seized the throne on several occasions—hill-nomads from the western margins, northern sea-raiders, and a [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian]] military occupation at the height of imperial expansion. Each time, within a generation or two, the conquerors adopted the Khelâthi language, religion, customs and dress. Foreign dynasties build pyramids, worship the gods of the [[affiliation-khelathpnthn|Khelâthi Pantheon]] and rule through the existing priestly bureaucracy. The Khelâthi count this as vindication: even those who conquer Aû'Khelâthu end up serving its gods.

## Government

"Begin with the throne, because everyone else does," Akherethu tells the secretary. "Then learn who actually moves a decree from the throne to a field, because that is where your embassy's business will stall."

The [[lore-garauu|Gar-Aû]] sits at the apex of the state, a living intermediary between the mortal world and the gods. In practice nobody believes the Gar-Aû is a god, everyone knows that nobody believes it, and the fiction is maintained anyway, because the whole structure of Khelâthi society rests on it: it gives the throne its authority, the priesthood its relevance and the people a sense of cosmic order. The trappings are elaborate. The Gar-Aû is attended by legions of priests, enters and leaves through sacred gates, eats ritually prepared food, and conducts affairs of state as religious ceremony.

The real work of governing falls to the provinces and the bureaus.

- **The Halzi'a.** The empire is divided into selatu (provinces), each governed by a hereditary Halzi'a who answers to the Gar-Aû and wields considerable local authority. The most powerful Halzi'a command armies, collect taxes and administer justice within their territories with little interference from the capital. The balance between throne and governors is the central political tension of the empire: a strong Gar-Aû keeps the Halzi'a obedient, and a weak one finds them acting as independent kings.
- The scribes. A vast scribal class staffs the bureaucracy and manages everything from tax collection to irrigation schedules to military logistics. The scribal schools are one of the few reliable paths upward in Khelâthi society: a talented peasant's child who enters them can rise to real influence, and the scribes as a class are a counterweight to both the priesthood and the military aristocracy.

So a decree from the throne needs a Halzi'a to act on it and a clerk to enter what was done, and a petitioner may have to win over all three.

### The Priesthood

The priesthood is the most powerful institution in the empire after the Gar-Aû, and in some reigns more powerful than the Gar-Aû. Each major temple complex is an economic, political and spiritual enterprise at once: the great temples own enormous tracts of farmland, employ thousands of workers, keep their own treasuries and wield real political influence. The high priests of the major gods—above all [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]] (the sun), [[lore-qearetdty|Qe'âret]] (order and justice) and [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]] (creation and craftsmanship)—are among the most powerful people in the empire.

Throne and temple need each other and compete. The priests confirm the Gar-Aû's divine authority through ritual and theology; the Gar-Aû grants the temples land, wealth and legal privilege. When a dynasty weakens, it is often the priesthood that arranges the transition to the next, and the high priest of Uqa'â has deposed or elevated more Gar-Aûu than any army.

#### The Temple Hierarchy

Every major temple in Aû'Khelâthu keeps the same three ranks, a structure so old that the Khelâthi hold it divinely ordained. The Gar-Aû stands above all three as nominal supreme priest of every god, and all temple authority is claimed to flow down from that office. In practice the Gar-Aû delegates religious authority entirely, but the claim matters: every high priest serves at the Gar-Aû's pleasure, and a Gar-Aû who wanted to replace one would have doctrinal grounds to do it. Few have tried. Fewer have succeeded.

- Thâz'Lekhau ("Great of Sacred Power")—the High Priest or High Priestess of a major temple and the supreme religious authority of one cult. Each major temple has one. The Thâz'Lekhau manages the temple's holdings, speaks with the god's voice on doctrine, and as High Ritualist conducts the great seasonal rites and the divinations that guide state policy. The **Thâz'Lekhau of Uqa'â** is arguably the second most powerful person in the empire, and the high priest of any major cult commands resources to match the wealthiest Halzi'a. The office is not hereditary: a Thâz'Lekhau is raised from among the senior priests by temple politics, demonstrated capability and—the priesthood insists—divine revelation.
- Lem'Nelgir ("Servant of the God")—the ordained priesthood and the working body of the temple. Lem'Nelgir conduct the daily rites, keep the sanctuary, manage temple lands and finances, teach in the temple schools, perform divinations for petitioners, and carry out the temple's part in local government. The rank spans every seniority from the newly ordained to veterans of decades who command temple districts and advise Halzi'a. Most specialize—in ritual, administration, medicine, scholarship or legal arbitration—and the politics inside a major temple are as tangled as anything at court.
- Wazu ("Purified One")—the acolytes of the temple schools. A Wazu enters as a child or youth and studies sacred texts, ritual, history, mathematics, medicine and the reading of omens for years before ordination. The schools are among the best in the empire, and those who leave before ordination carry skills that serve them in secular careers. The purification that names the rank is taken seriously: a Wazu keeps strict codes of diet, behavior and cleanliness that set them apart from ordinary life.

### The Military

The Khelâthi army is professional, well organized and, unusually for the region, made up mostly of native soldiers rather than mercenaries. Its core is a standing army of chariot corps and disciplined infantry, filled out by regional levies the Halzi'a call up. The chariot corps, drawn from the military aristocracy, is still the elite striking force, though in recent centuries the heavy infantry of the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian Empire]] and the fleets of [[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Harad]] have challenged its dominance.

The army's work is defensive: holding the western frontier against raiders from the herding country, keeping control of the delta ports, and now and then reasserting authority over a restive Halzi'a or border province. Aû'Khelâthu has fought no serious war of conquest for generations. It projects its power through trade and culture.

### Arms and Armor

Khelâthi arms and armor match anything made in Vylaria or **Provènzia**. The empire's smiths work steel as a matter of course, with iron ore from the eastern hills and centuries of accumulated craft. What sets Khelâthi equipment apart is its form: weapon shapes, armor styles and decoration that have evolved continuously from ancient originals and give a Khelâthi soldier a look nobody mistakes.

Weapons. The signature weapon is the khopesh, a curved sickle-sword with a hooked blade for slashing and for dragging a shield aside. The ancient khopesh was cast bronze; the modern one is forged steel, often with a slight recurve that makes it deadly at close quarters. It appears in tomb paintings, temple carvings and royal regalia across millennia, and officers and nobles carry one as a mark of status even where another weapon would serve better. Beside it the army uses the steel-tipped war spear of the infantry formations; the composite bow, a powerful recurve shot from chariots and by specialist archer units; and the bronze-headed mace or war club, which officers favor as weapon and badge of command. The image of the Gar-Aû smiting with a mace is the oldest and longest-lived icon of Khelâthi power. Short swords and daggers of many patterns complete the arsenal.

Armor. The Khelâthi tradition favors scale armor: overlapping steel scales riveted or laced to a backing of linen or leather. Scale has been the prestige armor of Khelâthi warriors since antiquity, and the modern form is lighter, more flexible and better ventilated than western equivalents, which matters in the valley's heat. Officers and nobles wear corselets with gold-inlaid or gilded shoulder scales to show rank. Under the scale goes the war-kilt of layered, quilted linen, which turns a cut surprisingly well and leaves a man free to move in the heat. Helmets are open-faced conical steel caps, sometimes with cheek guards and rarely with the full visors of colder western countries. A tall shield of wood and hide with a steel boss completes the standard kit.

War gear and court gear. The Khelâthi keep the two apart and take both seriously. A Halzi'a carries a steel khopesh and a steel corselet into battle, and appears at court with a ceremonial khopesh of bronze and gold, a gold-headed mace of command and a gold pectoral collar of rank. The ceremonial forms deliberately copy ancient prototypes—bronze, gold and lapis lazuli in patterns unchanged for millennia—while the war forms change freely. No Khelâthi commander takes bronze into a real fight, and no Khelâthi noble attends a temple ceremony in plain steel.

## Culture and Society

The [[lore-khelathiclt|culture note]] follows the Khelâthi through the doctrine that shapes a life: the two ledgers, the closing of a person's obligations, and what the household owes its widows and its dead. What follows here is the society as a state sees it.

### Social Structure

Khelâthi society is hierarchical without being rigid. The Gar-Aû and the royal family sit at the apex, then the priestly and military aristocracies, then the scribal and administrative class, then merchants and master artisans, and at the base the vast population of farmers and laborers who work the floodplain. Mobility between them is limited but real: the scribal schools are the main road up, and military service, the priestly orders and commercial success can each raise a family over generations.

### Titles and Forms of Address {#titles-and-forms-of-address}

Khelâthi nobility does not use the western **Ankarian** "Lord" and "Lady." The principal titles of rank are:

- [[lore-garauu|Gar-Aû]]—the divine sovereign and ruler of the empire. The title is sacred and carries theological weight beyond political authority.
- Iru'palu—the highest hereditary rank below the Gar-Aû, near what the west calls a prince or duke, reserved for the royal family and the most exalted noble houses. It marks closeness to divine authority.
- Halzi'a—the hereditary governor of a selat, commanding its army, collecting its taxes and dispensing its justice; near what the west calls a count or an earl.
- Legha'lutu (male) and Zabet'lutu (female)—"ruler of the estate" and "mistress of the estate," the landed noble who administers a manor, its fields and an attached village, answerable to the Halzi'a above.
- Zabet—a title of respect for a woman of noble rank whose authority comes from court position rather than land: a courtier, a high priestess, a noblewoman of the palace. It is near the western "Lady."
- Zemelu—"companion" or "friend of the Gar-Aû," an honorific for trusted courtiers and advisors that marks royal favor and access rather than territory.

The title always comes before the personal name: Halzi'a **Lersaîs**, Zabet'lutu **Thaminra**, Zemelu **Quztar**. The Khelâthi find the western habit of putting the title after the family name charmingly backward.

Khelâthi women hold far more legal rights than women in most of western [[place-ankrscntnnt|Ankaris]]. They own property, initiate divorce, serve as priestesses up to the highest offices, practice medicine and magic, and conduct business on their own account. The doctrine underneath belongs to [[lore-uzneradty|Uznêra]], whose faith holds that creation requires the balanced partnership of a masculine and a feminine divine principle. Several of the ablest rulers in Khelâthi history have been queens who governed as Gar-Aû in their own right.

### Notable Institutions

Two secular bodies shape political and economic life beside the priesthood and the army:

- [[affiliation-genzetpalu|Genzet'Palu]] ([[affiliation-genzetpalu|Council of the Nobles]])—a formal assembly of the most powerful aristocratic families, convened in the capital to advise the Gar-Aû on internal policy, succession disputes and quarrels between selatu. It has no legislative authority, since the Gar-Aû's word is law, but no Gar-Aû governs well without it. Its meetings run on full ceremonial protocol, and the members who mediate between its factions hold great informal power.
- [[affiliation-zebequzut|Zebequ'Zut]] ([[affiliation-zebequzut|Merchant Alliance]])—an informal network of the empire's major trading houses, bound by pacts of mutual protection, shared intelligence on routes and markets, and joint bargaining with foreign commercial powers. It has no charter and no premises; its business is done at banquets, on private estates and along the docks of the delta ports. Its members control a large share of the empire's wealth and can make or break a Halzi'a who crosses them.

### Architecture and Monuments

The pyramids, temple complexes, obelisks and colossal statues along the river are the empire's most visible achievement. They show the Gar-Aû's power, honor the gods, and give the afterlife industry its workplaces. Western Ankaris commonly believes slaves built them. Farmers built them, in seasonal work during the flood, when the fields are under water and no farm labor is possible, so the monuments are public works as much as acts of devotion.

A temple complex is a city inside a city: sanctuaries, libraries, schools, workshops, granaries and offices behind one wall. The greatest temples rival the Gar-Aû's palace in wealth and influence, and their walls are carved and painted with myth, ritual and history—the largest archive of pictures in the known world.

### The Culture of Death and Renewal

Death is the organizing preoccupation of Khelâthi civilization, and the Khelâthi treat it as the lens through which life makes sense. Life prepares for an eternal existence beyond death, and the quality of that existence depends on both conduct in life and proper funerary preparation. That belief drives a vast mortuary industry—embalming, tomb-building, funerary art and the making of grave goods—that employs a large share of the economy.

The theology is layered. The soul has several parts, each needing its own rites and protections. The weighing of the heart against the feather of [[lore-qearetdty|Qe'âret]] is the central doctrine of the end, and it shapes daily life: even the powerful fear the verdict, and the knowledge that wealth cannot buy a good one is a check, however imperfect, on the worst abuses of power.

_The scale of a burial tracks wealth exactly._ The embalmers sell their work in three grades by old custom. The first is the full treatment of some seventy days: the body drawn, dried in natron, anointed with costly resins and wrapped in fine linen with an amulet at every joint. The second is a cheaper course, and the third is a bare treatment for the poor. Tombs grade the same way:

- the Gar-Aûu and their kin lie in the pyramids and rock-cut Ways of the royal necropolises;
- great nobles, priests and officials raise mastaba-tombs and decorated rock-cut chapels in the cemeteries beside them, wanting even in death to lie near their king;
- merchants and scribes buy smaller tombs, places in shared galleries, or niches farther out;
- the common people go into pit-graves in the open desert sand, which is hot and dry enough to mummify a pauper for free, often better than a cheap embalming would.

Because the weighing spares nobody, nobody may go without the minimum rites. The temples keep paupers' fields and say the words over the destitute, and ordinary people pay into burial clubs—neighborhood and guild societies whose one purpose is that no member goes to the West unwrapped and unmourned. A bad burial is the abiding dread of the Khelâthi poor, worse than a hard life, because a hard life ends.

All that wealth in the ground has bred its profession. _Tomb robbery is old, organized and beyond stamping out_, and the wealthy build against it in layers: a great tomb is hidden, then barred, then trapped, then cursed and warded, and last of all, for those who can pay, guarded. Endowed watch-priests keep the ground above, and in the deepest chambers the bound dead keep the post: zaglu-soldiers, the [[being-damut|ḍumaṭu]], who wake to a broken seal and do only what their binding wrote, and revenant-wardens, the [[being-tereb|ṭerebu]], who keep their wits and their station in the dark. Neither side has ever won, and the fight is hardest in the great necropolis of [[place-khelzuret|Khelzuret]] and the burial fields of the capital at [[place-khelautithnt|Khelaut-Ithnet]].

### Arts and Sciences

Khelâthi civilization excels in mathematics, astronomy, medicine and engineering. Its astronomers map the heavens more precisely than anyone in western Ankaris. Its physicians blend observation with magical theory: their surgery is advanced, and their remedies draw on centuries of herbal trial. Khelâthi geometry grew out of architecture and land survey—the flood erases property boundaries every year, so the fields must be measured again—and it has shaped scholarship as far away as [[place-helionis|Heliónis]].

The visual arts are highly developed and deeply conservative. They keep conventions fixed millennia ago: figures drawn with the face in profile and the shoulders square, size set by rank, and a symbolic palette. The cause is theology. Khelâthi art is a magical act that makes an eternal reality; a properly painted tomb _is_ the afterlife it shows, made real by correct form. So the Khelâthi value perfecting the established form and do not value innovation.

### The Khelâthi Calendar and Dating {#the-khelathi-calendar-and-dating}

The Khelâthi do not use the western Ankarian calendar, and suggesting it gives offense. Their own dating, they will tell you, is older than the Vylarian people, and they are probably right, though the details are disputed. The full reckoning is in [[lore-khelathclndr|the Khelâthi calendar]].

_Daily life runs on regnal years_—"Year 12 of [[being-garaumeqesxv|Gar-Aû Meqes XVI]]," for instance. Every document, contract and temple record is dated by the reigning Gar-Aû, and the count starts again at Year 1 with each reign. Tax rolls, lawsuits and commercial records all use it.

History and theology count from the [[lore-qettelgu|Qet Telgu]], the "[[lore-firstoccasion|First Occasion]]": the moment in myth when the first Gar-Aû united the valley under one crown and the gods established the order of civilization. The Khelâthi place it about three thousand years ago, and the [[lore-garauu|temple king-lists]] record every dynasty and every Gar-Aû since, in unbroken sequence. The lists are real. Whether they are accurate for the earliest centuries is another matter. Western scholars point out that the earliest dynasties hold reigns of improbable length—the first two Gar-Aûu are entered at 150 years each—that some periods show suspiciously neat numbers, and that physical evidence for the oldest dynasties is sparse and contradictory. The Khelâthi are serenely unconcerned. The priesthood of [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]], keeper of sacred knowledge, compiled the lists, and that settles it for them.

Years from the Qet Telgu are written ST, "Selqur Qet Telgu," the Year of the First Occasion. The current year is about 2,830 ST, which is 720 AF in the western calendar. The age before it—the time of the gods, before civilization—is simply "before the First Occasion," and the Khelâthi seldom bother to date it. History, as they see it, begins with the Qet Telgu; before it was chaos.

_The year has three seasons of four months_: [[lore-azletssn|Azlet]] (Inundation), [[lore-geletssn|Gelet]] (Emergence) and [[lore-sheluessn|Shelu]] (Harvest). Each month has thirty days, and five intercalary days close the year. Khelâthi astronomers are proud of the system with good cause, and fond of pointing out that its seasons are named for what the river does while the western calendar begins from a senator's coup.

[[affiliation-okharis|Okháris]] and the smaller polities of the upper river use the Qet Telgu as a matter of course, the way western Ankaris adopted the Vylarian count. [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]] and the [[affiliation-tanvurempr|Empire of Tānvür]] keep their own systems, as does [[affiliation-mtrrchybth|Bethûa]], though the [[lore-bethuanclt|Bethûan]] system shows strong Khelâthi influence.

### Festival Life

For a civilization known abroad for its dead, Aû'Khelâthu lives loudly. The calendar is crowded with festivals—flood celebrations, harvest feasts, the birthdays of gods, and funerary commemorations that double as community feasts. [[lore-thubaidty|Thubâ'i]], god of prosperity and pleasure, is one of the best-loved gods in the empire, because Khelâthi culture finds no contradiction between devotion and enjoyment. Beer, wine, music, dance and long banquets sit at the center of both religious and secular life.

The market towns along the river are crowded, colorful and noisy. Goods from across the known world pass through the ports: spices from [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]], metals from the eastern hills, timber from the south and luxuries from [[place-midhalnrgn|Mídhalión]] come in; papyrus, linen, gold, grain and fine artisan work go out. The [[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Haradian]] merchant houses keep permanent factors in the delta cities, and the docks are a jumble of languages and peoples.

## Religion

The [[affiliation-khelathpnthn|Khelâthi Pantheon]] reaches into every part of Khelâthi life, and its great gods are arranged in theological systems argued over and refined for millennia. [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]] (the sun, life and purification) stands at the apex; [[lore-qearetdty|Qe'âret]] (order, truth and justice) supplies the moral frame; [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]] (creation and craft) governs the material world; [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]] (knowledge and writing) keeps wisdom; [[lore-uzneradty|Uznêra]] (fertility and healing) sustains life; [[lore-wethurdty|Wethûr]] and [[lore-hezmuiridty|Hezmuîri]] govern the passage into death; and [[lore-azuathisdty|Azu'âthis]] (chaos) threatens the whole order.

Each selat also has its own patron, and the local cults give the empire's religion great regional variety under the orthodox surface. The temple of Uqa'â in the capital may define official doctrine, but the fishermen of the delta worship Thubâ'i more fervently, and the miners of the eastern hills pray to Psaq'âru. The priesthoods tolerate this and often encourage it: the Khelâthi hold their gods too vast for any single cult, and their religious history is one long synthesis, absorption and reinterpretation.

## Magic

Khelâthi magic is priestly, and the line between priest and mage barely exists. Power comes from knowing sacred words, divine names and ritual formulae kept in temple libraries open only to the initiated, and the [[lore-khelunulekha|Khelunu Lekhau]] teach it in temple schools the way the temples teach scribes. The strongest magic is funerary: the workings that protect the dead, guide the soul through the afterlife and ward tombs against desecration.

The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Ordo Arcanis]] has no presence in Aû'Khelâthu and no prospect of one. The empire's own magical establishment is older, more deeply rooted and more closely bound to state power than anything the **Ordo** could build. Ordo agents occasionally visit the ports; the priesthood regards them as arrogant amateurs, and the few who have tried to recruit Khelâthi mages have been politely and firmly expelled.

## Relations

"Every embassy asks me who the empire's friends are," Akherethu says. "The empire has a rival it respects, a partner it bought, a neighbor it keeps, and scholars it lets into the library. Learn which one your master is."

[[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylaria]] is the empire's chief foreign relationship, across the [[place-vylarianse|Vylarian Sea]]. The two have fought wars, and have since settled into a wary balance—Aû'Khelâthu dominant in the south, Vylaria in the north—with extensive, profitable trade and a peace that has held for generations. The Khelâthi respect Vylarian military discipline and are quietly amused by Vylarian youth: the Vylarian Empire is a few centuries old, and Aû'Khelâthu has been here since the beginning of recorded history.

[[place-helionis|Heliónis]] has traded ideas with the empire for centuries, in one of the most productive cultural exchanges in the known world. Khelâthi mathematics, astronomy and architecture have enriched [[lore-helioniteclt|Helionite]] scholarship, and Helionite philosophy has entered Khelâthi theological debate. Helionite scholars are among the few foreigners admitted to Khelâthi temple libraries, and Khelâthi priests take part in Helionite arguments about the nature of the divine.

[[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Harad]] is the empire's most important trading partner, and the tie runs deeper than trade. When the Haradian merchant princes rose against Vylaria about twelve years ago, the Khelâthi court sent covert aid—gold, weapons and military advisors—that carried the rebellion through its most desperate months. The court called it "humanitarian commerce," and its purpose, weakening Vylarian influence in the eastern sea and buying a grateful partner, was plain to everyone involved. It paid. Independent Harad has given the empire preferential trade terms, lower harbor fees and diplomacy that consistently favors Khelâthi interests, and goods that once passed through Vylarian hands now move directly between Haradian and Khelâthi ports, to Vylaria's loss. Haradian houses keep permanent factors in the delta ports, and the Khelâthi are content to leave the sea routes to Haradian ships: the river is their highway, and they have no wish to build a long-distance merchant fleet of their own.

The [[affiliation-mtrrchybth|Matriarchy of Bethûa]], to the west, is the smaller neighbor and, in the court's cold reckoning, a useful proxy. [[place-galezkara|Galezkara]] backed Bethûa's war of independence from its [[skill-helonclng|Helonic]] metropole and has cultivated the Matriarchy ever since, for strategy rather than sentiment. A free, prosperous Bethûa aligned with the empire on the southern shore of the Vylarian Sea keeps Helonic and above all Vylarian ambitions in [[place-midhalnrgn|Mídhalión]] divided, competes with Vylarian trade, and gives the empire a partner at sea without the cost of a navy. The instruments are economic and institutional: the [[affiliation-garhalzi|Gár-Hálzi]] correspondent accounts that underwrite the [[affiliation-bthntrdhss|Bethûan Trade-Houses]], the loan-built fiscal and engineering administration the Matriarchy still runs on Khelâthi models, and a steady flow of quiet subsidy and diplomatic cover. The **Bethûans** accept the arrangement as the price of a freedom they could not otherwise hold, and they mark it in public: a Khelâthi envoy is the one foreign man received with deference in their militantly female-centered ports. Old frictions persist along the border, but neither power wants a real war, since Bethûa is worth far more to the empire alive, free and facing north.

The south shades into lands held by tribal peoples whose relationship with the Gar-Aû runs from nominal vassalage to open hostility, depending on the balance of military pressure and trade.

## The Selatu (Provinces)

The empire has thirty-nine selatu, each under a hereditary Halzi'a. They differ enormously in size, wealth and weight, and they fall into four classes:

- The [[affiliation-deltaselatu|Delta Selatu]] (ten selatu, about 6,000,000 people) are the wealthiest and most cosmopolitan. They control the empire's international trade and its finest garden farmland, and their Halzi'a are merchants and diplomats as much as governors. Chief among them is the great port-selat of the [[affiliation-selatgrnlght|Selat of Gar-Anlaghet]], the empire's sea-gate.
- The [[affiliation-upperrivrslt|Upper River Selatu]] (eighteen selatu, about 8,000,000) are the agricultural heartland: they grow the grain that feeds the empire and hold its oldest temple-cities. Their Halzi'a are conservative landowners who resist innovation and guard their privileges—among them the granary-lords of the [[affiliation-selatnlghztn|Selat of Anlagh-Zetûn]], the mortuary priesthoods of the [[affiliation-selatkhelzrt|Selat of Khelzuret]], and the scribal temple of the [[affiliation-selatgrrthsr|Selat of Gar-Reth'Sa'âr]].
- The [[affiliation-borderselatu|Border Selatu]] (ten selatu, about 2,500,000) in the south and east are military frontiers, and their Halzi'a serve as military governors. The mining march of the [[affiliation-selatzuzlqtt|Selat of Zu-Zel-Iaqtet]] in the eastern hills and the cataract march of the [[affiliation-selatzuzllgl|Selat of Zu-Zalu-Legulu]] in the south lead them. These are rough postings, and they produce the empire's most experienced soldiers.
- The [[affiliation-capitalselat|Capital Selat]] (about 2,500,000) is a single enormous province around [[place-galezkara|Galezkara]], the imperial city. It holds the Gar-Aû's seat, the central bureaucracy, the greatest temples and the royal necropolis, and its Halzi'a serves at the Gar-Aû's direct pleasure.

Galezkara is laid out along a single ceremonial axis aligned to the solstices, the **Sun's Road** ([[place-waluqaa|Wal-Uqa'â]]): the living city on the east bank, the sacred island of [[place-yathtelgu|Yath-Telgu]] midstream, and the royal necropolis of [[place-zugezer|Zu-Gezer]] on the western desert shore, in the order of the sun's daily journey. Yath-Telgu is the primeval mound and carries the **Great Temple of Uqa'â**, the palace, and the flood-gauge called **the Measure**. The city's two greatest civic rites, the midsummer **Festival of the Coming-Forth** and the midwinter **Festival of the Going-Down**, fall when the sun rises and sets exactly down that road.

Together the four classes hold the empire's nineteen million people. Each class note treats its most important selatu at length and rosters the rest.

## Notable Features

- [[place-zumeleshrvr|The Great River]]—the empire's lifeblood, its annual flood shaping every part of the civilization
- The pyramids and necropolises—tomb complexes along the western bank, the most impressive built landscape in the known world. At the capital, the royal necropolis of [[place-zugezer|Zu-Gezer]] is laid out as a timeline in stone: each dynasty's Gar-Aûu lie along their own crossing-road off the great death-road, by tradition the oldest nearest the river and the newest at the frontier, though the mightiest houses have reordered the row, and the reigning dynasty took the river-front itself by its **Great Removal**
- [[place-zelgeru|The Zelgeru]]—the great temple-estates, religious, economic and political institutions to rival the Gar-Aû's palace
- [[affiliation-linzethrthsr|Lin'Zethu elu Reth'Sa'âr]]—the scribes' road upward, producing the administrators who keep the state running
- [[place-amqelmiglet|Amqel-Miglet]]—the harbors of the delta, where Khelâthi civilization meets the wider world
- The festival calendar—a year-round cycle of religious celebrations, feasts and public entertainments, crowned at the capital by the solstice rites of the **Coming-Forth** (midsummer dawn) and the **Going-Down** (midwinter dusk)

## Commerce and Currency

The empire strikes no round coin. Its temples attest weight-pieces of copper, silver and gold in gezan and qelu units, and the [[affiliation-garhalzi|Gár-Hálzi]] temple-treasuries do the banking. A familiar sealed piece passes at face value; unfamiliar metal is weighed and assayed. [[lore-aukhlthcrncy|The Aû'Khelâthu currency note]] gives the weights and exchange rates.
