Khaset-Mehtet — "the northern desert-march" — is the dry country at the
northeastern edge of [[place-takheperurgn|Ta'Kheperu]], beyond the last of the
eastern border nomes, where the empire's uplands run out into the semi-desert
that faces [[place-dunharargn|Dunhara]]. It lies within Ta'Kheperu's country
but outside its nomes: no
[[affiliation-empirtkhpr#titles-and-forms-of-address|Haty'a]] holds it, no
render is levied from it, and the
[[affiliation-empirtkhpr|Empire of Ta'Kheperu]] keeps it only because the road
to the east and north runs across it and because whoever else held it would be
a day's ride from the mines. Its name answers that of
[[affiliation-nomekhstmn|Khaset-Imnt]], the western desert-march, but the
likeness ends at the name: Khaset-Imnt is a nome with a Haty'a and a temple,
and Khaset-Mehtet is a garrison holding a road.

## Geography

The march begins where the quarry-roads of
[[affiliation-nometstbtt|Ta-Set-Iabtet]] and the well-lines of
[[affiliation-nomeiabeth|Iabet-Heqa]] give out, and runs northeast across
stony plateau, gravel plain and low broken hills until the Dunhari sand seas
begin. It is **semi-desert**: a thin winter rain greens the wadis for a few
weeks and raises a short grass on the northern flats, and the rest of the year
the country is grey stone and dust under a white sky. Nothing is sown here
that a canal does not reach, and no canal reaches it. Thorn, tamarisk and
saltbush grow along the dry streambeds; date-palms stand only where a well has
been dug and walled; goats, camels and the small hard horses of the frontier
graze the wadis, and sheep only after the rains.

**Water** is the march. A chain of wells and rock-cisterns follows the line of
the wadis from the last Kheperi villages to the first Dunhari oases, most of
them dug or deepened by the garrisons and every one of them named, walled and
garrisoned or claimed by a clan. Between the wells there is nothing, and a
party that misses one does not reach the next.

## The Road

One thing gives the march its worth: the **caravan road** that leaves the
river valley through the eastern hills, threads the wells of Khaset-Mehtet,
and climbs into the Dunhara and on toward the oasis-belt of the
[[place-khzryndsrtrgn|Khazryn Desert]]. It is the empire's land-road to the
east and the north — the way by which spice, silk, jewels and the rare goods
of the far east come overland when they do not come by sea, and the way by
which Kheperi grain, linen, worked metal and gold go out to the desert
markets. Kheperi merchants and the desert caravans meet at the wells; Dunhari
guides carry a caravan from the last fort to the first oasis, and no Kheperi
caravan-master dispenses with one.

The road is also the empire's depth. The gold of Ta-Set-Iabtet and the
quarries of Iabet-Heqa lie a few days' ride behind the march, and a raid that
crossed the desert unopposed would reach them. Holding the wells holds the
road, and holding the road keeps the mines a garrisoned country rather than a
frontier.

## Why it is not a nome

The empire has never made Khaset-Mehtet a nome, and does not want to. A nome
runs on the flood, the harvest and the render, and the march has none of
them: nothing grows to be taxed, no estates can be granted, no temple can be
endowed from land that yields nothing. A Haty'a here would be a governor of
wells. So the march is held instead as a **military command** — a crown
appointment, not a hereditary seat — and its garrisons are paid, fed and armed
from the heartland, as the border nomes' are, with even less to show for it in
return. The court regards it as a cost of the road and of the mines, and
grumbles at the cost every year.

## The Garrisons

The march is held from a line of **forts on the road**, each built on a well:
mud-brick walls, a cistern, a watch-tower, a chariot-yard, and a company of
infantry and archers with a few chariots for the patrols. The largest, at the
wells where the road turns toward the Dunhara, is the seat of the **Imperial
Garrison** and of its commander, the **Overseer of the March**, who answers
to the Per-Aá's military officers at the capital and to no nomarch. The
garrison's whole duty is the road: to patrol the wells, escort the caravans,
collect the road-toll, and keep the peace with the clans on the other side
of it.

That peace is nominal and growing thin. It rests on custom rather than treaty
— on the clans' right to water at the wells and the caravans' right to pass
unmolested, which each side keeps as long as the other does — and it depends
on the Overseer, whose posting is short and whose ambitions lie in the
valley. Recent commanders have been soldiers of the ordinary Kheperi kind who
see a desert people as a nuisance to be managed rather than a neighbour to be
dealt with: wells closed to clan herds, tolls raised on Dunhari caravans,
hostages taken after a raid and not returned. Each such act has cost a
skirmish, and each skirmish a negotiation that failed. The cult of Mentu is
strong in the forts, as it is on every Kheperi frontier, and its priests
preach no patience with nomads.

## The Dunhara

The clans who contest the march are the western Dunhari, whose migration
circuits and oases of right run up against the Kheperi wells. The sheikhs
regard the wells as theirs by the Laws of the Well — water is never one
people's property — and the forts as squatters upon them. Chief
among the clans that water here are the Shiradar, whose sheikh
[[being-mthrnshrdr|Mithran Shiradar]] counts the Imperial Garrison among his
enemies, and their Valdun neighbours, whose raids the Shiradar are as often
blamed for as their own.

The two sides meet at the **Truce Wells** — the largest wells on the road,
where by long custom no blood is shed and a Dunhari who has eaten Kheperi salt
is a guest and not a prisoner. Here the caravan-tolls are paid, the guides are
hired, disputes over a stolen herd or a closed well are argued between the
Overseer's scribes and the sheikhs' poets, and the peace is renewed each year
with gifts. The Kheperi write the terms down; the Dunhari recite them; when
the two versions differ, which they do, there is a raid.

## Two Views of the March

To a Kheperi, Khaset-Mehtet is the empire's least-loved country: a posting
without a temple, a river or a tomb, where the water is bad and the natives
are thieves, and where a career goes to be endured before it is rewarded
somewhere green. The road is the point of it; the land is a thing the road
passes through.

To a Dunhari, the same country is the western edge of the world he knows —
grazing his clan waters by a right older than any fort, a road his clan
guides caravans along by the same right, and a settled empire's presumption
made into mud-brick. He will trade at the forts, guide for the
caravan-masters and take Kheperi silver; he will
not admit that the empire holds one well of it, and in the Dunhari version
of the peace it is the garrison that is tolerated, not the clans.
