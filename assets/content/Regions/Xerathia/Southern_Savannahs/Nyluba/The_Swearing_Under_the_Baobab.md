**The Swearing Under the Baobab** is the memory-song of the [[affiliation-nylbtrblntn|Long Pact]]'s swearing, held by the [[affiliation-nyalblion|Lion Totem]] and kept by the Griot of the Mvuzi. It is praise poetry in the griot's call and the hearers' answer: the griot names, and those gathered return the name. A memory-song is evidence as well as praise, so the hearers' answers also serve as a check, and the song is sung before people who already know it. The [[skill-griot|griot]] holds it to its received length, keeps [[affiliation-nylbtrblntn|Mwána-Mvuzi]]'s sentence whole, and says of the lion only that it was witness. Of the catastrophe that drove the five clans together, the song keeps what the Bombwe griots hold and nothing more. In the text below, the indented lines in italics are the answer of those gathered.

: The Swearing Under the Baobab {#song}

```poetry {form=praise lang=en}
Hear me, children of the five.
  _We hear you, keeper._
Hear me, lion-tooth and braided hair.
  _We hear you._
I carry what was put into my mouth.
I carry it the length it was given me,
no longer, and no shorter.
  _Carry it. We know it too._

Nine generations back,
count them down the braid.
  _Nine, and not one fewer._
In the lion's country, on the middle grass,
a baobab stood, and it was old already.
  _It was there before the five._

Five came to it, and none of them were friends.
Each had its cattle-feud and kept it warm.
They were being picked apart one at a time,
each one taken while the others watched.
  _One at a time, while the others watched._

What was it that picked at them?
  _Worn smooth, worn smooth._
The griots of the baobab say:
a host out of the middle of the grass country, coming south,
that took the cattle, took the women, took the lives,
and made no difference among the three.
That host is gone. It left no herds behind it.
  _Worn smooth, and gone._

Who came up from the river?
  _The crocodile came._
Kambezi of the reed canoe and the slow brown water,
who name the crocodile by its mother and her mother.

Who came down from the high country?
  _The eagle came._
Ngonzi of the red escarpment and the gold in the river-sand,
the only hands among the five that answer to stone.

Who came in from the edge of the trees?
  _The leopard came._
Sengala of many tongues, who know the road to the north
and say less about it than they know.

Who came from the standing water?
  _The baobab's people came. The tree stays where it is._
Bombwe of the grain and the well and the slow word,
who carry the fathers of all five in their mouths.

Who stood on their own ground?
  _The lion stood._
Mvuzi of the cattle and of the challenge made in the open,
first of the five, because the tree was theirs to stand under.

And who spoke for the lion?
  _Mwána-Mvuzi._
Say what the name is.
  _Daughter of the Lion._
Every senior woman of the lion carries that name now.
She carried it first.

And who watched?
  _The lion watched._
The song says witness. It says no more than witness.
  _No more than witness._

Now the terms. Count them on one hand.
  _One hand, five fingers._
First: the five do not lift a weapon against the five,
and the old cattle-feuds are put down and not picked up.
  _Put down, and not picked up._
Second: a spear against one is a spear against five.
  _Against one, against five._
Third: the grass, the rivers and the rains are shared,
and each keeps the others' manners with the spirits of those places.
  _Shared, and kept correctly._
Fourth: the five come back in their own bodies
every fourth dry season, and say it again.
  _Every fourth dry season._
Fifth: what was sworn here changes only when all five paramounts
say yes aloud, before the Indaba and before the griots.
  _All five, aloud._
That is the whole hand.
  _There is no sixth finger._

Some under the tree wanted more than a hand.
They wanted a house built over the five.
And Mwána-Mvuzi answered them. Hear her whole:
"We are not building a kingdom.
We are agreeing not to destroy each other.
The rest is for our daughters to decide for themselves."
  _The rest is for our daughters._

No king walked out from under that tree.
No law walked out of it but these five.
No herd was counted there for anyone but its owner.
  _Only the five, and the word._

Nine generations, and the five come back.
Each could say no. None of them has said it.
That is why the yes is worth carrying.
  _None of them has said it._

I have carried it the length it was given me.
  _No longer, and no shorter._
Who has checked it?
  _We have checked it. We know it too._
Then it goes on to the next mouth,
and it waits for the next dry season.
Hear me, children of the five.
  _We hear you, keeper._
```
