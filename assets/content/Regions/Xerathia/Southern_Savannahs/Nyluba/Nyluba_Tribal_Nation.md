The **Nyáluba Tribal Nation** is a confederation of five distinct clan-nations occupying the southeastern quadrant of the [[place-sthrnsvnhs|Southern Savannahs]] of [[place-xerathia|Xerathia]]—the highland belt where the great upland rivers run year-round, the central grasslands where the herds migrate, and the riverine wetlands of the eastern coast. They are the largest organized political body in the savannahs, perhaps two and a half million people across a territory roughly the size of Provènzia and Tarvénia combined, and yet they have no king, no capital, no written law, no standing army, and no apparatus that would be recognized by an Aurèldían or Vylarian as a government. They have, instead, the **Long Pact**—an ancient covenant binding five clan-nations to a shared way of life—and the apparatus by which the Pact is renewed: the Great Indaba, the drum-network, the griots, and the totemic law.

Northerners who reach the Nyáluba—and almost none do, since the Northern Fertile civilizations know the savannahs only through rainforest intermediaries—typically misread what they find. They see no palace and infer no power. They see no army and infer no defense. They see no priesthood and infer no faith. They are wrong on all three counts, and the Nyáluba find their bewilderment quietly amusing.

## Geography

The Nyáluba territory occupies the southeastern third of the savannah region, a land of three distinct ecological belts that the five clans have apportioned among themselves through long custom and longer pact.

The **central grasslands** are the heart of Nyáluba country—rolling open plains broken by rocky kopjes, isolated baobabs, and seasonal pans that flood in the wet season and dry to cracked clay in the dry. The great herds of antelope, wild cattle, and zebra-analogues pass through these grasslands twice a year on their long north-south migrations, and the human population follows them. This is the country of the **Mvuzi**, the lion-clan, who have herded their cattle across these plains since time before memory.

The **southeastern highlands** rise abruptly from the central plains in a long escarpment of weathered red stone. Up on the highlands the air is cool and the rivers run year-round, and the soil is dense enough to support sustained agriculture. This is also the country of the **ruins**—the dry-stone walls, raised platforms, and oval enclosures of the lost civilization that built in stone before the Nyáluba were a people. The Nyáluba do not call these places ruins; they call them the **Old Kraals**, and they regard them as inhabited by the spirits of those who built them. The **Ngonzi**, the eagle-clan, hold the highlands and live among the ruins, and they alone among the clans claim a direct ancestral connection to the stone-builders.

The **eastern wetlands** are where the great rivers from the highlands spread and slow before reaching the eastern coast—a maze of papyrus channels, oxbow lakes, hippopotamus pools, and crocodile-haunted reaches. The **Kambezi**, the crocodile-clan, are the river-people: they fish and hunt in the wetlands, navigate the channels in long reed canoes, and maintain the most elaborate spirit-relationships of any Nyáluban clan, since the river-spirits of their country are numerous and unforgiving.

The **northern grass-and-tree belt**, where the central savannah grades into the southern margin of the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]], is the country of the **Sengala**, the leopard-clan. They are the trade-tribe—the only Nyáluban clan with sustained contact with the rainforest peoples to the north and, through them, with the distant civilizations of the [[place-nrthrnfrtlrgn|Northern Fertile Region]].

The **scattered settled-belts**—small districts of perennial water, dense soil, and sustained habitation that lie in pockets across the Nyáluban territory—are the country of the **Bombwe**, the baobab-clan. They are the only Nyáluban clan that builds in any permanence, the only one with anything resembling towns (a few thousand people each, gathered around an ancient sacred tree), and the keepers of the senior griot tradition.

The clan territories overlap considerably and are not maintained as borders. A Mvuzi cattle-drive may pass through Sengala country in the dry season; a Bombwe sacred grove may sit in the heart of Ngonzi territory; the Kambezi fish-camps follow the rivers wherever they go. Boundary disputes are rare and, when they occur, are referred to the Great Indaba.

## The Five Clans

The clans are not tribes in the loose ethnographic sense; they are sharply distinct cultural and political nations that share a language, a religion, and a pact, but that differ in dress, dialect, ritual, marriage custom, and self-conception. A Mvuzi cattle-herder and a Kambezi reed-fisher recognize each other as Nyáluban—fellow heirs of the Long Pact—but they would not mistake each other for the same people.

**Mvuzi**—the lion-clan. The most numerous of the five clans (perhaps a million souls), the cattle-herders of the central plains, traditionally the first among the clans because the original Long Pact was sworn in Mvuzi country by Mwána-Mvuzi herself. The Mvuzi paramount—by long custom a daughter of the senior Mvuzi lineage—convenes the Great Indaba and bears the responsibility of speaking last when consensus is sought. Mvuzi men wear a single lion-tooth necklace at adulthood; Mvuzi women braid their hair in patterns that record their lineage genealogy. Their wealth is cattle. Their courage is their watchword. Their humor, when they have any, is dry as the dry season.

**Kambezi**—the crocodile-clan. The river-people of the eastern wetlands, expert in the navigation of the channels and the ritual handling of the river-spirits. Smaller in numbers than the Mvuzi (perhaps four hundred thousand) but enormously influential because the great rivers that water all of Nyáluba country pass through Kambezi territory before reaching the sea. The Kambezi maintain the most elaborate shamanic tradition of any clan; their _mwalimu wa roho_ are sought across the savannahs for matters of judgment, healing, and the negotiation of difficult spirits. A Kambezi who has lost a relative to a crocodile speaks of that crocodile by name and lineage and considers the matter a family affair.

**Ngonzi**—the eagle-clan. The highland people, herders and goldsmiths and inheritors of the Old Kraals. About three hundred thousand. They are the only Nyáluban clan that regularly works stone—small structures, livestock walls, ceremonial platforms—and the only one with any direct knowledge of the techniques the lost stone-builders used. Their relationship to the Old Kraals is intimate, lineal, and largely opaque to outsiders, including to other Nyáluban clans. Ngonzi gold (alluvial, panned from the highland rivers, then worked by hammer and bone-tool into ornaments and ingots) is the only Nyáluban export that reaches the northern civilizations in any quantity.

**Sengala**—the leopard-clan. The northern trade-tribe, intermediaries between Nyáluba country and the rainforest peoples. About four hundred thousand. They are the most outwardly oriented of the clans, the most polyglot, and the most likely to know things about the world beyond the savannahs. They are also the most stratified—Sengala society includes a class of professional traders whose status approaches that of a hereditary specialist caste, an arrangement the other clans regard with a mixture of admiration and unease.

**Bombwe**—the baobab-clan. The settled cultivators of the perennial-water districts, and the keepers of the senior griot tradition. Smaller than the others (perhaps two hundred thousand), but ritually senior, since the official memory of the Long Pact and the genealogies of all five clans is carried by Bombwe griots. Each Bombwe town centers on an ancient baobab—some are a thousand years old or more—and the senior griot of each town is also the spiritual custodian of the tree. Bombwe court a slow, deliberate manner that suits their role; they speak last in any council, and what they say is generally remembered.

## The Long Pact

The **Long Pact** is the foundational covenant of the Nyáluba. By tradition it was sworn nine generations ago—perhaps two hundred and fifty years past, perhaps somewhat more—under a great baobab in central Mvuzi country, by **Mwána-Mvuzi** ("Daughter of the Lion"), a Mvuzi paramount whose name has since been carried by every senior Mvuzi woman as a hereditary title. The clans that swore the Pact were fleeing some catastrophe: the precise nature of the disaster has been polished smooth by generations of telling, but the Bombwe griots maintain that it was the southward expansion of a now-extinct militarist confederacy from the central savannahs, whose warriors took cattle, women, and lives indiscriminately. The five clans, until then mutual rivals, agreed to stand together rather than be picked apart.

The terms of the Pact, as the griots recite them, are deliberately few:

- The five clans will not raise arms against each other, and the cattle-feuds that prevailed before the Pact are forsworn.
- The five clans will assist each other in defense against any outside threat to any one of them.
- The five clans will share the great pasturelands, the rivers, and the rains, observing each other's ritual obligations to the spirits of those places.
- The five clans will renew the Pact, in person, at the Great Indaba, every fourth dry season.
- The Pact may be amended only by unanimous consent of all five paramounts, speaking before the assembled Indaba and the senior griots.

What the Pact does _not_ do is also significant. It does not impose a common law beyond its own provisions. It does not establish a central authority. It does not regulate the internal life of any clan. It does not create taxes, levies, or shared institutions beyond the Indaba itself. The clans remain wholly sovereign in their internal affairs, and the Pact is, in effect, a peace treaty plus a defensive alliance plus a ritual schedule—and nothing more.

The simplicity is by design. Mwána-Mvuzi is reported to have said, when pressed by allies who wanted a more elaborate constitution: _"We are not building a kingdom. We are agreeing not to destroy each other. The rest is for our daughters to decide for themselves."_ The griots quote her on this point at every Indaba.

## The Great Indaba

The **Great Indaba** is the working institution of the Nyáluba. It convenes every fourth dry season at **Indala-Bomba**, an ancient stone enclosure in the southeastern highlands—one of the great Old Kraals—that the Ngonzi maintain as the Indaba ground. It lasts roughly six weeks, during which delegations from each of the five clans camp in apportioned grounds outside the enclosure and conduct their business in formal session within.

Each clan sends a delegation of the same size—usually thirty to fifty people—composed of the clan's paramount, the paramount's senior advisors, the paramount's senior griot, the clan's three or four most experienced shamans, and a contingent of younger lineage representatives who are present primarily to observe and learn. The total assembled body is around two hundred people, plus several hundred more in support roles outside the enclosure.

The Indaba's order of business is fixed by long tradition: defense and the conduct of any war begun since the last Indaba; inter-clan disputes that the clan paramounts have been unable to resolve directly; the state of the pasturelands, rivers, and herds; matters of the totemic law that have arisen since the last Indaba; and finally the formal renewal of the Pact, conducted by the senior griots and witnessed by all. Decisions are made by consensus—that is, by the convergence of the five paramounts, each of whom speaks in turn with the Mvuzi paramount speaking last. The Indaba does not vote. If the paramounts cannot converge, the matter is held for the next Indaba; the Pact does not require resolution of every disagreement.

Outside observers consistently underestimate how much actual coordination the Indaba achieves. Because the Indaba ratifies decisions the clans have already reached through their own internal processes—the actual debate, in each clan, having taken place over the months leading up to the Indaba—the formal sessions can appear ceremonial. They are not. They are the moment at which the clan-internal decisions become Pact-level commitments, with the weight of the totemic spirits and the witness of the griots behind them.

## The Drum-Network

The Nyáluba maintain a continent-spanning communication system based on the **drum-language**—a tonal-mimetic encoding of the Nyáluban tongue that can carry complex messages over long distances. Specialized **drum-speakers** maintain a network of relay stations across all five clan territories, and a message can travel from the Sengala northern margin to the Kambezi eastern wetlands—a distance equivalent to the length of Aurèldía—in perhaps two days, depending on weather and relay availability.

The drum-network is the operational backbone of the confederation. The Great Indaba may meet only once every four years, but the paramounts are in nearly continuous consultation through the drums. Defense decisions, disaster response, news of births and deaths in senior lineages, ceremonial coordination, even market prices and herd movements all travel by drum. The northern civilizations, which depend on relay riders or seasonal caravans, would find the speed of Nyáluba inter-clan communication startling if they knew of it. The Sengala have not been forthcoming with the rainforest peoples about how the drums actually work.

## Religion

The Nyáluba practice the [[affiliation-nyalbpnthn|totemic faith]] of the southern savannahs—a layered system of clan totems, land-spirits, water-spirits, hunt-spirits, and ancestor-spirits, related to but distinct from any of the codified pantheons of the wider world. The faith is organized around the **Three Postures**—venerate, appease, ward against—and is administered by a distributed body of religious specialists: griots (keepers of memory), drum-speakers (keepers of communication), shamans (keepers of the spirits), and elders (keepers of the totemic law). There is no priesthood in the Aurèldían sense, no temple establishment, and no central religious authority.

Each of the five clans maintains its own primary totem (the lion of the Mvuzi, the crocodile of the Kambezi, the eagle of the Ngonzi, the leopard of the Sengala, the baobab of the Bombwe) and its own subordinate totems, ancestor-shrines, and land-spirit relationships. The clans recognize each other's totems as legitimate kinsmen of their own clan-spirits, and inter-clan ceremonies—at the Indaba, at the cattle-circulations, at the great seasonal markets—formally acknowledge all five primary totems together.

For the full description of the totemic system, the spirits, and the religious specialists, see [[affiliation-nyalbpnthn|Nyáluba Pantheon]] and [[lore-nyalbsprts|Nyáluba Spirits]].

## Economy

The Nyáluba economy is built on **cattle**, **agriculture**, **gold**, and **river-trade**—in roughly that order of cultural importance, though not necessarily of monetary value.

**Cattle** are the social currency of all five clans. Cattle move constantly: through bridewealth (every marriage transfers cattle from the groom's lineage to the bride's), through seasonal lending (a clan with surplus pasture in a wet year lends cattle to a clan with surplus need), through gift-exchange (a chief honors a guest with the gift of a cow, knowing the gift will be reciprocated when the chief is the guest), and through ritual sacrifice (cattle are slaughtered at every major ceremony, with the meat distributed to all present). Wealth that does not move is, to the Nyáluban mind, dead wealth. A clan that hoards cattle is a clan that is failing in its obligations.

**Agriculture**—sorghum, millet, gourds, root crops, certain pulses—is practiced wherever water and soil permit, most intensively by the Bombwe in the perennial-water districts and by the Ngonzi on the highland terraces. Surplus grain moves through the same circulation networks as cattle, though with less ceremonial weight.

**Gold**—alluvial, panned from the highland rivers and worked by Ngonzi smiths—is the principal Nyáluba export. Worked gold (ornaments, ingots, ceremonial pieces) flows north through the Sengala to the rainforest peoples, who pass it onward to the Northern Fertile civilizations. The Ngonzi maintain a near-monopoly on gold-working, and the techniques are guarded along clan lineage lines.

**River-trade**—Kambezi reed-canoes carrying fish, salt (from a few coastal pans), papyrus, and exotic riverine goods up the eastern river systems to the highlands and the central plains. The Kambezi monopolize the river-trade in the same way the Sengala monopolize the northern overland trade.

**Imports**—metal goods (iron knives, hoe-blades, spear-heads), beads, glass, fine cloth, and certain medicinal plants and ritual pigments—come almost entirely through the Sengala. Total trade volume is small by Vylarian or Haradian standards but is significant for the goods involved, and the Sengala traders who manage it are wealthy by any measure.

## Defense and War

The Nyáluba maintain no standing army. Each clan musters its warriors when need arises, organized by **age-set regiments**—cohorts of young men (and, among the Mvuzi and Kambezi, young women) who pass through their initiation together, share a regimental name and an age-set totem, and remain bound by that fellowship for life. The age-set system makes mobilization quick: a paramount calls for the regiments of a particular age-cohort, and within days several thousand trained fighters can be in the field.

The clans have not fought each other since the Pact. They have fought, on rare occasions, against external threats: pre-Pact raiders from the central plains, occasional incursions by Bethuan slaving parties along the northern margin, and the great drought-war of three generations past, when famine pushed neighboring tribes into the Sengala country and the regiments had to be called out. They have not lost a war in living memory.

The Nyáluban warrior tradition is built around mobility, surprise, the intelligent use of terrain, and overwhelming local concentration of force. Pitched battles are rare. Ambushes, drives, encirclements, and the use of the drum-network to coordinate dispersed forces are the standard methods. A Nyáluban field commander who lost the element of surprise would be considered to have failed in the basic discipline of the craft, regardless of the outcome.

## Relations with Neighboring Peoples

To the **north**, beyond the Sengala margin, lie the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforest]] peoples. Relations are sustained, generally peaceful, and culturally important—the Sengala maintain marriage exchanges with several frontier rainforest tribes, and a substantial body of shared religious and economic practice has built up over generations. The rainforest peoples are also the Nyáluba's window onto the world beyond the savannahs, and the Sengala carefully cultivate that channel.

To the **far north**, beyond the rainforest, lie the civilizations of the [[place-nrthrnfrtlrgn|Northern Fertile Region]]—Bethua, Ta'Kheperu, Okháris. The Nyáluba know these peoples almost entirely through hearsay and through the secondhand goods that arrive via the Sengala. Bethuan slave-raiders did reach the northern Sengala margin twice in living memory; both expeditions were destroyed by mobilized age-set regiments before they could withdraw, and Bethua has not tried again. The Nyáluba consider the Northern Fertile peoples to be impressive in their stone-building and agriculture but politically deformed—too centralized, too reliant on hereditary autocrats, too quick to coerce their own people.

To the **west**, in the central and southwestern savannahs beyond the Mvuzi territory, lie the **dry-country tribes**—smaller, more dispersed, more nomadic peoples who are not part of the Long Pact and do not consider themselves Nyáluban. Relations are mixed: extensive trade and intermarriage in some places, sustained low-level raiding in others. The Mvuzi paramounts maintain individual relationships with the major dry-country chieftains and rely on personal diplomacy more than confederation policy.

To the **south and southeast**, beyond the Kambezi wetlands and the Ngonzi highlands, lie the unnamed coastal peoples and, beyond them, the southern ocean. The Nyáluba have no significant contact with the coastal peoples and no contact at all with whatever lies across the southern ocean.

## Famous Figures

**Nzobu Mvuzi**, current Mvuzi paramount and convener of the Great Indaba (sixth Mwána-Mvuzi to bear the hereditary name). About fifty years old, widely respected across all five clans for her judgment and her command of the totemic law. She has presided over three Indabas; the next will be her fourth.

**Bonzimbe of the Bombwe**, senior griot of the Long Pact. The official memory-bearer of the Pact's terms, the genealogies of all five paramounts, and the recorded judgments of the previous twenty-three Indabas. Approximately seventy years old, blind, and considered by all five clans to be the closest living thing to a neutral arbiter the Nyáluba possess.

**Mvura wa Kambezi**, senior _mwalimu wa roho_ of the Kambezi, said to have walked into a particular Old Kraal during her initiation and emerged speaking the Stone Tongue—the language no living Nyáluban understands but which the Ngonzi elders say was the speech of the lost stone-builders. The Ngonzi confirm the report; the Bombwe griots have entered it into the Indaba record. What the Stone Tongue is for, no one will say.

**The Drum-Master of Sengala**, an unnamed (or rather, name-suppressed) figure who maintains the central relay station of the northern drum-network. By long custom, the holder of this post has no public identity; only the other drum-masters know who occupies it at any given time. The arrangement is a security measure that has held for nine generations.

## Commerce and Currency

Nyáluba Tribal Nation operates almost entirely on [[lore-bartercnmy|barter]] and [[lore-kinhalcrdt|tribal credit]] customs. Coin is essentially absent from internal commerce; the few foreign coins that reach Nyáluban hands through long-distance trade are valued as exotic goods rather than as currency. Commerce with the Northern Fertile Region (Ta'Kheperan currency through southern trade-routes) is more developed than commerce with the western Vylarian system.
