The Southern Savannahs are the vast subtropical grasslands of southern [[place-xerathia|Xerathia]]—open herd-country that stretches from the southern edge of the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]] to the continent's southern coasts. Where the northern crescent teems with irrigated cities and the central rainforests choke with jungle, the deep south is spare: wide skies, long horizons, great herds moving with the seasons, and scattered peoples who follow them.

To the civilizations of the [[place-nrthrnfrtlrgn|Northern Fertile Region]], the savannahs are less a place than a rumor. Almost everything those civilizations know about the deep south comes second-hand through the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|rainforest tribes]], who maintain seasonal trade relationships with the savannah herders along the long southern margin where jungle gives way to grassland. Direct expeditions from the north have been rare, ill-advised, and mostly did not return.

## Geography

The savannahs are enormous—deeper north-to-south than the northern crescent is wide, and spanning the continent from its western coast to its eastern. Within this expanse the land is not uniform: it changes character as one travels south from the rainforest margin.

The **northern savannah belt**, just south of the forests, is the wettest part of the region—tall grass, scattered tree-groves, seasonal wetlands where monsoon-like rains flood great pans for part of the year. The great herds calve here during the wet season and move south during the dry. This belt is the most densely populated part of the savannahs, such population as there is, and the only stretch with any sustained contact with the forest peoples to the north.

The **central savannah** is open grassland—rolling plains broken by rocky kopjes, isolated hills, and the occasional acacia or baobab. Rainfall is seasonal and unreliable. The great herds pass through twice a year in their migrations, and the human peoples follow. This is classic herd country: the kind of place where a tribe's wealth is measured in cattle and the ability to read the sky for the coming of the rains is a survival skill equal to any martial art.

The **southwestern reaches** shade into true aridity—a belt of pale stone, red sand, and sparse vegetation where even the herds will not go for much of the year. Something like a proper desert exists in the far southwest, though the savannah peoples regard it less as a desert than as an ancestral home to which their dead return. Scattered rock-carvings and painted caves along the fringes of this dry country suggest occupation by peoples older than any living tradition.

The **southeast** is greener—a highland belt where upland rivers run year-round and the grass is denser. This is where the ruins are: the stone-walled enclosures, raised platforms, and mysterious passageways of some earlier civilization whose name has been lost. The ruins are known among the savannah peoples but are not discussed with outsiders; the forest intermediaries have learned not to ask.

The **far southern coasts** touch an unnamed ocean from which no northern ship has ever made landfall. Savannah peoples do not seem to be seafarers, though coastal communities presumably exist; the north simply has no reliable information about them.

## Peoples and Culture

The savannah peoples are **nomadic and semi-nomadic pastoralists**, **hunter-gatherers**, and—in a few favored localities—**settled cultivators** around perennial water sources. Their social organization is by **clan and lineage** rather than kingdom or empire; no single authority has ever claimed to rule more than a small fraction of the region, and most of the savannah peoples would not recognize such a claim as meaningful.

The **great herds** are the organizing principle of savannah life in the center and east. Antelope, wild cattle, zebra-analogues, giraffes, and several species of large herbivore whose northern names are imprecise or wrong graze the grasslands in enormous migrating populations. The human peoples who follow them maintain an intimate, ritually-structured relationship with specific herd-lineages, hunting selectively, timing their movements to the herds', and observing an elaborate etiquette of what can be taken, when, and with what apologies.

**Cattle** are central—both wild and domesticated. Cattle are wealth, dowry, religious offering, and walking larder. A savannah household's cattle-count is its social ranking. Raiding for cattle is both an economic activity and a rite of passage for young warriors, and cattle-feuds between clans can persist across generations.

**Language** across the region is a mosaic. There are several broad language families, each containing many mutually unintelligible tongues, and the savannah peoples maintain a rough pidgin—something like a trade-speech—for dealing with strangers from outside their immediate lineage. The rainforest intermediaries who trade with the northern civilizations use this pidgin as their working language in the south.

The few **settled communities**—Ékunda among them—function as trade-meeting points, ritual centers, or both. They are small by northern standards (a thousand people is large), typically located at perennial springs or river junctions, and often have populations that swell and shrink dramatically with the migration cycles.

## Religion

Like the rainforest peoples, the savannah peoples do not maintain a pantheon in any form the northern scholars would recognize. What they have instead is a layered complex of **ancestor veneration**, **herd-lineage spirits**, **land-patrons** tied to specific springs or mountains, and a diffuse but nearly universal recognition of a **sky-creator** whose name differs from tribe to tribe but whose nature is consistent: distant, uninvolved in daily life, appealed to only at moments of great crisis or great gratitude.

Ancestor practice is the most elaborate component. Major lineages maintain detailed genealogical recitation, funerary rites that commit the dead to specific sacred places, and a system of consultation in which lineage elders petition the ancestors for guidance in matters of succession, migration, and dispute. The ancestors are understood as continuing members of the lineage, differently embodied but not absent.

**The ruins of the southeast** generate a distinctive religious practice, largely opaque to outsiders, in which specific lineages claim ancestral connection to the lost stone-builders and make periodic pilgrimages to particular ruins. What is said at these pilgrimages, and what is asked for or offered, is not shared with people from other lineages, much less with northerners.

## Politics and Power

No polity, in the northern sense, exists in the Southern Savannahs. The political units are **lineages and clan-confederations**, with informal alliances that shift with seasonal pressures, marriages, raids, and feuds. A particularly successful raid-leader or particularly wise elder may command the loyalty of multiple lineages for a generation, but such personal authority rarely outlasts its holder.

This stateless organization is not a pre-political condition—the savannah peoples have functioning, sophisticated social systems. It is a deliberate form, adapted to terrain and economy, and the savannah peoples generally consider northern political arrangements (static cities, hereditary kings, bureaucracies) to be interesting but inferior.

## Trade

Savannah trade with the outside world runs almost entirely through the **rainforest intermediaries** along the northern margin. What the savannahs export: hides, bone and horn work, ostrich feathers, ivory, gold (from the southeastern highlands, where small-scale alluvial mining has been practiced for uncounted generations), and certain medicinal plants. What they import: metal goods (knives, hoe-blades, beads), glass, cloth, and salt.

Savannah-internal trade is largely through **cattle exchange**, marriage-gifts, and the limited resources (salt pans, metal sources, perennial water) that not all clans possess. A savannah clan that controls a salt pan is a rich clan, and salt disputes can ripen into the kind of multi-generational feuds that northerners associate with royal succession.

## Relations with Neighboring Regions

To the **north** lies the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]]. The savannah peoples and the forest peoples meet along a long, messy, ecologically transitional margin where jungle gives way to grassland. Contact is generally peaceful—long generations of seasonal overlap have produced a dense web of trading relationships, intermarriage, and ritualized exchanges. The forest tribes are the savannah peoples' principal contact with the wider world and vice versa.

The **[[place-nrthrnfrtlrgn|Northern Fertile Region]]** civilizations—Bethua, Ta'Kheperu, Okháris—are known to the savannah peoples only through forest intermediaries and through secondhand goods that reach the northern margins via trade. Most savannah peoples have never met a Kheperi, Bethuan, or Okhári in person; the northern civilizations are a distant rumor of strangely dressed people who live in places where buildings do not move. Savannah opinion of these northerners, insofar as it is formed, tends to range from amused curiosity to wary respect to frank disinterest.

To the **far south** lie the coasts of an unnamed ocean and whatever exists beyond it. Savannah knowledge of the southern coast is incomplete—different lineages know different stretches, and no unified picture has ever been assembled. Whatever lies beyond the southern coast is, for the savannah peoples, as mysterious as the savannah itself is to the north.
