## Overview

Kalihara is the only country in the world that has never had a war, and the only one whose people say their civilization was given to them. A Heliónite factor who has traded at [[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]] for twenty years tells a newcomer both things on the first evening, and then admits that they are the whole of what he knows for certain. The island lies in the western ocean, four hundred and fifty to seven hundred miles west of [[place-okharisrgn|Okháris]] and [[place-provenzrgn|Provènzia]], about a week's sail in fair weather and out of sight of land the whole way, and it holds one of the oldest continuous civilizations on [[place-worldthlrn|Thalorna]], older by millennia than the arrival of the [[lore-flksinale|Sinalë]] and the [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazári]]. Three million Kaliharans live on it. Outsiders are almost never admitted to the interior, and the rare one who is goes in through [[place-klhrcldpss|the Cloud Pass]] blindfolded. Trade with the world runs through four ports on the eastern coast, and even there foreign merchants keep to their own quarters under the eyes of the harbor-wardens.

What the world knows of Kalihara is a land of jungle, a ring of cloud-wrapped mountains and creatures found nowhere else, from the [[being-cnpyglr|Canopy Glider]] of the high forest to the [[being-mbrydct|Ember-eyed Cat]] beneath it, beautiful, dangerous and closed to the curious. The Kaliharans say very little about their home to outsiders, and what they say is chosen with care. Scholars in [[place-helionis|Heliónis]] and [[place-byzariargn|Byzaría]] have spent centuries assembling the fragments into competing theories of Kaliharan society, and most of those theories describe Ankarian assumptions better than they describe Kalihara. The factor's advice is the island's own: watch, and do not ask twice.

## The Precursors

The foundation of Kaliharan civilization is an event so old that the Kaliharans themselves hold only fragments of the original account, kept in oral tradition, in carved stone records, and in the silent testimony of structures that have stood for ten thousand years or more.

In the deepest prehistory of Thalorna, long before the Elder Races raised their realms and long before the first human kingdoms rose on the Ankarian mainland, beings came to Kalihara who were not of this world. The Kaliharans call them the [[lore-theithari|Ithári]] ("Those Who Came Before"), and the record describes them as people and not as gods or spirits: extraordinarily advanced, holding knowledge and capabilities beyond anything Thalorna has seen before or since, and recognizably mortal in their concerns and conduct. The Ithári did not conquer Kalihara. They settled it, and the Kaliharan record shows them established here and nowhere else, which is what that record is in a position to show, since what it records is the island. What they were doing on Kalihara, and whether the island was the whole of their business or a part of it, is one of the great unanswered questions of Kaliharan philosophy.

The record is clear that the Ithári found humans already on the island, small scattered groups living in the most rudimentary conditions, and that they took those people in. Over generations they taught the early Kaliharans agriculture, medicine, governance, a vocabulary for all of it, and the way of understanding the world on which everything Kaliharan civilization has built since rests.

The Ithári revered the natural order. Their teaching, as the Kaliharans have kept it, holds that the world is one living whole and not a collection of separate things: every tree, creature, stone, river and breath of wind takes part in a single connected totality, which the Kaliharans call **Thári'vaan** ("The Living Whole," or, less precisely, "The Great Other"). The whole is not to be worshipped, appeased or bargained with; it is to be understood, respected and maintained. To damage any part is to damage the whole, and to tend any part is to tend the whole. The purpose of a civilization, in Ithári teaching, is to take part in the world with understanding and care, and never to master it.

In **7,300 BF** the Ithári departed. The record gives the manner and not the reason: a disturbance of some kind went through them, they went to the caretakers of the instruments and gave them a further set of instructions in haste, and then they were gone, all of them, abruptly. Whether they returned to wherever they came from, moved on to some other purpose, or met a fate beyond Kaliharan knowledge, nobody on the island can say. The departure is the great silence at the heart of Kaliharan thought: the Ithári were simply no longer there, and the civilization they had built was left in the hands of those they had raised. The world-level account of the Ithári, of the works they left elsewhere on Thalorna and of the one occasion an Elder Race met them is [[lore-theithari|The Ithári]].

The Kaliharans did not collapse. They kept what had been given to them, the knowledge, the structures, the philosophy and the procedures, and eighty centuries later they keep it still.

## Cosmology and Faith

Ask the factor what the Kaliharans worship and he answers, correctly, that they do not. There are no gods on Kalihara, no deities who answer prayers, grant blessings or demand sacrifice, no doctrine of an afterlife and no reward or punishment beyond death. What there is instead is Thári'vaan, the Living Whole, and the three habits by which every Kaliharan takes part in it: the daily watching of some corner of the world, the tending of the garden, grove or shore that is one's own, and the exact keeping of inherited practice. The tradition note [[affiliation-tharivaan|Thári'vaan]] sets out the observances, the custodians' ranks and the liturgies in full.

The Kaliharans hold the connectedness of things to be observed fact and not doctrine, confirmed by several thousand years of attention to one island: the canopy and the reef, the volcanic soil and the monsoon, the household and the birds in its gardens, the [[being-dsksft|Dusk Swift]] flocks among them, each part's health answering to every other's. The posture that follows is a reverence without worship, a respect for the world as it is with an obligation attached. Kaliharans do not pray; they observe. They do not make offerings; they tend.

The nearest thing to a priesthood is a custodianship. The **Ithári'kaan** ("Keepers of What Was Left") are men and women trained from childhood in the relationships of the island's ecology, its seasonal cycles, its agricultural practice, and the maintenance of the ancient structures the Ithári left behind, and they form the spiritual and institutional heart of Kaliharan society. Maintenance here means tending and procedure and nothing else: the substance of a structure cannot be worked on, so there has never been anything for a custodian to mend, replace or shore up. Over eighty centuries the understanding of what the procedures are for has drifted. Sequences of action the Ithári may have meant as plain upkeep have taken on layers of ritual meaning, and the Ithári'kaan now perform them as liturgy, to the letter, in the settled conviction that the island's health depends on it, and with the original purpose forgotten or transformed into ceremony.

## The Ancient Structures {#the-ancient-structures}

Scattered across the interior of Kalihara stand the structures the Ithári built, and after ten millennia they are standing and in many cases working. Works of the same substance stand at scattered sites elsewhere on Thalorna, but only Kalihara knows that they share a maker, or that there was a maker. They are intact installations, not ruins, made of a material that resembles stone and is plainly something else.

To the eye the material is marble: smooth, softly lustrous, a uniform gray with no vein, grain or figure anywhere in it, the same across a whole chamber. To the hand it gives very slightly, like supple leather, and returns when the pressure lifts; Kaliharans grow up with this, and visitors find it hard to be easy about. It has no seam, joint or mortar, untouched by weather or by age, and in some places warm to the touch or faintly luminous in darkness. Nothing harms it. No tool, no fire and no working of any tradition has ever marked one. Each work is of one continuous piece, with no seam and no crack anywhere on it and no straight edge in any of it: ribbed and vaulted forms whose walls curve into floor and ceiling, entered through smooth oval openings with no lintel, jamb or threshold. Those openings have no door in them. The substance itself dilates, widening from the center and closing again smoothly and without sound, and the custodians' liturgies include the procedures that work them. That is the plainest case of what the whole custodianship is: an Ithári'kaan can open a given structure reliably and cannot say what it is that opens, and beyond Kalihara, where nobody has the procedures, whether a work admits a person at all appears to be nobody's to decide.

Nothing can be cut from a structure, added to one or joined to one, and no structure has ever been shifted from where it stands by any means anyone has tried. What the Kaliharans can do with them is use them where they are and build around them, which is the whole of what anyone can do, and it is why they are still here when every other ancient work on Thalorna was long ago robbed for its stone. The Kaliharans call the material **ithári'sul** ("precursor stone"), and they treat it with the same reverence they give the rest of the natural world, because in their understanding it is part of the natural world, placed there by the Ithári as part of the whole.

Some of the structures have observable effects. Certain installations appear to influence the weather, calming storms before they reach the island's interior or directing rainfall to the terraces in a dry season. Others seem to act on the sea itself: the currents around Kalihara behave in ways experienced sailors find uncanny, and the approaches are notoriously difficult without local knowledge. Whether the effects are produced by the structures or merely credited to them by Kaliharan tradition, no outsider can determine, since no outsider has been permitted to examine one.

What is increasingly apparent, though the Kaliharans speak of it only in the most guarded terms, is that the structures are beginning to fail. Effects once reliable have become intermittent; installations that glowed steadily for centuries glow weakly or not at all. These are living structures faltering, still gray, still giving under the hand, doing their office badly rather than ceasing to be, and they are where all the custodians' anxiety sits, because a work going wrong gives no sign of how long it has. Everything wrong with it is in what it does; nothing is in what it is, and there is nothing whatever to inspect. A few structures have stopped outright without altering in any way, and a few more have gone bone-white and hard, the give gone out of them, one or two of those since opening cracks. A whitened structure is unmistakable at a glance and beyond question dead; nothing goes part white. None of this is wear or damage. The substance takes no harm from anything, and a work that has ended has ended of itself.

The Ithári'kaan perform their liturgies with growing urgency, and there are whispered accounts of structures that have ceased despite the custodians' best efforts. The Kaliharans have no understanding of what underlies the works; they know the procedures and not the principles. A liturgy that has kept a structure working for a hundred generations may one day simply stop working, and the Ithári'kaan will have no way to repair what they do not understand. This slow failure is the most closely guarded secret of Kaliharan civilization. If the structures that calm the storms fail, the harvest goes with them. If the structures that make the approaches treacherous fail, the island loses its best defense against invasion. The Kaliharans have lived on the Ithári's legacy for eighty centuries, and what happens when it gives out is a question the Ithári'kaan contemplate in silence, because they have no answer.

## The Elder Races

When the [[lore-flksinale|Sinalë]] first arrived on Thalorna the Ithári were still in the world, and it was the Sinalë who met them: they came upon them by chance, in a place the Sinalë have never named, and came away with the impression that they were found quaint and politely declined any dealings. It is the only occasion on record of anyone outside Kalihara meeting the Ithári, and Kalihara had no part in it. The Sinalë learned nothing from it, not who they had met, not where those people had come from, not the name the Kaliharans use, and nothing that would have joined that afternoon to this island's builders. Kalihara has never told them, or anyone else.

The Ithári departed within two centuries. When the Sinalë afterward reached Kalihara and sought contact, it was the Kaliharans who answered: they received them with courtesy and refused them settlement. The pattern repeated when the [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazári]] arrived. In both cases the Kaliharan position was clear and unchanging. Kalihara was complete. It required no new inhabitants, no new knowledge and no new relationships; the Elder Races were welcome to trade at the ports, and the interior was closed.

The refusal was courteous and final, and it came from the same insularity that has defined Kalihara since the Ithári left. The island holds that the Ithári did not expand beyond it, did not seek to reshape the wider world, and tended what was theirs and left the rest alone, and it takes that as the example to follow. This is a belief, and one Kalihara has no means of testing: the only record it reasons from is its own, and that record describes an island. The Kaliharans have followed the example with extraordinary consistency across eighty centuries. They do not believe themselves superior to other peoples. They believe that their way of life is sufficient, that their island provides everything they need, and that entanglement with the outside world can only disturb a balance they have spent a very long time keeping.

## Geography

Kalihara holds [[place-kaliharargn|Kalihara Region]], the whole of the island's inhabited country, which is very nearly the whole of the island: some four hundred miles across and nearly as far from north to south. The island falls into three countries. The coast is jungle lowland and terraced river valley, with the four ports on its eastern shore and islets. [[place-klhrring|The Ring]] is the circle of tall volcanic mountains that closes the island on every side, crossed by three passes and broken in one place by a gap. [[place-klhrbowl|The Bowl]] inside it is the interior, mostly forest, with [[place-klhrlake|the lake]] at its center and [[place-klhrmassif|the Massif]] standing in the water, and on the Massif the oldest of the sanctuaries. The lineages hold the land of all three, each named for the feature it holds and tends, and the ports stand apart under their own structure.

The island note [[place-klhrcntnnt|Kalihara]] carries the land, the climate, the seas around it and the long way home, and the region note carries the country as it is lived in.

## The Port Cities

The interior of Kalihara is closed, and the Kaliharans have never been disconnected from the world; they decided where it may touch them. Four port settlements on the eastern coast and its islets are the interface between Kaliharan civilization and the rest of Thalorna. The greatest is [[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]], a trading port of eighteen thousand open to every flag, where the Council sits. [[place-nalurithan|Nalurithan]], [[place-vashuran|Vashurán]] and [[place-telvari|Telvári]] are smaller and more tightly held, each serving particular goods or particular partners: Nalurithan the island's own coasting trade and the gem rivers behind it, Vashurán the Tarûzelian captains, Telvári the pilots who bring every foreign hull in. A port's Harbor-Warden keeps its anchorage and decides what a foreigner may see from it; Ilovanu's is the principal anchorage open to foreign hulls, and the only one most foreign masters ever use.

The ports are in many ways a world apart from the interior. Centuries of contact with Ankarian, Tarûzelian and Vedyaran merchants have left them with mixed populations, mixed marriages and, in Ilovanu, well-kept temples of the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aurèldían]] pantheon serving the foreign merchants, the long-settled immigrant families, and the Kaliharans who have chosen the port over the interior and in some cases taken up the foreigners' faith. The Ithári'kaan regard the ports with pragmatic acceptance and philosophical distance. The ports are necessary, they bring goods and information that even Kalihara sometimes requires, and they are not part of the Kaliharan world in the way the interior is.

The division runs deeper than geography. A Kaliharan born in Ilovanu who has never been admitted inland has more in common, culturally, with a merchant in Heliónis than with a custodian in an interior sanctuary. Both sides understand and accept the division: in the Kaliharan view it is the cost of keeping a necessary interface with the world without letting the world come further in than it should.

## Governance

Kaliharan governance is one of the oldest continuous political traditions on Thalorna, and one of the hardest for an outsider to describe, largely because the Kaliharans have no interest in describing it. What can be pieced together from port accounts and the rare observations of traders permitted slightly deeper access is a system of extraordinary sophistication: layered councils, rotating responsibilities, and decisions balanced among regional, familial and custodial authority. It produces stable government without concentrating power in any one person or institution. The standings a Kaliharan can hold, from the Severed, who is spoken to by no one, to the Speaker for the Island, who alone may answer a foreign power, are listed above.

The Council sits at Ilovanu. Its members are seated by lineage and by charge over the works: Elders sit for their lineages, and custodians for the works in their charge. The Speaker presides. Every custodian, of whichever of the four ranks the faith keeps, stands at the fifth level of the island's order, below the Elders and above the Wardens of the Approaches; within the custodianship the faith's own ranks govern.

Beside the order of standing runs another, and it does not match. The works answer people unevenly and not by rank: a person a work answers feels whether that work is working, working badly, stopped or dead, and gains nothing else by it. Neither blood nor office decides who is answered. A bondservant may be answered by [[place-sulvaan|the Warm]] and an Elder by nothing, and the failing of the works scrambles who is answered without moving anybody's standing. The faith note sets out [[affiliation-tharivaan#the-answering|the answering]] in full.

The Ithári'kaan carry great influence and are not rulers in any conventional sense. Their authority is custodial: they keep the ancient knowledge and the ancient structures, and temporal decisions rest with secular councils whose composition and selection are, to outsiders, essentially unknown. The ports run under a separate structure with the flexibility foreign trade requires, while the interior follows older forms refined over millennia.

The result is a society that has avoided the cycles of expansion, civil war and collapse that mark most civilizations on Thalorna. Kalihara has never had an empire, never fought a war of conquest, and never suffered the dynastic upheavals that reshape continental nations every few centuries. Whether the stability comes from the councils, from the commitment to Thári'vaan, from the island's isolation, or from all three together is a question Kaliharan scholars presumably debate among themselves, and not with outsiders.

## Commerce and Currency

Kalihara stands outside the continental monetary systems. Internal commerce runs substantially on [[lore-bartercnmy|barter]] and on the island's own traditions of lineage credit, and the few foreign coins that reach the island through the port trade are valued as exotic goods and not as money. No foreigner has seen how the interior settles a debt, and no Kaliharan has explained it; what the ports take for spice and stones is foreign goods, foreign coin by weight, and letters of credit the merchant houses of Ilovanu honor among themselves.
