Jürthāt is a mountainous island kingdom to the southeast of the [[affiliation-tanvurempr|Empire of Tānvür]], separated from the mainland by a treacherous strait. Once a province of the empire, Jürthāt broke away over a thousand years ago in a bloody war of secession and has since developed its own fiercely independent culture. The empire still refers to Jürthāt by the dismissive name **Thātvōng** (Thātvōng, "Kingdom of Mountains and Seas") and has never formally recognized its sovereignty, considering it a renegade province in open rebellion.

Jürthāt's culture is unmistakably shaped by its Tānvür origins—the caste structures, the written script, the religious calendar, and the broad outlines of the Celestial Order all echo the mainland. But a millennium of separation has warped these shared roots into something distinctly Jürthātese, and the islanders are deeply proud of what they have become.

## The Emperor of Ten Thousand Dawns

Jürthāt maintains an emperor—the **Vānlëng Tëngzhëi** (Vānlëng Tëngzhëi, "Son of Heaven of Ten Thousand Dawns")—who claims an unbroken line of descent from the last imperial governor who led the secession. The emperor performs elaborate seasonal rituals, receives foreign ambassadors, and serves as the ceremonial heart of the nation. His court is a pageant of ancient protocol and exquisite aesthetics.

In practice, the emperor commands almost nothing. He has no standing army, controls no territory beyond the imperial capital district, and depends on the great lords for his household's upkeep. The court's real function is as a stage for the lords to compete for prestige and proximity to divine legitimacy—being named the emperor's chief advisor or receiving a court title carries immense social weight, even though the titles confer no actual authority. The emperor's power lies entirely in this ability to grant or withhold honor, a currency the warrior lords value above gold.

## The Great Lords

True power in Jürthāt resides with a handful of **Dākzhük** (Dākzhük, "Great Lords"), regional warlords who control the island's mountainous provinces as effectively independent fiefdoms. Each Dākzhük maintains their own army, administers their own justice, collects their own taxes, and conducts their own foreign trade. They owe formal allegiance to the emperor but treat this obligation as ceremonial—a matter of face rather than submission.

Below the Dākzhük sit the **Shëitzhük** (Shëitzhük, "Lesser Lords"), minor feudal holders who pledge loyalty to a Great Lord in exchange for land and protection. This cascading web of personal oaths and obligations holds the kingdom together—barely. Feuds between the Great Lords are constant, ranging from trade wars and border skirmishes to full-scale campaigns that can engulf entire provinces. The emperor occasionally attempts to mediate, but his rulings carry weight only when backed by a Great Lord willing to enforce them.

The current balance of power rests on three or four Great Lords of roughly equal strength, none willing to risk open war against a coalition of the others. This equilibrium is fragile, and every generation or so it collapses into a period of open warfare before a new balance emerges.

## Honor and Pride

Personal honor is the organizing principle of Jürthātese society, more deeply embedded even than in Tānvür's warrior caste. A lord's word is sacred; broken oaths are remembered for generations and can poison alliances centuries later. Insults—real or perceived—demand satisfaction, and the rituals of formal challenge and duel are codified in exhaustive detail.

The Jürthātese practice their own form of ritual suicide, **Rëitsīk** (Rëitsīk, "Sacrificial Death"), which evolved from the Tānvür tradition of [[lore-zeisik|Zëisīk]] but carries different cultural weight. Where Zëisīk in Tānvür is primarily an act of atonement or defiance, Rëitsīk in Jürthāt is more broadly understood—it can express protest, preserve a secret, demonstrate loyalty to a fallen lord, or simply assert that one's honor is more precious than one's life. A lord who orders a subordinate's Rëitsīk wields that power carefully, knowing it reflects on his own judgment as much as on the condemned.

## Relationship with Tānvür

Jürthāt's attitude toward the empire is a tangle of resentment, cultural debt, and fierce superiority. The islanders acknowledge their Tānvür heritage—they could hardly deny it, given the shared script, religious framework, and caste echoes—but insist that they have purified and elevated what the mainland corrupted. They view the empire as bloated, decadent, and spiritually fallen, a civilization that lost the Mandate of Heaven centuries ago and is too proud to admit it.

The core of this belief is religious: the Jürthātese hold that when their ancestors broke away, heaven itself blessed the act. The original secession is framed not as rebellion but as a divine calling—the gods withdrew their favor from the empire and bestowed it upon the Nine Mountains. The emperor's unbroken lineage is offered as proof: where Tānvür's imperial line has been interrupted by conquest and civil war, Jürthāt's has endured. To the islanders, this is evidence that they are heaven's chosen people, and the mainland is a fallen realm.

This belief fuels both their pride and their aggression. Jürthāt's superior navy raids the empire's southern coast, and the Great Lords openly claim sovereignty over coastal territories that were once part of their ancestral province. The empire views this as piracy and insurrection; Jürthāt views it as the rightful reclamation of lands that heaven intended for them.

## Divergence from Tānvür

A thousand years of separation have produced meaningful cultural differences:

**Caste system:** Jürthāt inherited Tānvür's caste structure but has bent it around its feudal reality. The warrior lords dominate completely—there is no equivalent of the Tānvür balance between Zhëklüng, Vëndük, and Vüshōk. Magic-users exist but are subordinate to their lords in a way that even the Vëndük's chattel status doesn't quite capture; in Jürthāt, a sorcerer is simply another retainer, not a separate pillar of power.

**Religion:** The Celestial Order is recognizable but has fractured into regional variations. Each Great Lord's domain tends to emphasize the deities that suit their territory—coastal lords venerate storm and sea aspects, mountain lords emphasize earth and metal. The Mandate of Heaven is interpreted locally: each Great Lord claims some measure of divine favor for their own line, diluting the concept from a single imperial mandate into competing claims of heavenly approval.

**Aesthetics and refinement:** Where Tānvür's culture prizes cosmic harmony and bureaucratic order, Jürthāt has developed an intense aesthetic sensibility—an almost spiritual devotion to beauty in craft, poetry, swordsmanship, and the rituals of daily life. The tea ceremony, flower arrangement, garden design, and the forging of blades are all elevated to spiritual practices. This refinement coexists with brutal violence; a lord may compose an exquisite poem in the morning and order a village burned by afternoon.

**Namelessness:** Jürthāt adopted the Tānvür convention of names as earned honors, but the practice has evolved. In Jürthāt, warrior names are granted more freely among the lord and retainer castes—personal names are a mark of service rather than exceptional achievement. The lower castes remain unnamed, as in Tānvür.

## Naval Power

Jürthāt's greatest military advantage is its navy. Island geography forced the development of a maritime tradition that the empire, with its continental focus, has never matched. Jürthātese warships are faster, more maneuverable, and better armed than their Tānvür counterparts, and the island's sailors are among the finest in the eastern seas. The Great Lords maintain their own fleets, and naval raiding—both against the empire and against merchant shipping—is a significant source of wealth and prestige.
