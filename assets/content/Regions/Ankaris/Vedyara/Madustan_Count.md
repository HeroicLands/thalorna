**The Madustan Count** is the [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara Region]]'s own year-count, kept alongside—and never converted from—the BF/AF reckoning the western kingdoms date from the founding of the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian state]]. A Vedyaran date names the year of its age: the present year is the hundred and ninety-sixth of [[lore-agepatrons|the Age of Patrons]], written 196 AP. It is a solar calendar: twelve months of fixed length paired into six seasons, a week of seven days running through them, and the moon kept separately, in a temple almanac, for everything the rites require it for.

## The Epoch

The count begins with [[lore-stndrdmdstn|the Standardization at Madustan]], the standardization of Classical [[skill-vedyarlng|Vedyari]], "roughly 1,200 years before the present," under the philosopher-kings of [[place-madustan|Madustan]], whose capital gave the era its name. That reign produced the great philosophical commentaries, legal codes and epic narratives that are still the prestige texts of the civilization. Vedyaran reckoning takes its first year from that reign, and from no Vylarian, Khelathi or Tantsou event. That year, 1 AK, falls in 480 BF. The count runs on unbroken from it, twelve hundred years to the present, and the scholars divide it into six ages, each of which numbers its own years from 1. A year before the count is numbered backwards from it and written BMC, [Before the Madustan Count]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}: the year before 1 AK is 1 BMC, which is 481 BF.

To carry a date to the [[lore-commoncal|Vylarian Calendar]], add the age's own year to the year the age began, less one, and read the result on the table below; the year 1 AF falls in [[lore-agecopyists|the Age of Copyists]], at 242 AC.

## The Year and Its Months

The count divides the sun's year and not the moon's. Twelve months carry it, seven of thirty days and five of thirty-one, and the twelve together fill the whole of the year. Nothing stands outside a month and nothing is added at either end to close the count.

The year opens at the vernal equinox, on the first day of [Prabhavakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, and each of the world's four fixed days opens a month: the summer solstice opens [Purnakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the autumnal equinox [Danyakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, and the winter solstice [Koshtakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}. The reckoners of the [[affiliation-ganokashalda|Ganoka-shalda]] call this the whole argument for the calendar: a month that began on any other day would wander off the sun within a lifetime, and not one of these does.

1. **Prabhavakala** (30 days), the month of the sources—the glacier-springs open above [[place-sthrnwall|the Southern Wall]], the rivers rise on snowmelt, and the pilgrim roads to the four heads gho up. The year opens on its first day.
2. [Pushpakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (31 days), the flowering—the plateau and the forest country come into flower, and the garland trade that serves the great temples runs at its height.
3. [Jvalakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (30 days), the flame—the hottest of the twelve and the last dry one. The wells drop, the plateau goes hard, and work moves into the early morning.
4. [Meghamukhakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (31 days), the mouth of the clouds—the first weather comes up off the [[place-meghrsmdra|Meghra-samedra]], and the rains break before the month is out.
5. **Purnakala** (30 days), the full—the rivers run bank-full and the fords close. The summer solstice opens it.
6. [Lipikala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (31 days), the writing—with the roads shut, the temple copyists do the year's work, and a house's name for books is made or lost in it.
7. [Naukala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (30 days), the boat—the water clears and falls, the landings and the coast ports open, and the panning grounds in the gold country are worked again.
8. **Danyakala** (31 days), the grain—the harvest, and the month the assemblies meet in because everybody is in one place. The autumnal equinox opens it.
9. [Himrakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (30 days), the snow—snow shuts the high passes and the source-roads, and the pilgrim houses on the upper stages stand empty.
10. **Koshtakala** (30 days), the store—the granaries and the cold-stores are counted, and less moves on the roads than in any other month. The winter solstice opens it.
11. [Karpasakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (30 days), the cotton—the dry-land harvest comes in and the caravan season opens on [[place-marchroad|the march road]].
12. [Sandyakala]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} (31 days), the twilight—the streams drop, the year's accounts are closed, and the count goes out on the day before the equinox.

## The Six Seasons

The months run in pairs, and a pair is a season. Six of them frame the year, and they are what a Vedyaran actually plans by: a contract for a season, a posting for a season, a debt due at a season's turn. Five of the seasons run sixty-one days; only the cold one is shorter, at sixty.

- [Haritaritu]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the green season—Prabhavakala and Pushpakala, sixty-one days. Snowmelt water and flower, and the season a long pilgrimage is begun in.
- [Diptaritu]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the blazing season—Jvalakala and Meghamukhakala, sixty-one days. The heat, broken at the very end of it by the first rain.
- [Varsharetu]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the rain season—Purnakala and Lipikala, sixty-one days. The monsoon, the rivers in flood, the roads shut and the work indoors.
- [Hiranyaritu]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the golden season—Naukala and Danyakala, sixty-one days. The harvest and the gold, which come off the country in the same weeks and are spoken of together.
- [Shitaritu]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the cold season—Himrakala and Koshtakala, sixty days. The shortest season of the year and the quietest.
- [Tamraritu]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the copper season—Karpasakala and Sandyakala, sixty-one days. The land dry and red, the caravans out, and the year closing.

## The Week

Seven days run on through the months without a break, so a weekday belongs to no date and the two are looked up together. The first is the sun's, which the year is sighted from, and the second the moon's, which the almanac tracks. The other five are the five lesser gods of the [[affiliation-vrnkpnthn|Varnuka pantheon]], one apiece. The three great gods hold no day, because the orthodox take the triad for the whole turning and not a part of it, and the two spirit-courts are hosts rather than gods and are given none.

[Surtar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: the sun's day, on which the year is announced from [[place-surtatempl|the Surta temple]].

[Chendravar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: the moon's day, on which the almanac for the coming fortnight is read out.

[Kalavar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: [[lore-kalavratadty|Kalavrata]]'s, on which the dead are burned and their lamps lit.

[Meghavar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: [[lore-meghonatadty|Meghonata]]'s, on which the tanks and channels are worked on.

[Jnanavar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: [[lore-jnanasuradty|Jnanasura]]'s, on which the academies examine and the libraries admit readers.

[Kamavar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: [[lore-kamavratadty|Kamavrata]]'s, on which marriages are made.

[Vahrativar]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}
: [[lore-vahratidty|Vahrati]]'s, on which nothing is begun and no debt is called.

## The Almanac

Vedyara keeps the sun for its civil year and the moon for its rites, and makes no attempt to have one serve the other. The twelve months are solar throughout: fixed lengths on a fixed year, and not one of them is a turn of the moon. The moon is counted separately, and the almanac that every temple of any standing publishes sets the two against each other, day by day, for the year ahead.

Everything in the ritual year hangs off that document. A [Weighing]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} (see [[place-weighingstn|the Weighing-Station]]) at the [[affiliation-suvrgrjnpd|Suvargeri Janpoda]] falls at a new moon; the janpodas of the middle reaches of [[place-chandrmahi|the Chandramahi]] plant by their almanac and not by the water, because the water has fooled them; a [Mahva-Melka]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, a marriage and a coronation are all taken from it. A court with no almanac is a court that cannot say when anything is.

The work is easier here than a foreigner expects it to be. [[place-vaelith|Vaelith]] turns new to new in exactly thirty days, so a lunar day is a day—the two counts never slip against each other, and neither is ever dropped or written twice to bring them level. A fortnight is fifteen days and is fifteen every time. The almanac is therefore not a repair but an arrangement: it states which day of the sun carries which day of the moon, which god's day it falls on, and what is consequently owed on it. Vedyari reckoners are proud of the instrument, and an almanac a reader can find an error in is a house's embarrassment for a generation.

## The Ages of the Count

A Vedyari scholar names a year by the age it falls in, the way a chronicle names it by the reign. The ages are the scholars' own divisions, taught in every temple school, and each takes its name from what Vedyara did in it.

**Before the count.** Everything older than the standardization is written BMC. The scholars tell two ages in it without numbering either: [[lore-unnumbrdage|the Unnumbered Age]], everything the janpodas say of themselves and nothing anyone can date, and [[lore-foundingage|the Founding Age]], from the highland temples of about 300 BMC (780 BF) to the eve of the count, when the [[lore-mhndkngdm|Mahanodi kings]] held the upper river from a seat the library never recorded.

**[[lore-agekingdoms|The Age of Kingdoms]]** opens with the count itself. [[place-rajepur|Rajepur]] is raised, the kingdoms grow along the rivers, and the six last kings of Mahanodi end the age in a famine.

**The Age of Copyists** opens in the year of [[lore-fortydays|the Forty Days]], 1 AC, when the kingdom of Mahanodi was dissolved. Four quiet centuries of libraries and endowments follow, and the Vyalendri crown rises and falls inside them.

**[[lore-hundredcrwn|The Hundred Crowns]]** opens with the end of the Vyalendri kingship, about 150 AF, and its small kingdoms fight each other until [[place-chendrapur2|Chendrapur]]'s temples refuse a king in about 163 HC.

**[[lore-hardgenrtn|The Hard Generation]]** opens in 1 HG and runs twenty-two years: the steppe host, the conch-door adventurer and [[lore-thirdcmpct|the Third Compact]], sworn at the Melka of 8 HG.

**[[lore-compactpeace|The Compact Peace]]** opens in 1 CP with the march forts standing and runs to the year before Rajepur went under [[affiliation-chendrapur|Chendrapur]]'s protection.

**The Age of Patrons** opens in 1 AP with that protection, and runs to the present.

## Who Keeps It

The [[affiliation-triyangasampradeya|Triyanga-sampradeya]], the largest and most orthodox of the Varnukan sects, sets the public year. Its high priests sight it at the [[place-surtatempl|Surta temple]], the pilgrim shrine at [[place-chandrprbh|Chandraprabhava]] where the Chandramahi comes out of the ice. The sighting is announced from there to every court and temple that keeps the Madustan count.

The reckoners of the Ganoka-shalda compute the same year instead of sighting it, and the two methods do not always agree. A single day between the sighted year and the computed one is enough to move the date of a Melka, a Weighing or a coronation. Neither method is dropped for the other. A court states which one it follows.

## Elsewhere

Vedyara's neighbors keep their own counts and not the Madustan one. [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Au'Khelathu]] dates from [[lore-qettelgu|the Qet Telgu]] and the [[affiliation-tantsouempr|Empire of Tantsou]] from the **[[lore-clndrstrlgy|Celestial Calendar]]**'s [[lore-greatcnvrg|Great Convergence]]. None of the three converts to either of the others in ordinary use. A trader or an envoy crossing between them carries both dates and translates neither.
