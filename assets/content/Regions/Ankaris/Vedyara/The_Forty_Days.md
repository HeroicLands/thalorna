**The Forty Days** is the dissolution of the Kingdom of Mahānadi as the [[place-rajavalilib|Rājavalī Library]] keeps it: the chronicle's entries, flat and dated by the [[lore-mdhvndrcnt|Mādhavendra count]], each followed by the gloss that [[affiliation-rajaprjnpd|Rājapur]]'s current Memory-Keeper, Rāmavāhana Smrti-Bhāra, gives it in his commentary on the dissolution. The year of the entries, M 240, is −240 AF. The originals lie in [[place-shitakoshtha|Shitakoshtha]], the library's oldest vault. The chronicle enters what was done; the commentary weighs it, and it keeps apart what the record states and what tradition adds.

: The Forty Days {#chronicle}

::: {.chronicle}
**Entry.** In the year two hundred and forty of the count, in the famine, the king died in the palace. He left no heir of his body. Before his death the household guard had opened the royal granaries and taken from them.

_Gloss._ The chronicle enters the death and not its manner. That the king was poisoned, and by his own cook, who could no longer endure the suffering of his neighbors in the villages, is tradition. The recitation carries it and says it is tradition, and this commentary does the same. What the entry does state is enough to judge by: a famine in the villages, a full granary in the palace, and a guard that took from it while the king looked on. The six who end the line stand one by one in the chronicle before this entry.

**Entry.** The people of the villages came to the temple of Vyālendra, which stood beside the palace, and asked the senior priest to convene a sabhā. They asked for no king.

_Gloss._ The last four words are the whole of the matter, and the chronicler wrote them without comment. The temple had stood beside the palace for the length of the line, and every king of the line had been consecrated at its altar. The people went to the house that had made the kings, and asked it for something else.

**Entry.** The sabhā was convened in the great hall of the temple, before the altar, and sat.

**Entry.** On the fortieth day the sabhā declared the kingdom dissolved and the line of its kings ended, with full honors to the line and acknowledgment of what it did in its better generations. The villages of the kingdom are joined as one janapada and governed jointly through the temple. The palace is to be taken down and its stones given to the temple. The royal granaries are common stores. The army is disbanded, and each of its men is given land. The council-chamber stands, and what is kept in it is kept whole.

_Gloss._ It is fair to ask why a sabhā took forty days to end what nobody in the hall wished to keep. The answer the decree gives is in its clauses. The sabhā was not deciding whether the kingdom was finished; it was deciding what to keep of it and what to unmake, and every clause is one such decision.

The palace was unmade, and the classical account is that its stones went into the temple so that the kingdom's work would go on holding something up. Its cellars lie open at the north end of the town, and nobody builds there.

The granaries were not unmade. They were taken out of the palace's hands and put where every village could see them, and the four-story granary in the temple court is a common store to this day. A drunk king's guard opened the royal doors; twelve men of the town watch lock the common ones at night.

The army was unmade, and its men were not. They took land and went into the cultivating and artisan households of the new janapada, and Rājapur has raised no soldiers since and has needed none.

The council-chamber was kept, and with it the record. This is the clause the commentary returns to most. The dissolution was a judgment, and a judgment is only as good as the record behind it; a sabhā that burned the chronicle of the six would have left its own decree with nothing to answer for it. The family that keeps the record has kept it since this decree.

The honor in the decree is not courtesy. The recitation names the just kings of the line at their places before it reaches the six, and it closes on the acknowledgment that the kingdom is no more. A kingdom that failed because it deserved to fail is the reason for the recitation; that any government can fail and be dissolved the same way, this janapada's own included, is the reason it opens every session.
