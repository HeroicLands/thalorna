**Sandhyāgriha** stands in the palace quarter of [[place-vyalendra3|Vyālendra]], four streets from the hall where the [[affiliation-vyalendra2|Loom-Council]] sits. It is the principal house of [[affiliation-twilighths|the Twilight House]], and satellite houses stand under it in each of the major Vedyaran cities. What a client buys here is an evening's company, a room his rivals also enter, and a room his servants do not follow him into.

The building is four courts deep and was laid out as a royal pleasure-house by the eleventh and last Mahārāja of Vyālendra. It passed to the House when his line ended, by a grant the House holds and shows to nobody. The Council has never tested the grant. The reason given is that the deed is sound; the reason understood is that testing it would require an accounting of how the line ended, and no Loom-Master wants that accounting read aloud in the cloth-court.

## The Two Doors

The street door is the one clients use and is watched by everyone in the quarter, which is the intention: the House sells discretion about what is said inside and none whatever about who came in. The garden door at the back of the fourth court opens onto a lane behind the treasury, is not overlooked, and is used by the handlers, by the two or three clients a Mistress decides are worth more unseen, and by anyone the House is moving out of the city.

## The Courts

The first court is public and holds the music-rooms. The second holds the private rooms, and the doors of it are thick, close-fitted, and hung double for that reason. The third is the school, where the girls learn the poetry in three court dialects, the four instruments, the reckoning and the reading of a room; no client crosses into it, and the rule has been broken twice in living memory and answered both times. The fourth is the Mistress's own, with the ledger-room beside it, and nobody enters it uninvited, the Companions included.

## The Ledger-Room

What is overheard in the second court is written up in the fourth, the same night, by the handler the Companion is assigned to. The room holds the transcripts, the client-list, and the tiers at which each is sold. It has no window and one door, and the House keeps a second copy of everything in it somewhere else in the city, which every Loom-Master assumes and none has located.

The Mistress of the Long Evening is dying, and the succession is being contested between three candidates who each hold a different part of what the House knows about the last king. The part that matters is not in the ledger-room. It has never been written down at all.
