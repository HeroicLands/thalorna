**The Five Grounds** is the lament the march companies of [[affiliation-bhumipal|Bhumipal]] sing in winter inside the wall of [[place-ludrapur|Ludrapur]], the burned capital of the kingdom that held [[place-marchroad|the march road]] before them. It is five short poems, each named for one piece of ground on the road and each opening on that ground: the spit at [[place-sandyapur|Sandyapur]], behind which the Bhumipal's boats stood off from [[lore-hndrdbnnrs|the Storm of the Hundred Banners]] in the spring of 315 AF; the fan of stones at [[place-lantustal|Lantustal]], the banner ground, where [Ashvakirti]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, last king of [Marupal]{.name data-type="affiliation/polity"}, met the host with everything the march had and died with it ([[lore-marupalfl|the Fall of Marupal]]); the bend in the road that the companies chain at dusk; the wall of Ludrapur the companies winter behind; and the temple inside it, which has stood without a god since [Devopala]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a priest of the Bhumipal court, carried its image down to Sandyapur a generation after the burning.

The poems are outer poems in the classical division of Vedyari verse, the poems of war, kings and death, with the ground named first and the feeling carried in the ground. The companies call the sequence a _solma_ (a song) and never a _birnu_ (a mourning), because a mourning is owed by a household and the dead of the march had none near enough to owe it.

Time moves along the road with the grounds: the night the boats stood off, the morning on the field, dusk at the chain, a winter night inside the wall, and the last night before the companies ride out in spring. A company puts up one man for each poem, in the court where the companies settle their quarrels, and every man present takes the close: two lines that return at the end of each poem changed by one word, and the verb of the close is the keeping that both kingdoms are named for, the desert-keeper and the earth-keeper.

The rendering keeps the short line, the catalogue and the returning close, and it keeps the king's order in the shape the companies give it, which is the shape of an order and not of a speech.

The singers are hired men, Vedyari, Dunhari and both, who never saw the kingdom they sing, and the last poem says so. Nothing in the song is record. Nobody came home from Lantustal to carry the king's order, and the song says as much and gives the order anyway; the names the third poem recites are the names the captains tell new men, and each company's captain tells his own three. The field is named nowhere in the song: the companies say its name by daylight, the song is sung after dark, and so the second poem is the fan of stones and nothing else, though its name holds the march's word for a banner, _lantu_. The Bhumipal court's account of the spring of 315 agrees with the first poem in every fact and differs from it in the weight: the court says the clan was the one fighting family of the march left whole, and the song says it was in a boat. The court has never forbidden the song. Its title to the march rests on Marupal having left no heir, and there is no heir in the song. What the record keeps open stays open in it: how old Marupal's crown was, where its treasury went, how many of the steppe's dead lie on the field, and whether the god of Ludrapur went down the road in Devopala's cart or stayed in the empty house, which is the question the companies settle their quarrels under and the one the song puts and does not answer. The host is "the hundred banners" throughout and its leader is never named, as in every Vedyari account. The travelers from [[place-khzryndsrtrgn|the steppe]] who leave offerings at the field's stones for their own dead stand in the third poem as the companies see them, from the road. The colors in the fourth poem are sung to whatever colors hold the gates that winter, and the line about the company with no gate is sung loudest by that company.

: The Spit {#spit}

::: {.verse form=lament lang=en work=spit}
At Sandyapur the spit is a mile of sand./
Everything on it can be moved by wind./
That night the boats lay behind it.

A household in the boats: the old salt master,/
his sons and daughters, the pan workers, the grooms,/
a hundred horses with their heads lashed low to the thwarts./
A horse finds poor footing in a boat./
Hooves cut the planks all night./
Nobody hit a horse that night.

North across the flats was a light,/
neither dusk nor dawn./
It had followed the road for three nights, one well each night./
Tonight it was the sheds among the pans.

The son spoke, a hand on a horse's neck,/
as a son speaks before the household./
We are Sentija. We have a hundred horses./
The king carried his banners up the road./
We are in a boat.

His father answered as a father answers./
The king sent word to the wells. The wells sent word to the road./
The road takes five days./
He was on the stones when his word reached the pans./
The word did not know./
When the king calls, a Samonta owes him his hundred./
The call arrived after the king./
We owe a hundred horses to the dead on the stones./
We will pay along the road, by daylight, with a fort behind us./
We will not pay tonight in the water.

He put the household under oath. His sons repeated it,/
then the grooms and pan workers. The horses stood through it./
If this household takes the march,/
a fort at every water. Remember this light when laying the footing./
If it holds its court behind the spit,/
let it hold it as a man holds a debt./
Pay the road first.

On the fourth day the light moved west./
They brought the horses ashore across the beach./
The horses would not stand on sand./
Then they stood.

The road is kept./
The spit keeps it.
:::

: The Fan of Stones {#stones}

::: {.verse form=lament lang=en work=stones}
Where the hills open onto the road, the ground is stone:/
a fan spread as a river spreads its stones./
The river is gone. Nothing grows there./
The companies have a name for it in daylight./
This song is sung at night.

Ashvakirti brought his host up from the walled town,/
every horse, every man, every banner the march possessed./
He stationed them among the stones.

The hundred banners came down from the hills./
Nobody counted. Who counts rain?

He issued one order. Nobody returned to carry it./
The stones carry it. This is how they give it./
Stand on the stones./
Behind you lies the plain. The plain is the kingdom:/
the wells, the pans, the walled town, the salt road./
Beyond this ground there is no ground.

The line gave way./
It went as a riverbank goes in the rains:/
in one movement, from behind./
Banners fell among the stones./
Horses passed over banners./
The king fell beneath his horse./
Nobody in the host went home.

No conch for [[affiliation-kalavrata|Kalavrata]]. The households were five days south./
No lamp. Their houses were burning./
No fire for [[affiliation-rasikara|Rasikara]]. Nobody remained to build it./
The hundred banners lay out their dead and speak their names to the wind./
Nobody laid these out. Nobody spoke their names./
Two hosts passed through one door together. Nobody closed it./
They are still inside.

In ten winters the cloth became threads./
The name remained./
If you must cross, cross at noon./
When fear rises from the stones,/
the hills at your back crowded with horsemen,/
the line gone and nowhere to run,/
know whose fear it is. It is not yours./
It belongs to the host. Carry it across the field,/
as the stones have carried it for four hundred years./
Put it down at the boundary. Keep going.

The road is kept./
The stones keep it.

: The Road {#road}

::: {.verse form=lament lang=en work=road}
The road is beaten ground, one wagon wide,/
from salt to sand./
For half a day it leaves its old course./
It turns south around the stones, costing half a day./
Nobody has asked to have that half day back.

A post at either end of the bend:/
a well, a fire, four men, a chain./
At sunset they stretch the chain across the road./
A horse could step over it. The chain is not for horses./
It makes a man who means to travel after dark/
touch the cold iron and raise it himself.

Whitewashed stones mark the field's boundary./
The companies whiten them after the rains./
An animal that crosses belongs to the field./
A herder who watches it cross lets it go.

The captain tells the names to the new men,/
in the proper telling: where each man was found.

at first dark, rope in hand./

rope in hand, with no horse tied to it.

They found him a hundred paces in,/

: The Wall {#wall}

::: {.verse form=lament lang=en work=wall}
Ludrapur's wall is mud brick on stone foundations,/
one mile around, with four gates./
It turns the wind, the work a wall is built for./
It does that with nobody inside.

Inside, streets have become long, low ridges./
The grass lets you read the houses:/
a door here, a hearth here, a courtyard wall./
The brick fell on them together./
The inner face of the circuit is burned black/

The townspeople died in their streets./
The town burned above them./
A town burning above its dead has burned its dead./
