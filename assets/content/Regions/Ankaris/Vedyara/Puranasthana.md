**Purānasthāna**, the old seat, is a terraced hill above the upper [[place-mahanadi|Mahānadī]] on the pilgrim road to [[place-mahaprbhva|Mahāprabhava]]. The villages of the upper river call it the ancestor's seat and graze goats on it. Their legend is that the first kings of [[lore-mhndkngdm|Mahānadī]] sat here before they moved to Rājapur in 480 BF, and were hallowed on an altar that stood on the top terrace.

The [[place-rajavalilib|Rājavalī Library]] does not record it. The kingdom's chronicle begins its count at Rājapur, and where the kings kept their seat before then is a question the library leaves open. Purānasthāna is legend and not record, and Rājapur's scholars say so when they are asked.

## What Is There

Five terraces climb the hill's southern face, faced in dry stone, now planted with nothing but grass and a few old trees. The top terrace carries a ring of dressed stone, square-cut and fitted, about forty paces across, with a gap on the river side that may have been a gate. In the middle of the ring the ground sinks into a shallow hollow, rectangular and stone-edged, that the villages call the altar's place. Nothing stands in it.

Pilgrims climbing to the source stop on the top terrace for the view, which runs a day down the valley. The goats keep the grass short.

## Who Comes

- **Pilgrims** on the road to Mahāprabhava.
- **Correspondents of [[being-anrjhrdvmbjkr|Anurāja Harshadevāmbujakar]]** and other scholars of the kingdom, who want the stone the first kings were hallowed on and have not found it.
- **The upper-river villages**, whose goats graze it and whose elders tell the legend.

## Hooks

- **The buyer.** The hill has been bought, quietly, through two intermediaries, by a family of the upper river. The Memory-Keeper's recitation names in each generation the collateral heir of the Mahānadī line, the one who would have reigned, and that heir's family is alive and holds land upriver. The kingdoms that call Anurāja a radical would find a living Mahānadī heir more useful than a dead precedent, and so would Anurāja.
- **The stone face-down.** A pilgrim party sheltering on the terraces from a storm finds a cut altar-stone lying face-down under the turf of the second terrace, older in its working than the sandstone altar at Rājapur. Turning it over is a day's work for ten men, and the village does not want it turned.
