Among the [[lore-nordheimnclt|Nordmen]] of [[place-targud|Targud]], this is the saga [[being-knlthnskrldmkh|Knilthann Skraldumakh]] tells the [[lore-bondirnk|bóndi]] standing their weeks on the wall at [[place-tvalgard|Tvalgard]], over the winter nights of the rotation, so that a new shift knows what the fort-duty asks of it. It is a _stórald_ in the north's own sense: a history of two households told in plain prose, with stanzas quoted where the prose needs a witness. It belongs to the Tvalgard repertoire rather than to either household, and the two names it goes by mark who is listening—the hall calls it by the tower, and the bóndi call it by the stone. Of its seven stanzas, two belong to the stone-mover, two to his neighbor's son, and three are the fort's own, made by the garrison skalds of the time and carried under the truth of the recital since. One line of the gate-stanza is gone; the [[affiliation-skaldscrcl|Skaldating]] would not settle it between two readings, so the fort recites the stanza with the line left out. The saga does not name the district, as a fort-saga does not, and it does not say which winter.

## The Gnurthendikh and the Dvirnendikh

There was a man called **Gnurthorv**. He was of the **Gnurthendikh**, who hold the land under the ridge where the river comes out of the marsh. They have held it since before the ground to the east of them was burned, when the farms ran a day further down the river than they do now, and three howes stand on it: the name-giver's, his son's, and Gnurthorv's father's, who came home from a fort on the burned ground with one arm and farmed with the other for thirty years. The Gnurthendikh can name all three at the ting, and the ground is odal by them. Gnurthorv was a big man and a close one. He paid his dues when they were asked for and stood his weeks at the forts when his turn came. He lent nothing. He had once sat a whole day at the ting over a dog, and won, and people remembered the dog longer than they remembered what the dog had done. He was not thought to be much of a neighbor. His wife was dead of a fever in the dark months, some years before, and he had not married again. He had one son, **Gnarthann**, who was grown but held no land of his own, and did the work of the holding with his father, and was better liked than his father was.

Below the Gnurthendikh land, on the river side, lies the land of the **Dvirnendikh**. It is the better hay, because the river floods it in the thaw and the ridge land gets what runs off. **Dvirnorv** had held it. He went through the ice at the landing in the last of one winter and was got out, and died of it in the spring, and he was three winters in his howe at the time of this saga; the howe stands at the landing, in sight of the house. His widow **Dvirnselda** kept the house and the keys, and their son **Dvurnthann** held the land. Dvurnthann was then twenty winters old. He had stood one turn at the forts, at the quiet one in the south of the district where nothing had come up the river in living memory, and come home from it, and people said he was a quiet man and would be a good one. Dvirnselda was known for a well-kept holding and for saying what she thought. Nobody in the district had got the better of her in a bargain for sheep, and the trader who came up from Tvalgard for the marten skins each spring went to her house last, when he had already made his profit elsewhere.

The line between the two holdings runs from the river to the ridge, and five stones mark it. The first stands at the landing and the fifth at the foot of the ridge, and the second and third have an alder and an outcrop to fix them by. The fourth stands in the open, in the hay, where there is nothing to fix it by except the stones on either side. That is the stone this saga is about.

## The Walk

At [[lore-sumarmal|Sumarmál]] the householders of the frontier walk their marks together, with a witness for the landvördr, and read each mark aloud and strike it, so that both households can say afterward where the line lay. Gnurthorv and Dvurnthann walked theirs the morning after the ships went into the river, as they had walked them the year before, Dvurnthann for his house and Gnurthorv for his. The first stone was where it had been, and the second and the third. At the fourth the witness stopped. He looked at the third stone and at the fifth and then at the fourth again, and said nothing. Then Dvurnthann said that the stone stood a pace into his hay.

Gnurthorv said that the frost had moved it.

The witness said that frost moved stones, that was true. He said he had not before seen frost move one toward the better ground. Then he read the mark and struck the stone where it stood, because that is what a witness does, and said that it was a matter for the ting.

A stone found moved is a matter for the ting and for nothing less. Two households do not settle it at a gate, because the witness has struck the stone and the striking is in his memory, and a stone struck in one place and found in another is a claim the landvördr's man carries to the Lawspeaker. So it went to the Sumarmál ting, which met that year at the [[lore-hersvaldrnk|hersvald]]'s hall, with the Lawspeaker there on his circuit from Tvalgard.

At the ting Dvurnthann brought the claim. The witness said what he had seen, and where he had struck the stone the year before, and where he had struck it this year. The Lawspeaker asked Gnurthorv for his answer. Gnurthorv stood up and spoke this:

: Gnurthorv's answer at the ting {#ting}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Frost works the field's edge;
the giant's knuckle shifts.
Who set it saw no sun
set on it a second time.
Ask the thaw, not the man,
how the mark went walking.
I keep what Hrandmýl kept:
a thing set down stays down.
```

The verse was taken more than one way. Hrandmýl is the first man of [[lore-gnumborinn|Gnúmborinn]]; what Gnurthorv meant by keeping what Hrandmýl kept, nobody at the ting asked him. The witness said afterward that he had heard a man deny a thing and own it in the same breath, and that he did not know which half he would be asked to swear to. The Lawspeaker said that a stone was not a thing the ting could look at from where it sat. He set the claim over to the next Sumarmál, when the walk would be made again and the witness could say whether the thaw had moved the mark back. Until then the fourth stone was to stand where it stood and nobody was to lay a hand on it. Both men said they were content with that. Dvirnselda had come to the ting; she said nothing there, and a good deal on the way home.

That summer the two households cut their hay to the stone, each on its own side, and the pace of hay between the old line and the new went to the Gnurthendikh, and nobody from the Dvirnendikh went down to look at it.

The district talked about it through the summer. Most people thought the frost had not moved the stone. Some thought Gnurthorv had moved it, and some thought Gnarthann had, to please his father, and a few thought Dvirnselda had moved it herself so as to have a claim, which shows how little those few knew her. Gnurthorv said nothing further about it to anyone, and Dvurnthann said nothing at all.

## The Rotation

Every bóndi in Targud stands his weeks at the forts in his turn, and the turns go round the districts through the year, so that one district's turn falls in the sailing weeks and another's in the dark months. That year the district's turn fell at midwinter. The hersvald made the count at [[lore-vetrnaetr|Vetrnaetr]]. Gnurthorv's name was in it and Dvurnthann's, and both were sent to **Draskborg**.

Draskborg is a beacon-tower on a rise above the river, on the burned ground, a day and a half east of Tvalgard by the sledge road and two days back against the weather. It was raised to watch the river and not to hold it. There is a palisade around the rise, one gate on the Tvalgard side, the tower with the beacon laid on top of it, and a hall under the tower that sleeps two shifts. The whole of its duty is to see what comes up the river, to light the _dresk_ so that Tvalgard sees it, and to hold the gate until Tvalgard comes. It is cold.

The district's men went out to it together, twenty of them on the sledge road, each with his own shield and spear and a sack of meal, and the district's sledge behind with the salt meat and the tallow for the beacon. Gnurthorv carried the shield his father had carried, with the Gnurthendikh mark burned into the boards. Dvurnthann carried his father's spear, because it was the better one and the house had no other that was any good. The two did not travel together on the road. The men noticed that, and said so to each other, and said nothing to either of them.

**Nalthvir** had the keeping of Draskborg that winter. He was a [[lore-hringberirnk|hirdman]] of the king's. He had stood the tower four winters, which is more than anyone wants to, and the bóndi who came to him were his to place. He had the two men's names from the hersvald, and he had the story of the stone, because everyone had it. He set the watches, and he put Gnurthorv and Dvurnthann on the same one.

Some of the men asked him why. Nalthvir said that there were forty men on the rise and that he had to put each of them next to somebody.

The men had their own view of it, and by the end of the first week a bet was running in the hall on which of the two would speak first, and what about. Nalthvir let it run. He said that a bet kept men awake.

The two stood the night watch together from the first night. They did not speak. Gnurthorv took the river side of the walk and Dvurnthann the ridge side, and when the wind changed they changed sides, without a word about it. When the one went down to the hall the other came up. At the table they sat where the count put them, which was together, and passed the dish. In the days the men cut wood on the burned ground for the hall and the beacon, in threes, because Nalthvir sent nobody out alone; in the nights there were two on the walk and one at the gate and one in the tower, and the rest asleep under the tower with the wind coming through the planks.

In the third week of the duty [[lore-jol|Jól]] came in. The hall kept the lamp in the gate and the sheaf on the tower, and the men spoke their dead at the table in turn. Gnurthorv gave his wife's name. Dvurnthann gave his father's. Gnurthorv had stood at Dvirnorv's grave-ale, as a neighbor stands; he did not look up from the dish.

## The Lights on the Ice

Before Jól was out the marsh froze hard, and after it the river. When the river freezes the [[lore-grukarfolk|Grukar]] have a road, and the road comes up past Draskborg, which is why the turn in the dark months is the one no district wants.

On the tenth night of Jól the wind dropped. Dvurnthann had the river side. Some time after the middle of the night he called Gnurthorv over, and the two of them stood and looked down the river together, which they had not done before. There were lights on the ice, far down, where the river bends out of the marsh. There were a great many of them. They were moving, and they were coming up.

The man at the gate said that it was the Ride, and that nobody was to answer it. Nalthvir came up the tower. He looked for as long as it took to count, and said that the Ride carried no lanterns, and had the beacon laid.

Dvurnthann spoke this, standing on the walk; it is the first verse of his that anyone kept:

: Dvurnthann on the walk {#lights}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Hard on the giant's rime,
where the bend lets the marsh out,
lamps go walking upriver,
no lamp-bearer under them.
Count them, keeper of the last hour,
count them a second time;
the tusk-host carries fire
to a hall that has its own.
```

Nalthvir lit the _dresk_ himself. The fort's verse on the lighting is this one; the fort had it before the rest of the saga was made, and has only the half of it:

: The beacon {#beacon}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Up went the Destroyer's tongue
on Draskborg's tower-top;
down on Hrímthur's rime-road
the tusk-host's torches crawled.
```

Then Nalthvir counted the men into the gate-rank. He put the ones who had stood a fight before in the front and the ones who had not behind them, and the count ran down the line as the watches had been set, so that Dvurnthann stood on Gnurthorv's left, under the edge of his shield, because that is where the count put him.

## The Gate

The Grukar came up out of the river-bend a little before dawn and sat down on the ice below the rise, out of bowshot, and waited for the light. There were more of them than there were men on the rise, and fewer than there had been lights, because many of the lights had been carried two to a hand to look like more. When the day came the men on the wall could see what they were: the small ones in a mass, the tall ones walking behind them, and a few thin ones that stood off on the ice and watched the tower and did not come up. Nobody on the wall tried to speak to them. There is nothing to say to them and nothing they will say back, and anyone they carry off they eat, and every man on the rise knew it.

They came up the rise at the gate when the sun was on the tower, in a crowd, with the tall ones driving the small ones from behind, and they came again at midday, and again at dusk. Between the goings-up they sat on the ice, and the thin ones walked up and down behind them. The rank held the gate all three times. In the first going-up Gnurthorv took a spear under the rim of his shield, in the side above the hip, from one of the small ones that got down low. He killed the one that gave it to him. He did not go back from the gate, and he did not say he was hurt. At midday Dvurnthann saw the blood on the gatepost and said so. Gnurthorv said it was theirs. The bet in the hall was paid that night, to the man who had said it would be Dvurnthann, and that it would be about blood.

That night he was given the bench nearest the fire and could not be made to lie on it. He sat up against the gatepost with his shield across his knees and spoke this, which is the verse the Circle holds for his last, though he lived two days after it:

: Gnurthorv at the gatepost {#gatepost}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Cold is the frost-line's work
along a man's left side;
I would say so of a stone,
and say less of myself.
The small ones throw badly.
The big ones stand back and shout.
What chills me is the post
I lean on. It wants moving.
```

The men took that as it was meant and laughed, and Gnurthorv laughed with them, carefully.

The second day went as the first. The fort's verse on the gate is this one, with the line the Skaldating would not settle left out of it:

: The gate {#gate}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Three times up the rise
the river's guests came calling;
three times the rim-row
gave them the gate to look at.
[…]
a shield-edge over a boy,
marsh-ash under the man's rim,
the gate's left post gone red.
```

On the third day the relief came over the ridge from Tvalgard, sixty men on skis with sledges behind them and the hersvald at the head, and the thin ones on the ice saw them before the men on the rise did. The Grukar went back down the river the way they had come. The fort's verse on the relief is this:

: The relief {#relief}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Over the ridge-snow, sunward,
the ski-host's spear-hedge showed;
the thin ones, the marsh-thinkers,
saw it first, and went.
The gate was still a gate.
The Destroyer's tongue was ash.
Of forty men, nine
were carried, and one of them talked.
```

## The Road Home

Gnurthorv was put on a sledge for Tvalgard with the other hurt men. He talked for most of the first day, mostly about the cold, and complained of the driving; the drivers said afterward that he had told them the road was worse than the Grukar, and that he had been right about the road. In the night he stopped talking, and he died before the sledges came in sight of the Tvalgard wall, on the second day. Dvurnthann walked beside the sledge the whole way. He had Gnurthorv's shield on his back; it had been handed to him when the sledges were loaded, because Gnurthorv could not hold it and the drivers had no room for it, and nobody asked him for it back. When the hersvald asked him at Tvalgard whether he would stay out his weeks or go home with the body, he said that he would go home, and the hersvald let him. The hersvald sent a rider ahead by the district road, and the Gnurthendikh began to brew the day the word came.

The body went home by the district road, and Dvurnthann drove it. The Gnurthendikh opened their wall for it. They lifted the planks out of the east side, nearest the ridge, and carried Gnurthorv in through the gap and out again through it, and closed it behind him. He had died of a wound taken in the gate-rank, so his was an honored death, and he was owed a warrior's honors on the pyre. There was a difficulty, because it was still the dark months and a pyre burns in daylight. The Gnurthendikh waited two days for a day with a noon in it and burned him then, with his spear and the knife he had used at the gate, and with the shield, which Dvurnthann had set against the Gnurthendikh door the night he brought the body and had said nothing about. Gnarthann gathered the ash into the _gnúm_, and the howe was marked out on the ridge side, to be raised when the ground gave.

Dvurnthann went home to his mother's house. What passed between them there is not told.

## The Grave-Ale

On the seventh night the Gnurthendikh held the _hrúmsminni_, and the neighbors came to it. Dvurnthann came. Dvirnselda did not; some tell it that she came as far as the door and stood there through the praise and went home before the cup, and the Tvalgard telling has her at home. The household had no skald and had not sent to Tvalgard for one, so the eldest of the kin gave the praise, and that was Gnurthorv's father's sister, who had seen two of the three howes raised and expected to see the fourth. No _hnúra_ was made.

The praise was short. She said that Gnurthorv had stood his weeks every time his turn came and had never once been late to them. She said that at Draskborg he had taken a spear on the first morning and kept his place at the gate for three days, and that was how he had met what came, and the praise of a dead man turns on that and on nothing else. She said that he had been a hard man to borrow from and a harder one to buy from, and that those are a way of meeting what comes too, and the house laughed. She did not speak of the stone. Nobody looked for her to; the stone belonged to the reckoning, and the reckoning came next.

Then the reckoning was spoken, what the dead man had owed and what was owed to him and who now answered for each. It was not a long list. A share in a boat at the landing, owed to him by the men who rowed it, and now to Gnarthann. Two sheep's worth of hall credit, owed by him at the hersvald's table since the Vetrnaetr feast, and now by Gnarthann. Some iron, borrowed, which the borrower stood up and named. A bundle of marten skins promised to the Tvalgard trader against meal already eaten, which Gnarthann said he would deliver at the thaw. The dog, which was dead, and was named anyway, because the Gnurthendikh name everything. Then the eldest said: "There is the fourth stone."

She said it plainly, as it has to be said. There was a claim against the dead man at the ting, that he had moved the fourth stone a pace into the Dvirnendikh hay. It stood over to the next Sumarmál. She said that a claim does not die with the man it is brought against, that his kin answer it, and that Gnarthann would answer it as heir if it was brought; and that a dead man whose kin will not answer for a stone is said to come back carrying it, asking where it goes. She said that she did not propose to see her brother's son walking the ridge with a stone in his arms, and that the Gnurthendikh would answer. Then she asked whether the claimant was in the hall.

Dvurnthann stood up. He was at the far end of the bench, where a neighbor sits.

He said: "He kept his place."

Then he sat down.

The eldest waited. When nothing more came, she asked him, in the words a reckoning uses, whether he brought the claim. He said that he did not. She asked whether he brought it against the heir. He said that he did not. She said that the stone stood where it stood, then, and that the reckoning was closed on it, and said it three times, so that the house had heard it. The reckoning was closed.

Then Gnarthann took the cup and drank it standing at the foot of the high seat, and swore before the guests the oath he meant to keep as head of the house, and stepped up into the _höfudsveld_, and the hall had an heir.

## The Stone

Dvurnthann went home that night. It is told that Dvirnselda did not speak to her son that winter, and that the house was kept as well as it had ever been kept. The meals were on the table and the stores held to the thaw. In the spring the howe went up on the Gnurthendikh ridge, and the Dvirnendikh sent a man to help carry turf, which is what a neighbor sends. Dvurnthann was the man. He carried turf for two days and ate at the Gnurthendikh table and went home, and Gnarthann walked him as far as the fourth stone and no further.

At Sumarmál the walk was made again. The witness for the landvördr was the same man. Gnarthann walked for the Gnurthendikh, since the land was his now and this was his first walk, and Dvurnthann walked for the Dvirnendikh. The first stone was where it had been, and the second and the third. At the fourth the witness stopped, and looked at the third stone and at the fifth, and looked at Dvurnthann, and asked him where the mark was. Dvurnthann said this:

: Dvurnthann at the fourth stone {#stone}

```poetry {form=skaldic lang=en}
Where the thaw has left it
the giant's knuckle stands,
hay on either hand of it,
and whose, the stone will say.
Read it, witness. Strike it.
A shield stood at a door;
turf went up a ridge.
A thing set down stays down.
```

The witness read the mark and struck the stone where it stood, and Gnarthann struck it, and Dvurnthann struck it. It stands there now. The line between the Gnurthendikh and the Dvirnendikh runs through it, and at every walk since it has been read as the fourth stone and struck where it stands, and no claim has been brought on it at any ting.

Dvirnselda kept the house eleven winters more. She was burned with her keys and her spindle, and her howe stands beside Dvirnorv's, on the river side of the fourth stone. Here ends the saga of Draskborg.
