## Overview

The finest shipyards in the north stand in Gnarthborg's harbor, and the clans that work them do not give up a hull's lines, a keel's proportions or a trade secret for any price a stranger has yet offered. The name is a jaw that will not open, and the kingdom built on it has a reputation to match. Twelve hundred people live within the fortified town, second only to [[place-knalthstead|Knalthstead]] among the settlements of the [[place-nrdlndsrgn|Nordlands]], and [[being-knghlrthni|King Hlurthann III]] holds his hall here, acclaimed at the ting like every king before him and answerable to the same assembly for every arm-ring he is handed back.

[[place-malagna|Malagna]]'s western footing shows plainly on Gnarthborg's wharves. [[place-elavendre|Élavendrian]] and [[place-aelwyth|Aelwythan]] traders keep standing arrangements with its merchant households that a more insular eastern kingdom would never tolerate, and the town's hersvaldar and harbor-reeves spend as much of their working lives brokering a toll dispute with a foreign factor as they do mustering men for the king. [[being-snrvthnvtrdmr|Snurvthann Vetrdómr]] thatches the halls that house this traffic, ordinary work that a town this size needs in quantity no smaller settlement does.

The jaw that will not open cuts both ways. Gnarthborg's shipwrights guard their craft from outsiders, and the same closed hand has made the town cautious of the king himself: a crown that leans too hard on one clan's yards risks a strike it has no way to compel, since a shipwright's skill is the one thing in the kingdom a hersvald's levy cannot muster by force. A northern king is not required to rule Gnarthborg the way he rules the rest of Malagna, and Hlurthann III does not try.

The killing that began the [[place-malstead|Málstead]] wergild happened in a Gnarthborg shipyard, three generations ago, between a [[affiliation-hrafnvar|Hrafnvar]] shipwright and a [[affiliation-grimvar|Grímvar]] boy. [[lore-mlgnahstry|Histories and Legends of Malagna]] tells what followed.
