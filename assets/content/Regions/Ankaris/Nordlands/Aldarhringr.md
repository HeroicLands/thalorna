Ask a skald where the world came from and she asks you which world you mean. That is the first thing a child at a northern hearth learns about the gods, before their names and long before their quarrels: the world underfoot is not the first world, and it is not the last. The skalds call the whole turning **Aldarhringr**, the ring of ages—_aldar-_, the age's, and _-hringr_, a ring—and the word carries the doctrine in it. An age is a _heimsaldr_ (world-age), and a world-age is born, lives and dies the way a person does. Nothing in the tellings stands outside that ring, the [[affiliation-asguardian|Ten]] included.

## The Age Before

Every telling of the beginning starts with an ending. Before [[place-worldthlrn|Mannguard]] there was another world, with its own ground, its own weather and its own gods, and it closed in fire as this one will. What the fire left was a _tvelg_ (remnant)—the lesser part, the coals and the ash and whatever in them still held heat—and the gods of this age were kindled out of it. A skald teaching a newcomer at [[lore-jol|Jól]] puts it in one breath: "The Ten were somebody's heirs before they were anybody's gods."

Nothing is told of the age before from the inside. No skald names its gods, and a skald who claims to know how many ages turned before this one is selling something. What the halls do agree on is the shape: an age that dies leaves a remnant, and the remnant is where the next age starts.

The halls do not agree on what else the remnant held. [[place-odholm|Ódholm]]'s rune-hall teaches that the [[lore-thursaett|Thursaett]], the giant-kin, are the part of the old age's remnant that would not take a new shape—the unworked coal beside the kindled one—and that the war which followed was the new age deciding which of the two would stand. The adherents of the [[affiliation-hrimthur|Faith of Hrímthur]] at [[place-holafell|Holafell]] teach the reverse: the giant-kin are the old age's own folk, older than the Ten, and the gods are the newcomers who took a world that was not theirs to cut. Both accounts are taught openly, and a hof that keeps one does not refuse a guest who holds the other.

## The War and the Void

The Ten fought the giant-kin and broke them. That much every hall teaches, and the [[affiliation-asguardian|Asguardian Pantheon]] holds it as the reason the Ten are ten: it is the war they won together, and it is the only thing all ten of them have ever done together.

The beaten kin were not killed. They were driven past the edge of anything made, into the _ginn_, the yawning void beyond creation, and walled into [[place-thursguard|Thursguard]], an enclosed world set out in that void with no road back from it. The rune-priests say only that the giant-kin are in it and that nothing has come back out. They say less about the void itself, because the void is where [[lore-theshadow|the Shadow]] comes from. [[lore-ginnauga|Ginnauga]], the Eye of the Void that [[lore-nahilddty|Náhild]] wears at her neck, looks into that same dark, and the priests decline to say what the Eye sees there or what the void has made of the kin shut into it. A rune-caster who presses the question is told to stop asking, and most do.

One giant did not go into the void. [[lore-hrimthurspr|Hrímthur]], the Rime-Giant, was killed, and the Ten cut Mannguard out of his body in the act the hofs call the [[lore-thursblot|Thursblót]]. The ground every hall stands on is his, the rime on the high fells is his, and the wound has never closed.

## People and Death

The world the Ten cut was empty until [[lore-motefnirdty|Mótefnir]] the Maker pressed the first two people out of its clay. The account of how he made them, what the other gods gave them, and how death came in through the same door as birth is told in its own place, under [[lore-gnumborinn|Gnúmborinn]].

## The Close and the Ring

This age will close as the last one did. [[lore-aldarlok|Aldarlok]] is the north's name for that close, and the skalds tell it whole at Jól: the gate of [[place-eldheim|Eldheim]] opens, [[lore-vetrulfr|Vetrúlfr]] slips his chain, the defending gods and the [[lore-valdrengir|Valdrengir]] muster on [[place-vigvoll|Vígvöll]], and the world-ash [[lore-heimsask|Heimsask]] burns and takes both worlds down with it. The Ten fall in that fire. [[lore-eidgardty|Eidgar]] goes down with [[lore-dulmvargr|Dulmvargr]], the hound chained against that day.

What the ring teaches about the fire is that it is not the end of everything. The fire leaves a remnant, as the last fire did, and out of the coals a new world is kindled—green, wet and quiet on its first morning, with ground and weather and people in it. The gods of that age are the [[lore-tvelgaett|Tvelgaett]], the heirs who come through the fire, and they stand to the next age exactly as the Ten stood to this one. The skald at Jól does not offer that as comfort. The Ten do not see the morning, and the heirs inherit a world nobody they loved will walk in.

## What the Ring Asks

The ring is the reason the north is grave without being hopeless. A people who know the world has ended before, and was kindled again from what it left, do not expect the last fire to be the last thing. They do expect it to take everything they love. So the hofs teach the ring as a lesson about remnants: what a person leaves when they go—a kept oath, a taught craft, a child, a howe with a name on it—is the coal the next thing is kindled from, and it is the only part of anyone that crosses into what comes after. A Hofgydja of Mótefnir put it to a newcomer plainly: "Every age gets made out of the last one's leftovers. See that yours are worth the making."
