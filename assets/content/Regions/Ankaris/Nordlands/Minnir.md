Minnir appears in the [[affiliation-odvar|Faith of Ódvar]] as a severed head from whom [[lore-odvardty|Ódvar]] continues to seek counsel. The well bearing Minnir’s name holds wisdom-bestowing waters, for a drink of which the god sacrifices an eye. [[place-odholm|Ódholm]]’s rune-hall tells that Ódvar seeks knowledge of the world’s end there, that the runes stand in the water he drinks, and that the first thing they spell for him is his own death at [[lore-aldarlok|Aldarlok]]. The eye is the only price the god pays for the runes, and the well is the only place he pays it. Its Rite of Minnir’s Well uses a blessed chalice to stand for those waters when the rune-staves are recast for the coming season, expressing the faith’s teaching that wisdom must be paid for.

: The Mouth Beside the Well

```poetry {form=epic lang=en}
Dark under the brim,
a darkness paid for;
under the darkness,
understanding wakens.
Ódvar goes down
to the wisdom water.
He bears the question
no triumph can answer:
what will the ending
require of the world?
Minnir's name marks
the well's deep water;
Wind in the telling
waits on the question.
Water reflects
what bends above it.
The price has a face;
the face is his own.

One eye knows distance;
one knows brightness,
the brightness that runs
on the restless water.
Thirst lies deeper
than that light can reach.
He would know more
than the crown of a wave.
An eye is offered
for the wisdom drinking.
He takes the drink;
the depth gives knowledge.
He rises changed
from the water's edge.
One side grows dark
within his seeing.
Hard grows the world
within his knowing.

Water grows still;
the wind goes wandering.
Light lies broken
where ripples remember.
The rim has its stone;
the god has his silence.
Nothing grows lighter
for being laid open.
The road from the well
has its great weight of knowing.
Each footfall bears
what no step can leave.
The question was thirst;
the answer stays bitter.
He drinks, and goes on;
the drinking is finished.

The answer is death;
the death is his own.
Vetrúlfr waits
in the last of his knowing.
The Winter-Wolf comes
when the binding breaks.
He paid for sight
of the mouth that takes him.
He paid for the loss
he must bear toward battle.
Who drinks deeply
if knowledge is easy?
Who calls wisdom
sure shelter from winter?
The well keeps its name
in the mouths of the faithful.
The god keeps death
in the life he goes on with.

Minnir is near,
a head without shoulders,
the counselor's face
beside the god.
No breastplate rises
beneath that presence.
No hand reaches forth
for the haft of a spear.
Still counsel is sought;
still, listening matters.
Answers need no iron
to open our wounds.
Voices need no host
to hold us silent.
The mouth bears weight
like a mighty doorway.
Listen, All-Father;
listening is labor.

“What have you learned?
What work will follow?
Will the wolf have the hall
before the last winter?
Will the bench lose welcome
before the last morning?
Will the dead lose names
before the feast ends?
You know what the field
will claim at the ending.
Days come before it
with demands of their own:
a hearth kept warm,
a word made binding,
a place for the worthy,
a cup passed onward.
Count these beside it;
consider their measure.”

“What good is the knowing,”
the god asks his counselor,
“when the thing I have learned
will take all I love?”
The head gives counsel
in the hall's own telling.
“What you love has life
before that losing.
The day has its dawn;
the dawn has its labor.
Attend to the asking
that stands before you.
The last wolf waits;
the guest waits also.
One must be answered
today at your threshold.”

He hears, and time
has weight in the silence.
Knowledge casts shadow
along the threshold.
Still stands the hall,
open for honor.
The worthy come
with the cost of their worth:
warriors fallen,
the sworn word kept,
mothers whose breath
failed at the birth,
craftsmen who died
where work needed guarding.
Gathered for loss,
he gathers them still.
The wolf in his knowing
has barred no door.

Ódvar speaks;
winter lies within him.
“I cannot unknow
what the water has given.
I cannot put brightness
back in this darkness.
But the hall is mine
while the hall is standing.
The guest has come
within my keeping.
Let the wolf have
what the ending grants him.
It shall not have welcome
before that ending.
My hand can lift bread;
my word can hold fast.
I have learned my death;
my life is not finished.”

At Ódholm, the chalice
is raised in the rite.
Its draft stands for
the depth of the well.
Rune-staves are recast
for the coming season.
Hand meets wood;
the hall holds quiet.
The drinker has
a smaller coin to offer:
comfort surrendered,
a vigil kept waking,
years given over
to questions and labor.
The weary back bends
to the work of learning.
The god's missing eye
has weight in that bending.

The watcher at Ódholm
feels cold in his fingers.
A lamp burns low;
the long night deepens.
Comfort calls him
away from his keeping.
He stays with the vigil;
the stars go onward.
No raven can keep
this watch in his place.
No rune can absolve
the hand of its labor.
Small is his coin;
hard is the paying.
The god's great cost
has a claim on his choosing.

Minnir gives counsel
in the songs of the god.
“Look at the current
where your choice must travel.
Runes give shape;
your hands have choosing.
Reading the river
is never commanding it.
Let not knowing
take hold of your wrists.
Branchings of wyrd
bear choices unmade.
No voice can promise
what virtue will purchase.
Stand before doom
with the deed still yours.
Give counsel its weight
in the work that follows.”

Ódvar bears knowledge
toward all he defends.
The feast keeps savor
beneath the last shadow.
The oath keeps weight
in the witness's hearing.
The eye is not restored;
the wolf is not forgotten.
His wisdom bears
what its winning cost him.
His courage goes forth
with nothing concealed.
Minnir's voice remains
in the mouth of the telling.
The severed head
has its place beside Ódvar.
Still, Ódvar listens;
still, wisdom is bitter.

Eldheim will open;
winter will descend.
Valsal will yield
its companies for battle.
Ódvar goes forth
to the field he foresaw.
The wolf will take him;
his son will take Vetrúlfr.
So runs the telling;
so the songs remember.
The age will burn;
the world-ash will fall.
A remnant will rise;
a new morning follows.
The Ten do not rule
in the world of their heirs.
Here stands the hall,
with its door still open.
```
