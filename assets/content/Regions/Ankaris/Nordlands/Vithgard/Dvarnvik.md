## Overview

"Look at the door," a whale-oil trader at Dvarnvík tells a southern factor who has just tied up. "There is no bar on it. There never has been, and no ship that can reach us in weather has been turned away from a berth or from water, whatever flag she flew." The custom is older than any living harbor-reeve's memory, and nobody now knows why a kingdom this far into the ice decided an open door served it better than a guarded one. Four hundred and fifty people live by it, and the port is busier than its size alone would suggest.

The name is a door that is never barred, and the port has made a policy of the image. The [[lore-hersvaldrnk|hersvald]] of the port district answers for keeping that door open even to ships [[place-vithgard|Vithgard]] has reason to distrust, which is why [[being-thrldrvvrnhm|Thraldorv Vörnheim]] earns steady work as a bodyguard to the merchants who do business with crews the hersvald would rather not have vouched for personally. The town's craftwork runs unusually fine for its size: [[being-thlmthnslvrgr|Thalmthann Sólvargr]] blows glass from sand brought north as ballast, and [[being-flrnthyrstnblt|Flarnthýra Steinblót]] throws pottery glazed with minerals no other Vithgard settlement bothers importing. An open door brings in trade goods a closed one never would.

Dvarnvík's recurring argument is the hersvald's own custom turned against him. A door that will not bar a stranger cannot easily bar a debtor either, and more than one trading dispute here has ended with a ship gone before the ting could rule on what it owed.

## The Ting That Burned Eleven

Dvarnvík's ting is remembered for a darker judgment. Forty-one years ago, after a winter of failed whaling, it accused eleven people of working the winds, and all eleven were burned on [[place-askholm|Askholm]], the skerry off the port. Some plateau clans still avoid Dvarnvík, and the descendants of the accused keep their own accounts of who spoke against whom. [[lore-vthgdhstry|Histories and Legends of Vithgard]] tells the burning and the ruling that followed it.
