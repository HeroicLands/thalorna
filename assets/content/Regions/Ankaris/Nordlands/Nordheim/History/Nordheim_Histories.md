Nordheim remembers its past in judgments, household claims and songs. The stories of a grave or a
haunted road can preserve a warning without proving every deed attributed to the dead. The **Heroes
of Asguard** belong to that epic tradition: they may or may not have lived, and their adventures are
no record of Nordheim's law or institutions.

## The Three Winters

About three lifetimes ago, three cruel winters are remembered as one long famine. Nordheim's interior
and [[place-vithgard|Vithgard]] both claim the memory. Some tell of old people going over the
_aettarfall_, the kin-fell above Ormstead, so younger kin might eat. Accounts differ on who went,
whether anyone was sent, and how much of the cliff story belongs to later telling. No known law
ordered it.

One version ends with five kings swearing at the King of All Clans that no hall should abandon its old
while another had bread. Whether they made that oath in those words, or whether the tale gives a form
to a duty older than the famine, remains disputed. The present obligation of kin and clan to feed and
shelter their own does not depend on proving the oath. Nor does the story establish the origin of
[[lore-kinhalcrdt|hall credit]].

## The Frostmark Land-Taking

Twelve years ago, Nordheim settlers carried their old hall-posts down the Frostmark road and planted
them at Nýstead in a grove claimed by the Thragvel. They walked bounds with fire and treated the
rite as a claim witnessed by their gods. The Thragvel burned the first stockade; the settlers raised
another on the same posts. The grove's oaks remain a claim the two peoples cannot both satisfy. The
Nordmen's rite is part of the dispute, not proof that the land was theirs to take.

## The Howe-Warden of Greyfjord

At Greyfjord, a ship-grave is said to hold a captain of Nordheim's founding age. A family story says
one descendant entered by the sea at low tide, took the dead man's sword and fought him in the dark.
The family keeps a sword it will not draw. The tale warns against disturbing a howe and taking what
was laid with the dead. Some call the captain a _haugverdir_, a warden of his mound; the descent and
the fight belong to the family's telling.

## Voices on the Frostmark Road

Travelers tell of a child crying between the last farm and the forest. The tale places the child in
the Three Winters and says its mother left it unnamed when food failed. Some call the voice a
_frostnár_, a lingering spirit, and say a name spoken by its mother's kin could lay it. Settlers and
Varokhi each fear the sound and sometimes blame the other. Neither the cry nor the old mother's
choice should be turned into an established charge against a living family.

## The Night-Rider of Skarholme

The Skarholme story begins with a hirdman's death, a thrall-woman accused of riding him in sleep, and
a ting that would not condemn her on a dream. She later walked into the fjord. People still turn shoes
outward and keep steel near the bed when they fear the _nóttreid_. Others say that a person in the
hall wanted the hirdman dead and used the woman as an easy target. The telling leaves the sender, if
there was one, unnamed; it cannot undo the court's refusal to treat a dream as proof.

## The Mothers under the Stones

At Blód, women speak of the _haugmaedr_: mothers of the older clans laid beneath the stones. They
credit these dead with keeping a household's luck. The winter-night observance associated with them
is kept among women, beyond the public rites of the godar. Men know that it is held but do not claim
to know what happens there. The belief says what the living ask of their foremothers; it does not
settle whether every misfortune is a dead woman's judgment.
