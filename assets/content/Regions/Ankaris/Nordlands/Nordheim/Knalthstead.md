## Overview

Knalthstead holds the king's hall, and its name is older than the hall: _knalth_ is the hard grade of the stem for an oath that needs no scribe to bind it, and a stead built on that word answers for every oath the [[affiliation-kngdmnrdhm|Kingdom of Nordheim]] swears in its own name. Fifteen hundred people live inside and hard against its timber ramparts, more than any other settlement in this account, and the wall is there because a capital cannot afford to lose what it keeps: the king's hall itself, the court where a níding is named and a wergild reckoned, and the gaol below it where a man waits on the ting's word.

A ship beaching at Knalthstead's quays meets a working harbor before it meets a market: caulking sheds, cordage-walks and salting houses crowd the waterline, because the town's wealth is the fleet it keeps afloat rather than coin it has never struck. No native currency moves here—[[lore-bartercnmy|barter]] in furs, sealskin and iron tools and the running tallies of [[lore-kinhalcrdt|hall credit]] carry a season's trade until the next catch or raid settles the account. The Harbour-reeve who tolls the wharves and the Landvördr who rides the king's dues out through the district work from the same hall as the Lawspeaker who recites the law at the ting and the Hirdstjóri who masters the muster, and three of the town's own households make their living on what that concentration of offices draws to one place: [[being-mrvrhrfnsvld|Murvir Hrafnsvald]] sets amber and silver for a court that must look the part, [[being-sklfvrasgrnd|Skilfvir Ásgrind]] sells what the forest yields to a kitchen that feeds the hall's hird, and [[being-hrndmylsnrvrkh|Hrundmýl Snarvarukh]] holds the keys to the cell a níding occupies until the ting decides his case.

The king himself holds the realm only as long as the ting goes on handing back his arm-ring, and Knalthstead is where that handing-back is watched most closely: a Hringberi slighted at this hall is slighted in front of every clan with business here, which is why a king's generosity is a public performance rather than a private virtue.
