Thrumufjall is a sheer seamount on the [[place-nordheim|Nordheim]] coast, topped by an ancient temple of [[lore-thrunvalddty|Thrúnvald]] and the mother-hall of the [[affiliation-ordrstrmspkrs|Storm-Speakers]]. The exposed summit serves their weather-craft training and the receptive examination held during thunderstorms. The god’s Hofgodi keeps the hof’s rites and sea-blessings beside the order, whose craft answers to different vows. In [[being-skrldmylstrmbrn|Skrildmýl Stormborn]]’s saga, the mountain is where Thrúnvald is said to have struck the earth with [[lore-thrunhamarr|Thrúnhamarr]], and the hero leads his people there through winter storms that cease after his appeal at the summit.

## The Climb Through White Weather

A coastal telling follows Skrildmýl's winter ascent and the clearing of the storm, then returns to the mountain's hof and mother-hall. The saga's answer to his appeal is remembered under the dangerous skies that still visit the summit.

: The Climb Through White Weather

```poetry {form=epic lang=en}
Name the mountain when winter wipes the shoreline away.
Name Thrumufjall when the sea has forgotten its blue.
Sheer stone rises where the coast meets breaking water;
its summit receives what no low sheltered roof could endure.
There our feet find their footing.

Before the climb: the village under long weather:
doors pushed inward, smoke beaten flat over frozen thatch,
ropes stiff with ice, the familiar lanes lost under drifting.
The wind has a thousand mouths and no word of reassurance.
People ask whether the Thunderer has turned his face away.

Skrildmýl hears the question beneath the loud hammering gale.
He hears it beside a failing fire;
he hears it when frightened eyes search his own.
He holds to his faith: the storm is a testing.
His people's lives weigh more than an answer.

He leads them toward the mountain named in the hammer's story,
where Thrúnvald, they say, struck the earth with Thrúnhamarr.
That place stands dark in the mind before seeing,
a mark in the distance that snow cannot scour from remembrance.
They set out; snow creaks beneath their walking.

First, wind pushes a shoulder against them.
Then cold creeps past the fastening of wool.
Then snow erases the footprint behind them.
The journey has room for no glory that leaves someone straggling.
A leader's first strength is the pace that his people can share.

Watch the line bend into the weather.
Watch a hand reach back where going grows steep.
No shining array meets the white waste in splendid formation;
these are the people who wanted their own doors to hold.
Their courage has the smell of wet cloaks and exhausted breathing.

The mountain is nearer, and harder to see for its nearness.
Stone emerges like memory beneath a blanket.
The sea below pounds out a rhythm that rises through rock;
the gale plucks speech from the mouth before speech can find hearing.
Still they go upward, their longing made heavy with snow.

Skrildmýl's name travels backward from shoulder to shoulder.
The singers may thunder it out till the hall shakes its cups;
yet think of the voice that must carry through wind on the climb,
the smallest command that a neighbor can hear and obey.
Come. Keep together. There is another footing before us.

The hammer's old striking is under their feet in the telling.
The stone bears their soles, cold and indifferent to pleading.
Above them the storm lays its hand on the summit's bare head;
below them the coastline dwindles to whiteness and sound.
Between, they carry the village that weather has threatened to scatter.

Here let the singer take breath while the people take none.
Let firelight show every listening face.
We know how the saga will finish; the climbers must climb.
The ending we treasure is hidden from those who are living it.
Their weariness reaches the summit before our praise does.

There stands Skrildmýl, with his people gathered behind him.
He calls to Thrúnvald above the long roar of the winter.
His throat is raw from the cold and the length of the journey;
his breath goes up like smoke that the wind has forgotten to scatter.
Behind him the people stand waiting, their faces turned into the storm.

The storms cease. The skies open clear.
Let the sudden silence be heard after all of that shouting.
Let blue seem strange where the white has been all they could see.
Let the sea find its horizon, the mountain its distance,
and the people find faces behind the masks of their fear.

No greater blow is required here:
the hammer is named, and a people stand under clear heaven.
The tale carries homeward the light they had feared was forsaken.
Skrildmýl has led them; their walking has borne them together.
Give joy breath before asking its meaning.

We return beyond that winter.
The ancient hof stands on the summit exposed to the sky;
the Hofgodi keeps the rites, and the ships receive blessing.
Near it the mother-hall holds the labor of Storm-Speakers,
whose listening to weather is learned under vows of their own.

One summit, two callings; the sea wind visits them both.
The Thunderer's priest keeps the offering;
the Speaker must read what approaches before choosing to call.
A roof may be shared by shadows that fall at different hours.
Their service meets on the stone without making their promises one.

An aspirant stands with the thunder breaking over his head.
Rain strikes his eyelids; the iron taste gathers on his tongue.
His strong hands open, empty, against the lowering heaven.
He waits for the weather to stir in the depths of his hearing,
while his teacher watches through rain for the answering sign.

The teacher reads cloud as the sailor reads water below,
and the priest lifts his offering under the gathering weather.
At the harbor mouth spray lashes a face turned seaward;
on the summit cold water runs down the stone of the hof.
Skrildmýl's clear sky shines in memory under this darkness.

Thrumufjall holds no weather forever; the cloud comes again.
Rain scours the summit, and salt whitens stone by the water.
The hall keeps its craft, and the hof keeps its rites through the turning.
People below keep their ropes and their watch and their roofs.
The mountain is sacred in the midst of that continuing labor.

So sing the ascent when a winter has narrowed your doorway.
Give Skrildmýl the faith and the courage his saga remembers;
give also the people their footsteps, their hands, and their weariness.
A name for the mountain can keep a direction in darkness.
The way must be walked by the feet that have need of it.

Now lower the voice to the sound of the sea under stone.
Now leave the clear summit in memory above our fire.
The wind moves outside; we draw close enough to hear one another.
Thrumufjall, hold the tale of the climb in our hearing.
When the white weather rises, remember the hands reaching backward.
```
