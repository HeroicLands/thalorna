A Leigen of the river villages, asked by a foreign traveler whether the Tantsou pray to their grandparents, laughed and told him no: "We feed them, and they work for us. Your dead are resting. Ours have jobs." Ancestor worship in the [[affiliation-tantsouempr|Empire of Tantsou]] is the upkeep of a working relationship between a household and its dead, and it is the faith every Tantsou practices, from the Emperor to the poorest farmer.

In this faith, the dead are not gone. They are posted. When a person dies, the [Ministry of Final Account]{.name data-type="affiliation"} reads his **moral ledger** and assigns him by its balance. A good balance makes him an **ancestor spirit**, a minor official of heaven whose post is to argue for his living descendants before the celestial bureaucracy. A bad one sends him to labor in the lower realms for a term, or to dissolution.

An ancestor's posting is work, not rest. He files petitions for his descendants, intercedes with the officials of the bureaus and spends the credit on his own ledger to ease their way. A strong ancestor with a full ledger can turn rain toward the family's fields, lean on a magistrate's ruling or arrange good omens before a venture. A weak one, his ledger spent, can do nothing.

## The Economy of the Dead

The faith divides the empire's families into the spiritually rich and the spiritually poor, and each condition feeds itself.

A rich family burns spirit money by the basket, pays for temple petitions and sponsors festivals, and every offering credits its ancestors' ledgers. Well-credited ancestors argue more effectively, the family prospers, and its prosperity pays for more offerings. The economy of the dead mirrors the economy of the living, and both hold the castes in place.

A poor family cannot afford proper offerings. Its ancestors' accounts run low, weakened ancestors cannot argue for it, and its fortunes sink further. Poverty in Tantsou is a debt in heaven as well as a want on earth, and the system keeps it in the family.

The Tantsou do not call this unjust. Each family stands where its accumulated ledger places it: poverty means one's ancestors earned too little credit, and wealth is the proof of ancestral virtue. The system, they say, is the system.

## The Shrine

Every household in the empire, the imperial palace and the meanest hut alike, keeps an **ancestor shrine**. It holds the **ancestor tablets**, carved wooden plaques bearing the names and death-days of the family's dead; offerings of food and spirit money; and a small brazier for burning spirit documents.

The shrine is as necessary to a household as its hearth or its well. A family without a kept shrine has no channel to its dead, no advocates to carry its petitions and no way to send them anything. It is cut off, with no one to speak for it in heaven.

Each tablet is an ancestor's address: the place where the spirit receives what is sent to him and from which he answers. Moving a tablet without the proper rite is like moving house without telling the post, and the ancestor's mail goes astray. Breaking one cuts the ancestor off entirely.

## What Each Side Owes

Visitors from the Aureldian west often take ancestor worship for fond remembrance of the dead, and in Tantsou it is nothing of the kind. The living and the dead are bound by obligations that run both ways.

**The living owe the dead** offerings of spirit money and food, a shrine kept in order, a yearly report on the family's affairs filed through the Hearth Monitor (_Tsaiken_), the keeping of every death-day, and children to carry on the line. A family that fails in these has broken a contract, and an ancestor whose offerings are neglected loses credit and strength, which is a harm the living do to the dead.

**The dead owe the living** advocacy in heaven, warnings sent through dreams and omens, the protection of the household and counsel on great decisions. An ancestor who ignores his descendants' petitions has abandoned his post, and a family may in the end dismiss him: take his tablet from the shrine, stop his offerings, and cut him off from everything the family can send.

What unsettles visitors most is that **the dead give orders**. Through a Leigen, in a dream or by an omen, an ancestor may arrange a marriage, forbid a venture, demand that a feud be carried on or an alliance made. A Tantsou treats such a command as a directive from heaven. Disobeying it is a breach of the cosmic order as well as of filial duty, and it costs heavily on the ledger. The older and stronger the ancestor, the more binding his word, and in most families the dead have the last say on every great decision.

## The Leigen

The Leigen (spirit masters) arise in the [[lore-nouwen|Nouwen]] villages with the gift of speaking to the dead. They carry a household's business to its ancestors and bring back the answer, as interpreter, go-between and spirit-clerk. They receive and read ancestral commands, treat sickness that comes from the spirit side, often alongside the Yaiwo healers of the [[lore-tsaihuo|Tsaihuo]], perform the village rites that enter a birth, a union or a death with heaven, and settle disputes by asking the ancestors concerned to rule.

A Leigen is a peasant by caste, and every caste respects him. Speaking with the dead, he speaks with heaven, and that gives him an authority caste cannot measure. Warlords and even [[lore-zhazho|Zhazho]] consult Leigen on hard spiritual questions, always in private, since to acknowledge a peasant's authority openly would break caste.

## The Elder Dead

The [[lore-flksinale|Sinale Folk]] and [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazár Folk]] within the empire do not keep the Tantsou rites. The Sinale honor their dead by planting trees and tending sacred groves, in private ceremonies with nothing of the ledger about them. The Khazari make memorials and keep the tools and weapons of the dead as sacred things. Both traditions stand wholly apart from the human faith, the celestial bureaucracy has no place to file them, and the [[lore-tanchei|Tanchei]] do not look into the matter.

## The Festival of the Dead

The greatest festival of the dead is the [Festival of the Dead]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} (Singlei Jui), held in [Zhenyou]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the Month of Snow, at midwinter. Families provision their ancestors for the coming year: spirit money and paper goods are burned in great heaps, accounts are settled, and the Leigen speak with the dead to learn their instructions for the year ahead. It is the holiest and the costliest event of the Tantsou year, and a family that cannot keep it properly faces a year with weakened advocates in heaven.
