A Tānchéi matchmaker who has arranged two hundred weddings tells every new bride's mother the same thing: "You are not giving your daughter away. You are opening an account." Marriage in the [[affiliation-tantsouempr|Empire of Tāntsou]] merges two families' spiritual standing into a new household, registered with the celestial bureaucracy and given a [[lore-celestlrdr|moral ledger]] of its own. The Tāntsou keep love and marriage in separate categories. Love is a private feeling; marriage is a filing that changes the cosmic standing of two houses.

The customs differ by caste, and one rule holds at every level: a marriage creates a new household in the classification, and the new household has to be sorted correctly.

## What a Marriage Joins

When two clans agree a match, the new household receives three things. It has one moral ledger, opened from the balances of both families. It has one set of [[lore-ancstrwrshp|ancestor spirits]], drawn from both lineages, to argue its case in heaven. And it is assigned a [[lore-celestlrdr|Hearth Monitor]] (_Tsàikén_), the minor celestial official who watches the household and reports on it.

That is why the clan council arranges the match and the couple does not. The council asks which families' ancestors will work well together, which pair of ledger balances makes the strongest new account, and which alliance best serves the clan's standing in heaven and on earth. The couple's feelings for each other do not enter into it.

## Who May Marry

**The marrying castes.** Legal marriage, registered with the celestial administration, is open to the [[lore-zhazho|Zhazhò]], the [[lore-wohou|Wòhou]], the [[lore-tanchei|Tānchéi]] and the [[lore-tsaihuo|Tsàihuo]]. These castes stand high enough in the classification to warrant the formal filing.

**The castes that may not.** The [[lore-nouwen|Nouwén]], the [[lore-shuonong|Shuonong]] and the [[lore-noutsou|Nòutsou]] may live together and raise children within their caste, and their unions are not registered. The classification holds their households too low to justify the filing. Most Nouwén accept it as the way of things; most Shuonong, who could afford a wedding finer than many nobles', resent it in private.

The Nouwén have their own answer. A village Léigèn (spirit master) performs a bonding rite that presents the couple to the ancestors of both families, if not to the bureaus of heaven, and in the villages that rite carries all the weight a temple wedding carries in the cities. The empire does not recognize it, and nobody in the village cares.

**The clan's choice.** Every legal marriage is arranged by the clan council, and the council weighs four things: the combined ledger balance of the two families; whether their ancestors will agree, which a Tānchéi or a Léigèn learns by divination; the political and material gain of the alliance; and the astrology of the match, read from the birth-years and the spirit animals of the couple (see [[lore-clndrstrlgy|Calendar and Astrology]]).

## The Wedding

The wedding is a registration, and each of its five steps does a piece of administrative work.

**The contract** (_Zhaolòu Hou_). The two clan councils sign a contract, drafted by a Tānchéi and sealed with both clan seals. It sets out the terms of the merger: what property passes, which ancestor tablets move to the new household's shrine, what each family owes, and on what grounds the contract can be dissolved.

**The petition** (_Léi Séi Chou_). A spirit document is burned at the temple, addressed to the Bureau of Creation ([[affiliation-tengwouronglei|Tsài Séi]]), asking that the new household be registered and given its Hearth Monitor. It carries the names, birth-dates and spirit animals of the couple and a statement of both families' ledgers. A petition filed with an error is held to leave the household wrongly classified from its first day, and families pay a scholar to check it twice.

**The consultation** (_Kuò Léi Wou_). Before the union is final, a Léigèn speaks with the ancestors of both families to win their consent. An ancestor who objects can forbid the marriage, and defying the prohibition costs the household heavily on its ledger. Clans bargain with reluctant ancestors much as they bargain with each other, with extra offerings burned until the spirit relents.

**The tablet transfer** (_Kuò Chou Dou_). Chosen ancestor tablets are carried from the bride's family shrine to the groom's. This is the solemn heart of the wedding, because it changes where those ancestors are addressed in the celestial filing: from tonight they argue for this household. A Léigèn or a Tānchéi performs it, in exact ritual words, so that the change of address is recorded correctly.

**The sealing** (_Hou Wō_). A seal carved for the occasion is pressed into red clay and set on the new shrine beside the transferred tablets. Every spiritual filing the household makes will carry that seal, and from the moment it is set, the household exists in the cosmic order.

## Love

Love is not forbidden in Tāntsou; it simply does not count. Affection between a husband and wife is welcomed when it comes, and a marriage without it is not thought the worse for that: it was filed correctly, and that is what a marriage is for. Love outside marriage is tolerated within the bounds of caste propriety, so long as it brings no dishonor onto the household's ledger.

The elite castes court with poetry, gifts and music, and treat courtship as one of the social arts, not as a path to marriage. A good deal of the [[lore-weizhairui|Wéi Zhài Ruì]], the court's game of intrigue, is played over who will marry whom.

## Consorts

A man or a woman of the marrying castes with the means may keep consorts alongside a spouse, as many as the household can support in goods and in offerings, since every member of a household adds to its ledger and to what its ancestors must be fed. Consorts belong to the household and are not parties to the contract: they bring no ancestor tablets, and their families' ledgers stay separate. Their children are acknowledged and raised in the household, and are named heirs only when no child of the marriage is fit.

## Children and the Heir

A child born of a legal marriage is registered at birth by a temple rite that gives it a place on the household's ledger and a classification under the spirit animal of its birth-year. The clan leader and the council choose the heir by ability, character and astrology, and very often by divination to learn which child the ancestors favor.

The heir inherits the duty with the property. He keeps the ancestor shrine, carries the family's petitions to its dead, and answers for the household's ledger, and an heir who neglects them impoverishes the dead along with the living.

## Dissolution

A divorce is rare, because it removes a registered household from the cosmic order, and the celestial administration does not undo its filings willingly. A clan council considers it for three causes: dishonor heavy enough to threaten both families' ledgers, failure to produce an heir, or proof that the petition was false, in which case the household was never properly classified at all.

The process takes a petition to the Bureau of Creation, the return of each ancestor tablet to its own family's shrine, and the breaking of the household seal. It is costly, it damages both families' ledgers, and it is a social disaster, because it says that the classification made a mistake, and the Tānchéi and Léigèn who approved the match carry the blame.
