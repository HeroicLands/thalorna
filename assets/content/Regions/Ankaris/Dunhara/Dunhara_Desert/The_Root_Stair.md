## Overview {#overview}

**The Root Stair** is a spring in a narrow gorge of the foothills along the eastern edge of the [[place-dunhardsrtrgn|Dunhara Desert]], two days north of [[place-marchroad|The March Road]], where the desert's mountain margin climbs toward [[place-graznmntns|The Grazian Mountains]]. The water rises in a pool at the foot of the gorge's western wall. Above it, wild figs grow out of the cliff from cracks at the height of three men, and their roots come down the rock to the water in a mesh as thick as a man's arm, crossed and grown together so evenly that they make a ladder. At the top of the ladder the figs' crowns shade a ledge. Two tribes of the [[affiliation-dunhartrbs|Dunhara Tribes]] water their herds at the pool in the cool months, and their poets keep a verse about a tall stranger who drinks there at dawn and is gone before anyone comes down to the water.

## Who Holds It {#who-holds-it}

The spring is shared by right of usage between two tribes whose circuits meet in the gorge, and a Water-Warden named by the two keeps the order of their watering: which tribe brings its herds down first, on which days, and for how long. The order is recited, not written, and each tribe's poets keep it.

## Layers {#history}

The figs and the ladder are in the oldest verses of both tribes, and in none of them are the figs young. A desert mystic who kept a vigil on the ledge in the time of the present sheikhs' grandfathers came down saying the ledge was made to be slept on, and the tribes have counted the spring a holy place since, though no pilgrimage round includes it.

## What Is There Now {#present}

The order of watering is in dispute. The Water-Warden who kept it died in the summer, and when the two tribes came to the gorge in the autumn their poets recited the order differently: each version gives its own tribe the first days after the rains. The herds of both tribes are in the gorge now, the pool cannot water both at once, and the young men of each tribe stand by the water with their hands near their knives.

A new Water-Warden must be named by both tribes, and neither will name a man of the other's choosing. A desert mystic has climbed the root ladder to the ledge and refuses to come down until the tribes agree, and neither sheikh will be the one to drag a mystic off a holy place. While he sits there, the dispute cannot be settled by force without both tribes being shamed.

## Dangerous Neighbors {#dangerous-neighbors}

Two tribes with their herds and their warriors in one narrow gorge are the danger, and a single blow at the pool starts a feud that the Keepers of the Feud reckon for generations. Flash floods come down the gorge after a storm in the mountains, often under a clear sky over the gorge. Scorpions and vipers shelter in the root mesh in the heat of the day.

## What a Party Finds {#what-a-party-finds}

A gorge between walls of red rock, its floor trodden to dust by the herds, narrowing to a clear pool at the foot of the western wall where the water comes up through sand. Above the pool the fig roots come down the cliff in a mesh, the thickest of them running from the trees straight down to the water and the lesser ones grown across between them at even steps, each step the height of a stride and worn smooth on top. At the head of the ladder the roots part around a ledge the length of three men lying end to end, floored with flat stones grown over by the roots that hold them, and roofed by the figs' crowns, which keep it in shade through the hottest hour. At the back of the ledge a crack in the rock holds a little water that drips from above and is cold even at midday. The mystic sits on the ledge with his back to the cliff.

## Hooks {#hooks}

- **The order of watering.** Both sheikhs agree to hear an outsider who has eaten the salt of neither tribe, and will each pay a party that finds a way to settle the order before blood is shed at the pool.
- **The old Water-Warden.** The dead Water-Warden had a son who walked the circuit with him and may know the order as his father kept it, and the son has gone with a caravan to the March Road.
- **The mystic.** The mystic says that whoever climbs the ladder and spends a night on the ledge comes down knowing who the spring belongs to, and invites anyone willing to try.

# The Truth of the Root Stair {#truth .secret}

**The camp.** The gorge is **Nalleristo**, "the place of the root", a cool-season camp of the [[lore-flksenali|Senali]], abandoned. Companies of Senali came down to this spring from the mountains in the cool months, when the figs bear their winter crop, and stayed until it was eaten. They slept on the ledge out of the sun and above the floods, and when the crop was done they went back up into the mountains. They planted the figs in the cracks over the water and led the roots down the rock as they grew, so that the trees made the ladder, the shade and the floor of the ledge.

**The leaving.** The visits dwindled with the Senali themselves, and the camp was empty long before either tribe walked its circuit. The tall stranger of the poets' verse is a story the tribes tell; whether any Senalo still crosses the margin and stops here is for the game master to decide.

**The ledge.** A night on the ledge tells nobody whom the spring belongs to. It is a cool, quiet place where a tired person sleeps well, and that is all it is.

> [!TIP]
> The quarrel is about the order of watering, not about the spring. A party that finds the old Water-Warden's son, or gets the two versions of the order recited side by side before both sheikhs, can show where they part. The planting of the figs, the visits and the cool-season camp are this note's own account; a game master may change them.
