**Óthlin vorth**, the Youngest Son's Telling, is a _vorth_ of the [[place-skathwald|Skathwald]] households: hearth prose, told by the mistress of a house to its children on a night of _grema_, when the [[place-vrystwldrvrs|river]] is a road and the children are old enough to be sent across it. It is a wonder tale and a piece of law in one. A [[lore-varokhiclt|Varokh]] feud-cord is knotted, a payment is carried over the ice, a voice under the ice offers what nobody may ask, a [[lore-lynxttm|lynx]] lies in the track with a thorn in its foot, and the matter the tale turns on is the household's: who may carry, who may sell, and what a witnessed count is worth. The details are the teller's to change, and the telling marks its own variants as it goes. The version here is the one told at Vandholt, the [[lore-foxttm|fox]] village of Skathwald named in the [[place-vrystwald|Vrystwald]] roster, and in the clearings around it; the landings downriver tell it to the same point and end it the other way, and the Skathwald telling reports the downriver ending in its last breath.

Put another stick on the fire. This is the telling of the youngest son of a Vandholt household, and of the grain that went over the ice, and of the woman who carried half of it. It is a long one and the fire has to last.

You have seen the river in _grema_. It goes white and quiet and the sleds go over it, and the war-band goes over it, and your mother goes over it to the landing with the furs. It is a road then, the only road in this country that does not go through trees. You have also seen the dark places in it, where the water runs too fast under the ice to let the ice thicken, and you have been told not to walk there, and told again. The telling is about that. Keep it in your head.

There was a household at Vandholt, in the years when the hall stood where the old hall stood, which is to say nobody knows when. The man of the household killed a man of the kindred across the water, at the landing, in autumn, over a word. The word is not in the telling. Some say it was about a boat and some say it was about a trapline, and either way the man was dead for it. The Other Chief heard the matter before the households. The Skathár of the far bank knotted the cord, one knot, for one man. The payment was reckoned aloud before witnesses: six sacks of grain of the big measure, to be carried over the ice in _grema_ when the river is a road, because grain was what the household had. The mistress of it had traded a whole winter's furs for that grain at the landing, meaning to eat it.

Then before the ice came the man of the household died of a thing in the chest that no charm reached, and they buried him behind the hall with his axe. The household's southern woman, its [[lore-othmundrnk|Óthmund]], dug the ground, because the ground was hard and that is what she was for.

So the household that owed was a widow, and three sons, and the woman from the south, and six sacks of grain. The _frath_ was at the watches on the Grukar paths, where the men are in _grema_, and none of the three sons was of it yet. The eldest was near his name. The second was a winter behind him. The youngest was at the age where he carried water with the southern woman, because there was nobody else in the hall for him to carry it with.

Her daughters were at the landing with the furs, which is where a daughter is in _grema_. Her sons were in the hall. That is why the sons went.

You should know one more thing before the first crossing, and it is this. On this river, while a cord is knotted between the banks, no fire is lit on either bank for the other's watch. The far bank lit no fire for Vandholt that winter and Vandholt lit none for the far bank, and the Grukar paths run along both sides of that river. So the grain was owed for a dead man, and it was owed for the fires too, and the mistress knew both when she counted the sacks.

The eldest went first.

He took the sledge and the six sacks and the straight way, because the far bank's landing is straight across from Vandholt's and a man can see its smoke from the ice. The southern woman was at the water hole with the axe when he set out, and she said to him, "Go where the ice is loud."

He said he had crossed that river every winter of his life.

"On a sled," she said, "with your mother driving."

He went straight. The straight way crosses the dark water, where the current runs under and the ice above it was a hand thick that morning. The ice over dark water is quiet. You do not hear it under you. He did not hear it, and then he heard it once. It held him. It did not hold the sledge. He went in to the chest with the sledge under him and the sacks going down past his knees, and he got out because the ice behind him held, and he got two sacks out wet by their necks, and the sledge and the other four went to the bottom. He came home short.

He came home with two wet sacks and the skin of his face gone white where the water had been, and his mother put him by the _glóm_ and said nothing to him for three days, which was the whole of what she said about it.

Now say it with me, because you will say it twice more. He came home short.

The mistress spread the wet grain on hides by the fire and turned it until it dried, and she lost some of it to the fire and some to the mice, and what was left was not two sacks. She went to her sister's household, and she came back with a borrowed sledge and sacks enough to make the count six again. There was bark behind that count. "If this goes in the river," she said, "we eat bark until the thaw, and then we eat the bark the thaw leaves."

The second son went next.

He took the borrowed sledge and the six sacks and he did not take the straight way, because he had seen his brother's face. He went up along the bank to cross above the dark water, where the ice is loud. The track there runs between the bank and the thorn thicket, the big one that is there yet, and in the track lay a lynx with one forefoot held off the snow.

A lynx is nobody's _wesk_ on this stretch of the river. Vandholt's is the fox, and the far bank's is the fox, because the far bank was Vandholt once, and a fox would not have been lying in a track with its foot up. A fox would have had the thorn out by the first evening and a story about it by the second. The lynx is a different beast. It stays where it is and it watches, and it does not ask. Keep that in your head with the ice.

The second son was a winter from his name and had a sledge behind him and the day was short. He said what everyone says, that a lynx is a lynx, and he went around it, through the thorns, with the sledge.

The thorns opened a sack. He did not know it. He crossed above the dark water where the ice was loud, and he crossed well, and the sledge went over behind him, and from the thicket to the river and from the river to the far landing there was a line of grain in the snow behind him as straight as a drawn cord. Every bird in Skathwald fed on it. That was the one good thing that came of the second crossing.

At the far landing the mistress of the far bank came down with her Skathár and her witnesses, and she counted the sacks on the ice before she let one be carried up, and the count was five and a sack with its mouth open and nothing much in it.

"A payment that is short is not a payment," she said. "It is a gift. I am owed. I am not given to." And she sent him back over the ice with all five, and the open one, and the sledge, and he came home short.

He came home with the sacks he had set out with, less a line of grain the birds had, and his mother did not put him by the fire, and did not speak to him either, and he sat where he could.

So that was two crossings. Say it. He came home short, and he came home short.

Now there was grain for five sacks and part of a sixth, and the mistress made the sixth. She took what was on the hides. She took what her daughters sent up from the landing by a woman going that way, and that was not much, because the daughters were trading and a trader does not send her stock home. She swept the sister's floor. She made six sacks of the big measure, and she tied them, and this time there was not bark behind the count. There was the count.

The sister wanted her sledge back, and the thorns had cut its lashings anyway. So there was no sledge.

Six sacks of the big measure is a sledge-load. A strong man carries three on a frame. Nobody carries six.

The mistress looked at what she had in the hall. She had an eldest son by the _glóm_ with his face coming off in strips, and a second son nobody was speaking to, and a youngest who had never crossed that river except behind her on a sled, and the southern woman.

The southern woman cut the hole where the ice was thin and carried the water up the bank twice a morning, and in nine winters she had never once gone in, which was more than the eldest could say that week.

"Take the woman," the mistress said to the youngest. "She knows where the water is."

He said, "She is from the south. There is no ice in the south."

"There is none," the woman said. "There is a river, and it does not freeze, and if it did, nobody would be fool enough to walk on it." She said this tying a sack onto a frame. "I have cut your water hole every morning of every winter since before you could carry a bucket, and a water hole is cut where the ice is thin, because that is where it wants to be cut. I know where the river is thin. I know it to a step. I know it better than the fish do."

The mistress told her she would be beaten if the grain went in the river, and the woman said nothing to that, because a woman who has cut a hole every morning for nine winters knows what the household eats. The sacks went on the frames, three and three, and that is why there were two who carried.

They went out at first light.

The lynx was in the track.

It lay where the second son had found it, between the bank and the thorns, with the one forefoot off the snow, and it had been there three days, and you could see it had, because the snow around it was packed down the way snow is packed by a thing that has turned and turned and not gone anywhere.

The youngest stopped.

"Your brother did not stop," the woman said.

"My brother came home short."

He put his frame down on the packed snow and went to the lynx, and it let him, and some say it let him because it was his own beast and had been since he was small, and some say it let him because it had a thorn in its foot three days and would have let a Grukar pull it. You may tell it either way; both are told. The thorn was as long as his finger and black, and it had gone in at the pad and come to the bone, and he pulled it with his teeth because his hands were in mittens. The foot bled. He packed the pad with snow and held it there until the bleeding slowed, and the lynx lay with its ears flat and did nothing to him.

Then it got up and went down the track. Down, not up. Away from the far bank's smoke.

"It is walking where it is loud," the woman said. She had her head on one side. "It is walking the wrong way, and it is walking where it is loud."

"We go up," the youngest said. "The far bank is up."

"The far bank is across. Where you cross is the question. I will tell you a thing about ice and then I will tell you a thing about me, and both are short. Ice is loud where it is thick and quiet where it is thin, and your brother took the quiet way because it was the short one. That is the thing about ice. The thing about me is that I have three sacks on my back, and you have the other three, and a payment that goes two ways is two gifts. Where you walk, I walk."

He went after the lynx. She went after him.

The river bends below Vandholt and narrows, and the ice there is black and you can see the water under it, moving, and the ice talks. You have heard it. It knocks under you like a fist on a post, and it runs away from you along the river in a long crack like a thing let go. The lynx kept to the shore ice along that stretch, where it is thick and loud, with the black water a spear's length out, and the two of them went along it behind, and in the middle of it, where the river is narrowest and the water under the black ice is fastest, the voice came.

It came up through the ice in his father's voice. It had his father's cough in it, the one from the autumn.

"Óthlin," it said. That is what it called him, which is what his father had called him and what everyone calls a youngest, so it proves nothing either way. "I know what they mean to do with you on the far bank, when the grain is in their hands and the witnesses have gone in to the fire. Ask me, and I will tell you."

The youngest kept walking. He said, "I have three sacks on my back and I cannot put them down on this ice."

"Walk," the woman said behind him.

He walked. The ice knocked under him.

"Óthlin," the voice said, in his father's voice with his father's cough in it, "I know the names of the men who will do it, and which hand each of them favors, and which of them will stand at the door. Ask me, and I will tell you."

The youngest did not stop. But he turned his head, and the ice spoke under his heel when he turned it, one word, in its own tongue.

The woman said, from behind him, and she did not say it to him, "Your father is not under the river. He is behind the hall. I dug the ground, and the ground was hard, and he is in it, and his axe is with him. I know where every man of the household is put, because I put them there."

"Then you are a voice with his cough in it," the youngest said to the ice. "Keep the cough. I did not like it the first time."

He walked.

"Óthlin," the voice said, and now it did not cough, "I know whether he is with the fox, or walking. Ask me."

That is the one that stopped him.

The ice spoke under him when he stopped. The woman heard it; she had cut that river nine winters. "Walk," she said. He did not. The lynx ahead had stopped too and was looking back, with the foot up.

"Nobody asks that," the youngest said. He said it to the ice, and he said it slowly. "The Weskár does not ask that, and the Weskár goes out to the dead. I carry water. I carry grain. I do not carry that." And he lifted one foot, the way you lift a foot off ice that has spoken, and put it down ahead of him, and the other, and the knocking under him went along the river away from him like a thing let go, and after that there was only the ice, which does not talk in words.

Some say the voice was a _hrág_, that lives in dark water and wants nothing in the world but a person standing still on thin ice for as long as it takes. Some say it was the river, which talks in _grema_, and the boy had his father's cough in his own ears because he had listened to it all autumn, and the rest he put there himself. The grain went over either way.

The lynx went on down the river, and the river widened, and the black ice went white, and the knocking stopped. Below the narrows the river spreads out over stones. The Vandholt women call it **Vandhreth**, the exacting ford, for what it costs a boat in summer. In _grema_ it is the one place on that river that is frozen to its stones. There is no water under it to talk. The lynx walked out onto it on three feet and stopped in the middle and sat, and the two of them came past it with their frames and went over the ford to the far bank. When the youngest looked back from the far bank it was gone, and there were no tracks on the far side.

Then they walked up the far bank to the far landing. It is a long walk. They came to the landing at the end of the light, with the smoke of the far bank's hall going straight up, and the far bank's dogs telling the whole far bank that something had come up the river that was not a sled.

The mistress of the far bank came down to the landing with her Skathár and her witnesses and the fire behind them in the door, and she looked at the two of them with the frames on their backs, and at the way they had come up the bank from below, and she said, "You came by the ford."

"We came by the ford."

"Nobody comes by the ford."

"My brother came the other way," the youngest said. "You counted him."

"Why two of you?"

"Nobody carries six."

"Your brother carried six."

"That one had a sledge. My eldest brother's is in the river."

She counted them. She counted on the ice, before she let one sack be carried up, the way she had counted his brother. She had the sacks off the frames and stood up in a line and she went along them and opened each one to the arm and she said each number aloud for the witnesses. Six sacks of the big measure. Her Skathár said six. The witnesses said six.

The woman from the south said, "Count them again. I carried three of them, and I would like to hear it twice."

The mistress of the far bank looked at her for a while. Then she counted them again, aloud, and the witnesses said six again, and the mistress of the far bank sent a girl of her household up to the hall for the cord.

Now the cord. You have seen a _skathdreth_ and you have been told not to touch one. The far bank's had a knot in it for the man killed at the landing, and the mistress of the far bank held it up in the door with the fire behind it, so the knot showed black, and she untied it with her own hands before her witnesses. Then the Skathár said it, and he said it the way it is said:

"The blood of the man killed at Vandholt landing is paid. It is paid in full, and it is witnessed."

And the kindred of the far bank said it back to him word for word, every one of them that was in the door: "It is paid in full, and it is witnessed." That was the end of the cord.

"Tell your mother," the mistress of the far bank said, "that when the paths are walked this spring, there will be a fire on this bank."

"I will tell her."

Then she looked at the southern woman again.

"That one knows the ice," she said. "She came by the ford with three sacks on her back and she asked for the count twice. I will give your mother a sledge for her, and four sacks of the big measure, and that is a good price, and it is what you lost in the river, and you know it is."

The youngest said, "She is not mine to sell."

"I did not say yours. I said your mother's. You are your mother's mouth tonight, on my landing."

"Then my mother's mouth says ask my mother," the youngest said. "I carry grain."

The mistress of the far bank laughed at that. Some say she laughed because the boy was a boy. Some say she laughed because she had got the answer she expected and had wanted to hear it said in front of her witnesses, so that there was no question later whose the woman was. Either way she gave them a place by the fire that night, which is owed to anyone who has paid, and in the morning she said to them at the door, "Go home by the ford. The straight way has a sledge in it."

So they went home by the ford, and the lynx was not on it, and they came up the Vandholt bank at midday with empty frames. The eldest got up from the _glóm_ when they came in, with his face the way it was, and the second got up from where he sat, and the youngest told his mother the count, and the witnesses, and the knot, and the fire for the spring, and the price. Nobody in that hall said short. Nobody has said it in that hall since, and it is said at every other hearth on the river.

The woman told it shorter. "Six," she said. "Twice."

A new cord was knotted on that river before the thaw, for another man and another word, and the fire waited. That is Vandholt.

Here is where the telling parts. Listen, because downriver they tell it the other way, and at the landing theirs is the one.

This is how it is told at this hearth. The mistress heard the whole of the crossing from her son, and the voice under the ice, and the ford, and the price, and she said, "A sledge and four sacks is a good price. It is what we lost. She has set it to the measure." And then she said, before the household, which is how a thing is said that is meant to stay said: "That one carried half the blood-payment of this household on her back over the ice, by a ford nobody walks, with a thing under the river talking in my dead man's voice. A person who has carried a household's debt is of the household." And at the thaw she freed her, before the Other Chief, and she was married into the household, to the youngest, when he had his name, and the carrying earned it. The name is not in this telling, because every hearth puts its own youngest son's name there, and so does this one, when he is awake to hear it. The children of that marriage were Varokh, and some of them are your grandmothers, and some of them are the far bank's.

Downriver, at the landings, they tell it that the mistress took the sledge and the four sacks, because a sledge and four sacks is a good price and it was what she lost, and that the woman went over the water to the far bank in the first boat of the thaw. The household ate through the rest of _grema_ on the price of her and had a sledge for the next crossing, and the far bank got a woman who knew the ice to a step and lit its fire a season the sooner. Downriver they say that is the better ending. They say that a mistress who turns a crossing into stores has done the thing a mistress is for, and that the woman was sold where she could see her old hall across the water, which is more than most who come up the river get. That is the downriver telling, and they tell it well. This hearth tells it the other way, and has told it.
