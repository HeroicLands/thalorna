The richest man in a river city of [[affiliation-tanvurempr|Tānvür]] walks behind the potter in a procession, and the law says why. The Shüknürk are the merchants, traders and moneylenders of the empire, the seventh caste, ranked below the farmers because a farmer makes the grain and a merchant only moves it. A Shüknürk house may own warehouses in three provinces and lend to a Warlord, and its wealth buys comfort and not one step of standing: the law that permits it to prosper is written to keep its silver out of politics, and it is enforced with execution.

## Trade and Lending

A Shüknürk house buys what the [[lore-zathuk|Zāthük]] make and the [[lore-nokvur|Nōkvür]] grow, carries it from the province where it is cheap to the province where it is dear, and sells it. The great houses keep warehouses and factors in several river cities, run barge trains along the canals, and hold the empire's trade with the foreign merchants who are let into the walled quarters of the ports. The smaller ones keep a stall, a cart and a ledger.

Some houses lend money. Lending is legal and closely supervised: the rate is capped, every loan is witnessed and entered, and a moneylender who presses a Nōkvür household into ruin answers to the magistrate. The profit is large enough that the great houses lend anyway, to one another, to Vüshōk officers short of cash before a campaign, and, quietly, to Warlords. [[lore-tanvrcrncy|Tānvür Currency]] explains how those debts are recorded and enforced.

## Rights and Restrictions

The [[lore-castelaws|Caste Laws]] give the merchant three rights:

- **Wealth**: a Shüknürk may accumulate as much as commerce brings him, and his holdings are protected by law.
- **Travel**: he may move freely within the empire, and may hire [[lore-zathuk|Vüsnürk]] soldiers to guard his goods on the road.
- **Contract**: he may make binding agreements with other merchants and with clients of any caste.

They bind him with five restrictions:

- **Tax**: he pays at rates far above any other caste's, and a merchant who fails to pay loses his goods, then his liberty, then his life.
- **Fair dealing**: usury, fraud and sharp practice against the Nōkvür or anyone below are punished by fines, prison or death.
- **No politics**: he may take no part in governance or military affairs, and any attempt to sway state policy is tried as treason.
- **No marriage**: a merchant may keep a household and concubines, and the law recognizes no wife and no heir; his house passes by its own articles, not by inheritance.
- **No personal name**: he is known by his house's name and his place in it, as "Third Factor of the House of the Jade Scale."

## The Commercial Law

The [[lore-zeisik|Tëng Lüt]] carries a long body of commercial law, and every merchant's clerk knows four of its rules by heart:

- Every significant contract is witnessed and entered in the register.
- The prices of food and medicine are watched, and gouging on them is a crime.
- Fraud, false weights, deliberate overcharging and misrepresentation are serious crimes.
- Bankruptcy voids nothing: the debtor's holdings are seized and divided among his creditors, and what remains unpaid stays on his house's record.

The merchant pays two taxes. The commerce tax is a share of the profit on every transaction; the wealth tax is levied each year on everything the house holds. Hiding wealth to escape the second doubles the penalty, and evasion of either is treated as rebellion against the [[lore-zhekzhos|Zhëkzhös]], whose revenue it steals.

## The Bar Against Politics

The empire's lawgivers looked at the lands where merchant councils and guild oligarchies had grown strong enough to rival the warrior lords, and wrote the Shüknürk out of power entirely. A merchant who conspires to influence policy, who tries to form a council claiming any authority, or who negotiates with a Zhëkzhös on a matter of state is executed for treason, and his partners and household may share his sentence.

The bar is enforced by the people it protects. [[lore-vushok|Vüshōk]] administrators audit the merchant houses, make inspections without warning and pay informants inside them. A house that proves compliant for long enough is trusted further and may see its rates eased; a house that falls under suspicion finds its contracts slow to be entered and its credit worth less.

## Standing

A Shüknürk is necessary and distrusted. The empire needs his barges, his warehouses and his loans, and it holds him below the farmer and the potter because his living comes from other people's work. A wealthy merchant dresses well, keeps retainers and eats better than a Vüshōk officer, and he still steps aside for a Zāthük craftsman in the street. Money buys him everything the Caste Laws do not reserve, and they reserve the things he wants most.
