Pinned to the wall of every veterans' hall on the Haradian coast is a broadsheet gone brown at the edges: a broken chain, a rising sun, a ship under a Haradian flag, and the words _we bleed gold for an empire that gives us nothing but chains_. The guilds printed it twelve years ago to raise a war. The men who keep it on the wall now read it as a promise the guilds made and did not keep, and that reading is the shortest true account of the Confederation of Haradian States.

The Confederation is a league of five merchant city-states on the islands of an archipelago in the eastern [[place-vylarianse|Vylarian Sea]], off the coast of the [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Sultanate of Amradad]]. It is the leading naval power of the eastern sea, it is rich, and its politics are as cutthroat as they ever were under imperial rule. Roughly twelve years ago Harad fought and won a war of independence from the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian Empire]]; the merchant oligarchs who paid for that war now hold the freedom its veterans bled for. The island shores are crowded with wealthy trading cities, and the hill country of the larger islands belongs to tribal communities and ancient ruins.

## At a Glance

- **The five city-states:** [[affiliation-tamavar|Tamavar]], [[affiliation-kethara|Kethara]], [[affiliation-varoshan|Varoshan]], [[affiliation-ashkabel|Ashkabel]] and [[affiliation-azhun|Azhûn]], each with its own laws, militia and council.
- **Governing body:** the **Grand Council**, meeting at Tamavar, under a rotating **Arch-Consul**.
- **The three great guilds:** the [[affiliation-auricompct|Auric Compact]] (banking and trade finance), the [[affiliation-corsairleg|Corsair League]] (shipping and naval power) and the [[affiliation-mrchntryvl|Merchantry of the Veil]] (luxury goods and information brokerage). Between them they rule the Confederation in all but name.
- **The navy:** the most powerful naval force in the eastern Vylarian Sea, built from the makeshift fleet that won independence and operated largely through the Corsair League.
- **The founding myth:** the Battle of [[place-tamzirshoals|Tamzîr Shoals]], celebrated every year with naval reviews and public festivals.
- **The archipelago:** the islands themselves, whose channels control the eastern sea routes and hold the Confederation's naval bases and trading posts.
- **The bazaars:** the markets of the great port cities, which sell goods from across the known world.

## The Islands

Harad is an archipelago of half a dozen islands in the eastern Vylarian Sea, lying forty to eighty miles off the Amradi coast, and it holds no shore on the mainland. The islands' coastlines are deeply indented, and their natural harbors have made Harad the premier maritime region of western Ankaris. On the larger islands the ground rises from narrow coastal plains through rolling hills to arid scrub. The climate is warm and dry, and the hot summers are tempered by the sea breeze. Across the strait to the east, the caravan roads of the [[place-khzryndsrtrgn|Khazryn Desert Region]] and the [[place-dunharargn|Dunhara Region]] reach the sea on the Amradi shore, and their goods cross to the islands by ship.

The channels between the islands are the other half of the country. Whoever holds them controls the trade routes of the eastern sea, and the Confederation holds them: the islands carry its naval bases and its trading entrepôts, and, for those who know the passages, its smuggling havens.

## History

### Under the Empire

Harad was a Vylarian colony, among the first lands the expanding Vylarian Republic absorbed, and it was an old possession by the time the Republic became the Empire in Year 1. For centuries it was the empire's eastern anchor and its gateway to trade with the [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Aû'Khelâthu]], Dunhara, the Khazryn and the lands beyond. Haradian harbors funneled eastern wealth into imperial coffers, and Haradian ships carried Vylarian goods to markets the legions could never reach. By any measure it was one of the empire's most valuable provinces.

It was also one of its most restive. The Haradian city-states had been trading powers long before Vylaria conquered them, and they never fully accepted the role of provincial subjects. Haradians paid their taxes, obeyed their governors and sent their sons to the legions, and each generation did so with deeper resentment than the last.

### The Rise of the Merchant Princes

Success planted the rebellion. As the empire's reach extended east and south, Haradian merchants became its indispensable intermediaries: they knew the languages, the sea routes, the customs and the contacts that Vylarian traders lacked. The great trading families of Tamavar, Kethara and Ashkabel grew spectacularly wealthy and built commercial empires inside the political empire that ruled them.

These **merchant princes**, the heads of the great trading houses, held power that sat uneasily beside Vylarian authority. They controlled the shipping lanes, the warehouses, the counting houses and the networks of agents and factors that made the eastern trade work. A Vylarian governor issued edicts; Haradian merchants decided whether goods moved. The empire needed Harad's commerce far more than Harad needed the empire's governance.

The Vylarian court saw it, and its answer was heavier taxation and tighter regulation. New tariffs fell on Haradian exports, preferential trade agreements were rewritten to favor Vylarian merchants, harbor fees rose, and customs inspections became more frequent, more intrusive and more expensive. The governor's office, once a sleepy posting for minor aristocrats, went to harder men with instructions to bring the merchant princes to heel.

What galled the Haradians most was that they could not contest any of it. They had no representation in the imperial Senate. A petition was ignored; a bribe bought a season's relief before the next levy. They paid for the empire's legions, roads and bureaucracy and had no voice in how those taxes were set or spent. The rallying cry of the independence movement—_we bleed gold for an empire that gives us nothing but chains_—was meant as accounting.

### The Independence Movement

The merchant princes who led the revolution began as lobbyists, petitioning the imperial court for trade reform, tax relief and seats in the Senate. When lobbying failed they turned to economic pressure: coordinated slowdowns in the harbors, delayed shipments and sudden shortages of critical goods. The empire answered with arrests, confiscations and more legions sent to "maintain order."

At that point the merchant princes made a calculated decision. If they could not reform the empire from within, they would leave it. A revolution needs soldiers, and they had warehouses full of goods and no armies, so they recruited a cause. Broadsheets appeared in every harbor district, tavern and market square, printed at guild expense and attributed to "the people of Harad." They spoke of freedom and self-determination, of a Harad that governed itself instead of serving as a cash-box for foreign aristocrats, and they carried the broken chains, the rising suns and the ships under Haradian flags. The merchant princes presented themselves as patriotic leaders defending their people's dignity.

The people answered. The harbors were full of men who had spent their lives loading cargo for the enrichment of Vylarian factors. The fishing villages were full of families whose sons had been conscripted to fight imperial wars that had nothing to do with Harad. The hill tribes had endured decades of imperial administrators who taxed their livestock and took their young men and gave nothing back. The grievances were real, whatever reasons the men channeling them had of their own.

The militias that formed in the port cities drew on every class. Dock workers drilled beside ship captains, and fishermen sharpened boarding pikes next to merchants' sons. The tribes sent warriors down from the island hills, fierce fighters who cared nothing for merchant politics and hated the empire with a conviction the coast could only admire. For a brief moment Harad was united across class, country and culture.

### The War

The Haradian War of Independence began roughly twelve years ago, and it began badly. The legions garrisoned in Harad moved to crush the rising before it spread, and the first engagements—skirmishes outside Tamavar and an attempted blockade of Kethara—went decisively the empire's way. Tamavar was briefly besieged. Haradian militias were routed in three successive engagements on the coastal plains; brave and disorganized, they were no match for professional legions in open battle. The hill tribes did better harassing the imperial supply lines in country they knew, but they lacked the numbers to force a decision. Rebel morale wavered, and the merchant princes, who had expected a swift and nearly bloodless separation backed by economic leverage, found themselves financing a real war with real casualties and no assurance of victory.

Aû'Khelâthu saved the rebellion before Haradian arms could save it themselves. The merchant princes had sent envoys south at the start of the rising, and the Khelâthi court received them with the elaborate hospitality that signals serious interest. Aû'Khelâthu had its own reasons to want a weaker Vylaria: the empire's eastern provinces, Harad chief among them, were where the Vylarian and Khelâthi spheres met, and a Harad under imperial control was a Harad whose harbors served Vylarian strategy. An independent Harad would be a grateful trading partner with no reason to prefer Vylarian commerce to Khelâthi.

The Khelâthi were not sentimental about the calculation. They supplied gold, weapons, naval stores and experienced military advisors who understood how to fight a professional imperial army. Khelâthi gold kept the rebellion solvent while the merchant princes' own resources hemorrhaged; Khelâthi weapons armed militias that imperial confiscations had left short of steel; and Khelâthi naval advisors helped the Haradian captains turn their ragtag flotilla into something close to a coherent fighting force. The aid was never acknowledged. The Khelâthi court kept up the polite fiction of "humanitarian commerce" with a troubled neighbor, and the merchant princes knew that foreign backing, advertised, would undercut the story of Haradian self-determination. Everyone involved, the Vylarian court included, knew exactly what was happening, and the empire's protests to Aû'Khelâthu were met with bland assurances that fooled no one and were meant to fool no one.

Aû'Khelâthu was not the only foreign hand in the war. The Sultanate of Amradad, whose relations with Vylaria have long been antagonistic, was among the powers that helped the Haradians win their breakaway, and the islands have kept cordial relations with the Sultanate since.

The sea saved the rebellion on the water. The legions were formidable on land, but on the islands they depended on naval supply lines, because the roads through the hill country of the larger islands were too slow and too exposed to tribal raiders. The Haradian captains knew these waters better than any Vylarian admiral: the tides, the shoals, the hidden channels between the islands, the harbors where a shallow-draft vessel could shelter and a deep-keeled warship could not. They began a campaign of harassment, cutting supply convoys and intercepting troop transports, until the eastern Vylarian Sea was too dangerous for imperial shipping without a heavy escort.

A flotilla fought that war, not a navy: warships from the merchant fleets, armed traders, fishing boats fitted with rams and boarding planks, and harbor craft loaded with archers. Each was outmatched by the Vylarian navy's heavy warships, but they were fast and numerous, and their captains knew every rock and current in the eastern sea. They raided, retreated, drew the imperial squadrons into unfamiliar waters, and refused the decisive fleet engagement the empire wanted.

The turning point came at the Battle of [[place-tamzirshoals|Tamzîr Shoals]]. A Vylarian squadron, the largest force the empire had assembled in the eastern sea, moved to break the Haradian blockade of the island channels; if it passed, the empire could resupply its garrisons, land reinforcements and strangle the rebellion. The Haradian fleet—warships, armed merchantmen, fishing boats and harbor craft under captains who had never fought together—met it in the shallows of the Shoals, on ground the Haradians had chosen. The sandbars, submerged rocks and hard currents were water the deep-drafted imperial warships could not navigate. The imperial admiral, committed to the channel, found his ships unable to maneuver, unable to retreat and unable to bring their superior weight to bear, while the lighter Haradian vessels worked the channels around them like wolves around elk.

The battle lasted two days. At its end the squadron was destroyed—burned, captured or driven onto the shoals to break apart—and the imperial admiral was dead. Several of the Haradian captains had expected to die there and found themselves instead in command of the eastern Vylarian Sea. With no naval supremacy, the garrisons ashore were cut off from supply and reinforcement, and over the following months they were reduced one by one, by siege, by negotiation and by desertion as imperial soldiers understood that no relief was coming. The empire, already stretched by pressure on its other borders, chose pragmatism over pride, and its reluctant recognition of Haradian sovereignty followed within the year. The war remains the defining event of the Confederation and a turning point in the politics of the whole Vylarian Sea.

### The Betrayal of the Revolution

Victory tasted of ashes. The merchant princes had financed the war: they had organized the supply lines, bribed the officials, printed the broadsheets and paid the soldiers. During the chaos of the fighting they had also, quietly, positioned themselves to control whatever government came out of it.

On paper the Confederation that took shape in the months after the victory was a democratic federation of equal city-states. In practice the merchant guilds wrote its charter. The Grand Council's voting rules gave disproportionate weight to property owners, the judiciary was staffed with guild-friendly magistrates, and the tax code was rewritten in favor of commercial enterprise. The harbors, customs houses, warehouses and banking networks stayed in the hands of the families that had held them before the war.

The veterans—dock workers, fishermen, tribal warriors and the ship captains who had sailed into the teeth of the imperial fleet at the Shoals—learned that the freedom they had bled for was not theirs. They had been promised land, guild membership, pensions and a voice in the new government. They received a discharge, a small purse of silver and the knowledge that the men who had paid for the revolution now owned the result.

The merchant princes had not lied, exactly. They had spoken of freedom, and Harad was free: free of imperial taxation, of Vylarian governors, of conscription into foreign legions. The princes had meant their own freedom—to trade without imperial interference, to set their own tariffs, to grow rich without sharing it with a distant emperor. The common people heard something different in the same words, and they fought and died for a promise that was never actually made.

The guilds moved fast. The Auric Compact, the banking cartel that had underwritten the war, called in its debts; the new Confederation owed it staggering sums, and it used that leverage to bend government policy to commercial interest. The Corsair League, a brotherhood of war veterans at its founding, was taken over by the merchant marine and turned from a fraternal body into a shipping cartel. The Merchantry of the Veil went on as it always had, in the shadows, profiting from the confusion of the transition.

Within two years of independence the Confederation was an oligarchy in all but name. The guild families lived in palatial estates and the veterans in harbor tenements. The hill tribes, who had sent their warriors to die for a revolution of the port cities, received nothing at all and were worse off than before, since the Vylarian administrators had at least kept up the roads. The people who had fought saw plainly that they had traded one set of masters for another. The Vylarian governor's palace in Tamavar became the headquarters of the Auric Compact, and the men behind its desks were no kinder than the men they replaced. Guild fees took the place of imperial levies and fell as heavily on the same backs; private enforcers took the place of the legions, and the fist was as hard.

### The Suppression

The guilds did more than ignore the veterans; they suppressed them. The men and women who had led the militias, captained the war-flotilla and organized the resistance were the people the new oligarchy could least afford to leave at liberty: experienced fighters with popular followings, networks forged in combat and a grievance every dock worker and fisherman shared.

A single dramatic crackdown would have looked too much like the imperial oppression the revolution claimed to have ended, so the guilds marginalized them slowly and methodically. Prominent veterans were accused of debts they did not owe, charged with crimes they did not commit, or implicated in conspiracies the guilds' intelligence services had fabricated. Some were bought off with minor posts and small pensions, enough to live on and not enough to organize with. Others were arrested, tried in guild-friendly courts and imprisoned. A few disappeared.

The most dangerous, the captains and militia leaders who refused to be silenced, were declared outlaws. Some were accused of piracy, an easy charge to fabricate against any captain who had served in the war-flotilla; others of sedition, of conspiracy against the Confederation, or of the catch-all crime of "disrupting public order." Warrants were issued and bounties posted, and the Auric Compact's enforcers and the Corsair League's patrol ships were given broad authority to hunt them down.

Many of the outlaws took to the sea, and if the guilds wanted to call them pirates, pirates they became. The island channels and hidden coves that had sheltered the war-flotilla became their hunting ground. They raid guild shipping, shelter in ports the Corsair League cannot easily reach, and keep loose networks of mutual aid among the dispossessed. They call themselves the **Free Captains**, the **Unchained** and the **Shoals Brotherhood**; the guilds call all of them pirates.

## The Harad of Today

The undercurrent of rebellion has never gone away. It rises—a harbor strike in Kethara, a tax riot in Tamavar's dock districts, a tribal raid on a guild caravan—and each time it is put down hard. The guilds learned from the empire's mistakes and send no legions, because legions make martyrs. They use economic pressure instead (revoked licenses, called-in debts, blacklisted families), quiet enforcement by guild enforcers, and the steady weight of an economy built to keep the powerless where they are.

The anger accumulates. The veterans of the Shoals are growing old, and their children have inherited the betrayal. The dock workers and fishermen know what was promised and what was delivered, and the hill tribes remember everything. In the taverns, the veterans' halls and the hidden anchorages of the archipelago, people still pass around the old broadsheets and read them with a bitterness their authors never intended.

So the Confederation stands free, prosperous and seething. The merchant guilds rule through money and the institutions they control. The veterans and their children nurse the betrayal, the outlawed captains raid from the island channels, and the hill tribes trust no one. Everywhere—in the dock districts, the fishing villages and the tribal camps—people remember the words that recruited them and wait for someone to mean them.

Into this sails the [[affiliation-thetamzir|Tamzîr]]. Her captain fought at the Shoals, believed the promises and watched the merchant princes take the victory. He now runs cargo on the margins of a Confederation that would rather he did not exist: too famous to ignore, too dangerous to tolerate, and too stubborn to kill quietly. The guilds want him brought to heel, the veterans want him to raise the flag again, and he wants to keep his crew alive and his ship moving. Nobody involved expects that position to hold.

## Government

A clerk of the Grand Council puts the constitution to a new arbitrator in two sentences: "Every city has an equal voice in the Council. Count the votes by property and see whose voice carries." The Grand Council is made up of representatives of each member city-state. In theory each city has an equal voice; in practice the wealthiest cities and their merchant guilds dominate the proceedings. The office of Arch-Consul rotates among the cities and is more ceremonial than powerful, and the real decisions are made in back rooms by guild leaders.

Each city-state keeps its own internal government, militia and laws, and the patchwork of regulations between them bewilders outsiders. Smuggling and legal arbitrage—profiting from the differences between one city's laws and another's—are thriving trades.

## Society

Haradian culture is mercantile at its core: wealth, reputation and the ability to strike a profitable deal are its highest virtues (see [[lore-haradianclt|Haradian]]). The great trading houses, sprawling family enterprises that combine banking, shipping, warehousing and political influence, are the true power in Harad. Their warehouses line every harbor, their agents work in every port of the Vylarian Sea, and their influence reaches into the courts of Vylaria, [[place-provenzrgn|Provènzia Region]] and [[place-helionis|Heliónis]].

Society divides sharply between the guild elite and everyone else. The guild families live in palatial urban estates, patronize the arts and educate their children in rhetoric, mathematics and mercantile law. Below them a large middle class of independent merchants, skilled craftspeople and ship captains forms the economic backbone. At the bottom, dock workers, sailors and the urban poor live in cramped harbor districts and grow more restless every year. The tribal communities of the island hills stand apart from the port cities: they keep older traditions, speak archaic dialects and resent the coastal cities' dominance, and they supplied many of the foot soldiers of the war of independence and have seen little benefit from it.

Haradian cooking is famous across Mídhalión, built on spiced meats, flatbreads, honeyed pastries and strong coffee.

## Religion

Haradian faith is syncretic. The [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aurèldían Pantheon]], imposed in the Vylarian period, still dominates the coastal cities, where the merchant class favors [[lore-venusiadty|Ólvenía]] for prosperity and [[lore-menervadty|Ménérva]] for knowledge, and sailors and naval officers honor [[lore-taranondty|Táranon]] of the storms. The eastern states and the hill tribes keep strong ties to the [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|Āsháian Pantheon]], a tradition older than the Vylarian conquest; [[affiliation-ahurdaen|Āhúrdáén]] (order and harmony), [[affiliation-korsadra|Khóršádrä]] (knowledge and eternal light) and [[affiliation-aravesta|Árávēštä]] (prosperity and hedonism) have large followings. In many Haradian cities the Aurèldían and Āsháian temples stand side by side, and a single family often patronizes both.

## Magic

The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Ordo Arcanis]] kept a strong presence in Harad through the Vylarian period, and it is still active in the major cities, on worse terms. The merchant guilds regard it as a possible tool of Vylarian influence and have restricted its work. Some guild leaders would expel it outright; others see its uses and would rather co-opt it.

Independent practitioners—hedge mages, alchemists and fortune-tellers—are more common in Harad than in Vylaria proper, partly because the Ordo's grip is looser here. The Ordo resents it and keeps pressing for its monopoly to be enforced, sometimes using Vylarian imperial agents as proxies.

## Relations

Relations with Vylaria are officially correct and privately hostile. The empire has not forgiven the loss of Harad, and Vylarian agents, often working with the Ordo Arcanis, operate in the Haradian cities, gathering intelligence and cultivating sympathizers. Haradian merchants, for their part, are pushing hard into markets the empire once dominated.

Heliónis, to the north-west, is Harad's natural trading partner, and the two share cultural and intellectual ties. Aû'Khelâthu, to the south, is its most important long-distance partner, and that relationship runs deeper than commerce. Khelâthi gold, weapons and military advisors were instrumental in the war of independence, a debt the merchant princes deny in public and honor in private, through preferential trade agreements, reduced harbor fees for Khelâthi vessels and quiet diplomatic coordination that favors Khelâthi interests over Vylarian ones. The surge in Haradian–Khelâthi commerce since independence is one of the largest economic shifts on the Vylarian Sea: trade that once passed through Vylarian intermediaries now runs directly between Haradian and Khelâthi ports, enriching both and further weakening the empire's commercial position. For Aû'Khelâthu the investment in Haradian independence has paid for itself many times over.

The Sultanate of Amradad holds the mainland across the strait, and relations with it are cordial: the Sultanate, long antagonistic to Vylaria, was among those who helped Harad win its independence. Beyond its shore, relations with the nomadic tribes of Dunhara are complicated: the desert peoples hold the overland trade routes the island cities depend on, which makes the relationship profitable to both and often tense.

## The City-States

- [[affiliation-tamavar|Tamavar]]—the largest and wealthiest city and the de facto capital; seat of the Grand Council and of the Auric Compact.
- [[affiliation-kethara|Kethara]]—the naval stronghold on the narrows at the heart of the archipelago; home of the Corsair League and of the war's veterans.
- [[affiliation-varoshan|Varoshan]]—the eastern gateway on the island nearest the mainland, where sea trade meets the caravan goods ferried across the strait; the most cosmopolitan city, where the [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|Āsháian]] faith is dominant.
- [[affiliation-ashkabel|Ashkabel]]—the cultural heart, famous for its ship design and its academies, with a more balanced politics than its sisters.
- [[affiliation-azhun|Azhûn]]—the river-mouth port on the [[place-alzriver|Alz]], whose quays are the conduit between the Dunhari desert trade and the western sea routes, ruled by its merchant guilds from the **House of Factors**.

## Commerce and Currency

The Confederation pays in the [[lore-hardncrncy|Haradian currency]], the system it built after secession and administers through the **Bayt al-Khazînah**, the treasury of the [[affiliation-sodnaqirin|Sôd-Naqîrîn]]. Its denominations are the Vylarian ones (Aurion, Argo and Bit), but they are backed by Haradian reserves rather than by the Vylarian [[affiliation-aerarimmpr|Aerarium Imperii]], and the Haradian Aurion is slightly underweight by the Vylarian standard. A network of Bayt-affiliated moneylenders carries the banking. Vylarian script is not honored in Harad, and Haradian script is not honored in Vylarian territory.
