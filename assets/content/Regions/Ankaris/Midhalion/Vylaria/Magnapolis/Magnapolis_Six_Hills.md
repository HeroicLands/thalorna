## Overview

Everything in [[place-magnapolis|Magnápolis]] is carried up or down one of six hills, and a bearer learns the names before he learns the streets. The Six Hills stand in a cluster on the eastern bank of the [[place-vylarisrvr|River Vylaris]], and the city's bounds follow their contours rather than any drawn circle: an irregular sprawl, kinked along the river and notched and bulging wherever the high ground runs.

A bearer who has carried firewood, water and wine up the hill-lanes for thirty years teaches an apprentice by the slope: "The high ground is the good ground. Better air, better view, and nobody's cart-wheels in your ear. But nothing up there comes to you. You carry it, one load at a time, and that is how the great houses know what an address costs."

## The Hills

- [[place-monsaquila|Mons Aquila]], the Eagle Hill, is the highest. The walled [[place-urbsaquiln|Inner City]] and the [[place-palatimgnm|Grand Palace]] crown it.
- **Mons Corónus** (the Coronal) and [[place-monslaurine|Mons Lauríne]] (the Laurel) are the twin patrician hills that together make **the Heights**. Corónus is the older and prouder, ringed with the ancestral villas of the senatorial houses; Lauríne is greener and newer, and the **Gilded Quarter** climbs its lower slopes.
- **Mons Sápiens**, the Scholars' Hill, carries the [[affiliation-panepistmm|Scholars' District]] and the **Circle of Ménérva**.
- **Mons Vétus**, the Old Hill, was once second only to Aquila. Its early monuments weather unrepaired, its great families have drifted to Lauríne, and its once-proud Circle has gone shabby, dignified still but visibly fading.
- **Mons Rípae**, the River Hill, is the crowded working height above the wharves, its slopes packed with the densest of the **Warrens**.

## Between the Hills

The saddles, valleys and riverside flats between the hills carry the city's daily business: the **Grand Market** on the central flats, the **Wharves** beneath Mons Rípae, and the [[place-circusmaximusmagnapls|Circus Maximus]] along the valley between Vétus and Rípae. As a rule the high ground is prized and the low ground is dense, busy and poorer, except where the river makes the wharves rich.

The hills explain where the city's twelve Circles fall. A Circle is three hundred yards of level pavement, and level ground is what a hilly city is short of, so the Circles sit on the broader hilltops, in the saddles and on the river flats, never on the grades. The avenues that string them together run to irregular lengths and bearings as they climb and descend.
