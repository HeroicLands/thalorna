## Overview

The Grand Council Hall stands at the head of the merchant quays of [[place-besikar2|Besikar]], where the Guild Quarter meets the harbor road, and it was built to count money. It is the old imperial customs-house: a long hall of gray stone behind a colonnade of twelve columns, raised on a broad flight of steps above the quay so that the carts could unload at its doors. A pediment over the colonnade once carried the device of the empire, and the device has been cut away with chisels, leaving a rough patch of pale stone in the gray. The [[affiliation-grandcouncil|Grand Council]] of the [[affiliation-cnfdrtntrzlnstts|Confederation of Taruzelian States]] sits here twice a year.

## The Hall

Inside, the customs floor is a single room the length of the building, lit by high windows and paved in squares of white and gray marble worn hollow along the old paths of the clerks. The twenty-five councilors sit in a horseshoe at the far end, on five benches of five, and over each bench hangs the banner of its city: [[affiliation-besikar|Besikar]], [[affiliation-yamuzel|Yamuzel]], [[affiliation-qudumel|Kudumel]], [[affiliation-bazunel|Bazunel]] and [[affiliation-sazumun|Sazumun]], in the order they signed the charter. The Arch-Consul's chair stands at the open end of the horseshoe, a step higher than the benches and no wider.

Between the chair and the benches stands the lectern. It is the customs-house's own: a heavy desk of black wood on a stone base, sloped to hold a ledger open, where the imperial collectors once read out the day's tariffs. On the first morning of a session the Reckoner of the Weight stands at it and reads the weights of the five cities from the register of the [[affiliation-merazuni|Merazuni]], and the councilors write the figures down before any other business is read.

The public stands behind a bronze rail at the near end of the floor, where the merchants once queued to pay their dues. Any freeman of a member city may stand there during a session, and in the first days the rail is crowded with factors, clerks and the runners of the great houses, who carry each count up the street to the counting houses before the Clerk has finished entering it.

## The Rooms Behind

Two doors at the far end of the hall lead to the rooms behind. The Clerk of the Council keeps the left-hand rooms, where the acts of the Council lie in iron-bound chests and the petitions of the year are lodged, read and sorted. The right-hand door opens on a covered passage to the Arch-Consul's residence, a merchant's house of three floors next to the Hall that the Confederation keeps for whichever councilor holds the seal that year. This year it belongs to [[being-dnshkmn|Danashu Kumuni]] of Kudumel, and his kitchen feeds half the councilors at the noon break.

Between sessions the hall is shut and its floor swept once a week. The Clerk's rooms stay open every day but feast days. A freeman who wants a petition read takes it there, pays the copyist's fee and is given a number, and his city's councilors decide whether to sponsor it.

## What Is Said of It

The porters of the quay say that the hall's floor still remembers the tariffs: the clerks' paths are worn deepest in front of the old Vilérian collectors' desks, and a councilor who walks them to his bench is walking where the taxes of the empire were counted. The veterans who come to hear their petition read on the last afternoon of every session repeat a harder telling, that the councilors of the first session cut the empire's device off the pediment with their own hands and then sat down inside to count in the same way.

## The Present {#present}

The Hall is shut until the summer session, and the Clerk's rooms are the busiest they have been in years. Petitions lie in their chests by the dozen, and the one the Hall's porters talk about is the veterans', which [[being-zlmrzzn|Zilmar Ezzinu]] brings to the residence before every session and which has been read on the last afternoon of every one. [[being-dnshkmn|Danashu Kumuni]]'s kitchen is feeding councilors already, and every house that wants a measure read early has sent someone to his table: [[being-dlzrtndn|Delzuru Tanaduni]] in person for the League's toll, and a clerk of [[being-trmnqzkn|Tirmana Qizikuni]]'s for the audit of the treasury.

## History {#history}

The Hall was the imperial customs-house of Besikar, where the empire's collectors read out the tariffs at the lectern and the merchants queued at the rail to pay their dues. The Grand Council took it for its seat after independence, and the device of the empire was chiseled off the pediment. Since then the Council has sat on the customs floor twice a year, the Reckoner of the Weight has read the cities' weights from the lectern at every opening, and the house beside it has been the residence of each year's Arch-Consul.

## Hooks {#hooks}

- **The last afternoon.** [[being-zlmrzzn|Zilmar Ezzinu]] wants a party at the rail for the whole session, to count how many councilors are still on their benches when the petition is read.
- **The copyist's fee.** A freeman who paid the copyist's fee for his petition has been given a number and nothing since, and he wants a party to learn whether [[being-dnshkmn|Danashu Kumuni]] has ever seen it.

# The Truth of the Hall {#truth .secret}

**The order of business is sold.** The Arch-Consul sets the order in which measures and petitions are read, and he gives the list to the Clerk on the night before each sitting. The Clerk's senior copyist writes it out fair for the lectern, and he writes a second copy, which he sells before dawn to whichever house has paid for the session. This session the buyer is the [[affiliation-balinuni|Balinuni]], who sent Danashu Kumuni to the Council and like to know what their man intends before he does it. A party that learns of the second copy can sell the knowledge to any of the other houses, or to Danashu, who does not know.

**The passage is a meeting-room.** The covered passage to the residence has a side door onto a lane behind the Hall, and the door's key is kept by the Arch-Consul. Councilors go to the residence by the passage at the noon break, and the house men who want a word with them come in by the lane. More measures are settled in the passage in the noon hour than on the floor in the afternoon.
