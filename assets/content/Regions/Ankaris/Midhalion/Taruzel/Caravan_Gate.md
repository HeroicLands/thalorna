## Overview

The Caravan Gate is where the east comes ashore in [[place-qudumel2|Kudumel]]. It stands on the city's eastern waterfront facing the strait, forty miles across from the Amradi coast, and the ferries that carry the caravan goods of the [[place-dunharargn|Dunhara Region]] and the [[place-khzryndsrtrgn|Khazryn Desert Region]] over the water land there and nowhere else in the city. Everything that comes off them is counted, weighed and taxed before it passes the arch into the eastern market, and the counting is the Gate's whole purpose.

## What Is There

The landing is a long quay of cut stone with six ferry berths, each with a broad ramp running down into the water so that camels and horses can walk off a ferry's deck without being hoisted. Behind the berths lie the watering pens, walled yards with stone troughs fed from the city's cisterns, where the animals are held until their loads are cleared. The noise of the pens carries across the whole waterfront.

Three buildings stand between the pens and the city wall.

- **The weigh-house**, a low hall with wide doors at both ends, holds the great beam-scales of the Warden of the Weights. Bales go in at one door, are weighed, sampled and sealed with a lead tag, and come out at the other. The city's standard weights, cast in bronze, hang on a rack behind a grille at the back of the hall, and any merchant may ask to have his own weights tried against them.
- **The dues hall** belongs to the Treasurer of [[affiliation-qudumel|Kudumel]]. Its clerks sit at a long table under a painted list of the dues, written in Taruzeli and in the two tongues of the desert, and every bale's lead tag is entered and paid for there in coin.
- **The Balinuni desks** stand under an awning against the dues hall wall, where clerks of the [[affiliation-balinuni|Balinuni]] chalk their house's share on the bales and write up the partnerships. Their ferry-master, [Nirabu Balinuni]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a stocky, sunburned man with a gold ring in one ear, knows every caravan master on both shores and is usually at the end of the quay shouting.

The arch itself is the city's eastern gate, a tall mudbrick gateway faced in blue-glazed tile with a tower on either side. Its doors are cedar, banded in bronze, and they stand open from first light until the last ferry is cleared. Through it lies the eastern market.

## How the Gate Works

A caravan master landing at the Gate waters his animals, hands his bales to the weigh-house, pays the dues on their tags and walks his goods through the arch, usually in one long day. The measurers of the weigh-house, the _ashurum_, speak a little of every tongue the ferries bring and settle disputes over weight on the spot. A merchant who thinks he has been cheated takes his complaint to the Warden of the Weights at the weigh-house door, every morning at the opening of the doors. The council's watchmen keep the arch, and no guild enforcer stands at it.

In the last weeks of summer, when the east wind brings the grit off the mainland, the Gate closes at noon and the ferries wait out the afternoon in the strait.

## What Is Said of It

The porters of the landing say the tiles of the arch were glazed blue so that a caravan master coming over the strait could see the Gate from halfway across. The caravan masters say they can see it from further than that, because they can see the price of it. The water-sellers at the pens say that a ferry carrying a dead man always lands at the sixth berth, and the sixth berth's ramp is the only one kept clear on a crowded morning.

# The Truth of the Gate {#truth .secret}

**The Gate's takings are down,** and the dues hall knows why without saying so. A part of the caravan goods is coming over the strait by night on open boats and landing on the north beaches of [[place-nishurel|Nishurel]], where it pays no dues at all, and two councilors who take a share of every landing make sure the Gate's officers are never sent north to look.

**One set of weights is heavy.** The bronze standards on the rack behind the grille are true. The set the weigh-house actually uses for the heaviest bales, the spice and the dyes, is a copy cast a little heavy, and it was put on the beam three seasons ago. A bale weighed against it balances early, is entered short, pays less due than it owes, and sells in the market at its true weight. The measurer who keeps the beam at the far end of the hall is paid by the bale by the same men who land goods on the north beaches, and he swaps the true weights back onto the beam whenever the Warden's own inspector is in the hall. A merchant who asks for his weights to be tried against the standards is shown the true ones.
