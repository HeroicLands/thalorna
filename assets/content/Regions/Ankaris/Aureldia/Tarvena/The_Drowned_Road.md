"You'll see the lamps tonight if the fog comes in, and it will," the harbor teller of [[place-durvolka|Durvolka]] tells a priest newly come down from the inland valleys. "One in every seaward window on the Lantern Stair. That's not for the boats. The boats have the bell. The lamp is so that the ones who are not coming back in a boat can find the door." The Drowned Road is the folk faith of the [[place-galvaren|Galvaren]] coast. Its whole doctrine fits in a sentence: the sea keeps the people it drowns, and in the fog they come home.

## What the Coast Believes

The coast holds that a drowned man does not go where other dead go. The sea keeps him, and he stays in it, cold and patient, until a fog comes off the water thick enough to walk in. Then he comes up out of the sea and along the drowned road, the way from the water's edge to the passage grave of his own parish, where the old ones (_zekudi_) of the coast are waiting. The graves are older than the Tarveni and older than anything else on the shore except the standing stones, and the coast holds that they are where the mortal world thins. The drowned go to them for that reason, and for the same reason nobody else goes into them without need.

A drowned man who cannot find his grave wanders. He comes to the doors of the living instead, cold and lost, and the cold he brings is the cold that takes the old and the sick on foggy nights. So the house shows him the way. A lamp set in the seaward window, the threshold lantern (_ezkobe_) that every Tarvenan house fills for its dead, tells him the house knows he is out there, and sends him on up the road. The pale lights that drift among the stones in fog, which outsiders call [[being-vprwsp|vaporwisps]], are to the coast the drowned who have been out too long and forgotten the way; they shun a steady light, and that too is what the lamp is for.

The coast also tells what the sea does with pride. The drowned ruin under [[place-kaurema|Kaurema]], the Black Cliffs, is, by the tellers' account, a city the sea took for it, every soul in it drowned at once and none of them ever told, and the fog that lies thickest over that stretch of water is their fog.

## What a Household Does

The practice is plain and almost entirely domestic, and a family of the coast keeps it without a priest.

- **The lamp.** On a foggy night the house sets its lamp in the seaward window and keeps it burning till the boats are home and the fog has lifted. A house that lets its lamp go out while a man of the household is at sea has, by the coast's reckoning, told him not to come home.
- **The iron.** Every boat carries a smith's hammer, because iron struck hard breaks up whatever walks in the fog. A crew that hears something alongside in a fog strikes the gunwale with it and keeps rowing.
- **The stones.** Nobody walks between the standing stones in a fog, and a shepherd brings his flock in off the clifftops at the first sight of a gray bank.
- **The graves.** Nobody clears a passage grave of its [[being-lrgspdr|spider]], because the spider keeps the door. A family that must go in, to carry a body or to leave something for its own old ones, goes with a smoking torch of green wood and leaves a dead fish at the entrance on the way out.
- **The cold man.** A man found on the shore at dawn, cold and weak, is put by the fire and made to talk, and kept awake till noon, because a man the fog has touched who sleeps cold sometimes does not wake.

## The Tellers and the Roll

Every harbor of the coast has a teller, and the teller keeps the harbor's roll: the name of every one of its people the sea has taken, the boat each was lost from, and the year, said from memory and never written in the old harbors, though [[place-ulhera|Ulhera]]'s has been copied into a book. Tellers are nearly always women, most often widows, and a teller learns the roll by heart from the teller before her across years of standing beside her on the [Night of Lanterns]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}. When the old teller dies, the tellers of the two neighboring harbors hear the new one say the whole roll through, and if she says it without fault they confirm her.

A teller's work is to say the names. When the sea gives a body back, the grave-keeper carries it up to the passage grave, and the teller stands at the door and says the name aloud, so that the old ones know who is coming in. When a boat does not come home, the teller holds its crew on the roll as out for a year and a day, and at the next Night of Lanterns after that she calls them in: she says their names at the grave door among the drowned, and from that night the coast counts them dead.

**The calling-in has the force of law.** The courts of the [[affiliation-kngdmglvrn|Kingdom of Galvaren]] accept a teller's calling-in as proof of death, and nothing else on the coast serves, because the sea leaves no other proof. Until a man is called in his wife is not a widow and cannot remarry, his share in the boat and the curing house is held, and his heir inherits nothing. A teller who calls a crew in too soon, or holds one out too long, is making a decision about property, and every family on the roll knows it.

The tellers answer to nobody but one another. Where two harbors dispute a name, which harbor's roll a man belongs to, or whether he drowned or ran, the [[place-ezkoba|Ezkoba]] teller hears both and settles it, and the coast does what she says. Her post is the only seniority the road has.

## The Night of Lanterns

On the night before the [Feast of Thresholds]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, when houses all over [[place-tarvenirgn|Tarvénia]] fill their threshold lanterns for their dead, the coast sets its lamps on the sea walls and the harbor quays instead, one for each house, until every harbor is a line of fire along the water. At midnight each teller goes up to her harbor's passage grave with the families behind her, and at the door she says the roll from the first name to the last, the oldest drowned first and the year's drowned at the end. Then she calls in the boats that have been out a year and a day.

A family answers each of its own names with the name of the boat that drowned him, so the roll and the answers together give the whole harbor's losses back to it in one night. A house is answered for back four and five generations, so the night gives every family its whole history at sea, told aloud in front of its neighbors. Ezkoba's teller says the roll of the Black Cliffs from the top of the Long Lamp, and the boats of three harbors lie off the cove in the dark to hear it.

## The Faiths and the Road

The Drowned Road has no temple, no priest and no god, and it claims no quarrel with the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]]. The priests of the pantheon tolerate it with varying patience. The [[affiliation-brazemas|Faith of Brazemas]] gets on with it best, because its priests already bless the fleet with lamps and the coast sees no difference between their lamp and its own; the teller stands beside the priest at the spring blessing in most harbors. The [[affiliation-selmoros|Faith of Sélmoros]] objects most, because a body carried into a passage grave without its rites is, to Sélmoros's priests, a soul left unattended, and some of them preach that the road keeps the drowned in the sea by refusing to let them go. On the coast itself they keep that sermon short.

The crown's quarrel with the road is about property. What the sea brings ashore from a wreck is the king's by law, unless it can be shown to belong to the living, and the teller's roll is what shows it. A crown officer who wants a wreck counts its crew drowned the moment it strikes. A family that wants its man's goods holds him out on the roll.

## Who Belongs and Who Is Put Out

Nobody joins the Drowned Road. A child born on the coast is on it from birth, as one of the shore kin whose house sets a lamp and whose harbor will answer for it, and an incomer belongs once he has a house with a seaward window and a lamp in it. The tellers are confirmed by one another, and the grave-keepers are chosen by their parishes.

The road has one sin, and its name is the wrecker's. A man who shows a false light to bring a boat onto the rocks, or who strips the drowned before the teller has said their names, is struck from the road at the next Night of Lanterns: the teller says his name at the grave door as one the road will never carry in. No house sets a lamp for him, no teller will say his name again, and when he dies no passage grave on the coast takes his body. The coast holds that he wanders the fog forever.
