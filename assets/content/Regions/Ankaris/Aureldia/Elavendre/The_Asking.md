**The Asking** is a [[lore-flksinale|Sinalëan]] song held in [[place-elavendre|Élavendre]]. The
[[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan]] Tradition Keepers hold it with the tribal songs and count it, with
[[lore-seventowers|The Seven Towers]], among the songs first heard at [[place-aelirosse|Aelirossë]]
and carried out of the [[place-silvrfrsts|Silver Forests]] by people to whom it was given freely; the
[[affiliation-bardicolgs|Bardic Colleges]] sing it to the harp in nine turnings, each named for what
stands out in it. The song is the Sinalë's own account of the [[lore-pelwarpepl|Pelwar]] band that came
over the eastern pass into the interior, of the herdsman Dhárgan who sat four days at the wood's edge and
asked, and of the generations after him: the Pact sung at the lowest pool of [[place-longstair|the Long
Stair]], the watch set at the fog-hollow, the chief made Kinless at [[lore-tenaspr|T'éna]]'s fire in the
Black Snow year, the fires on the pass, and the giving-over at the stone court of
[[place-ethalosse|Ethalossë]].

The Dhárgandi hold that the Dhárgan of the song is the founder of their line, and the Kháldendi are the
line the song calls cold-handed. The Sinalë have confirmed neither claim and corrected neither, and the
Tradition Keepers sing the song as it was received, adding nothing. The Sinalë names in it, for the wood,
the pool, the clearing, the fog-hollow and the epithet the song gives Dhárgan at its close, are sung as
the Sinalë say them, and are the only Sinalë words most Áelendan ever speak.

: I. The Last Trees {#asking-1}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
A child comes up the hill at night
  and leaves a loaf beneath the birch.
  She does not wait. She does not search
the dark for us. We stand in sight.

We watched her mother do the same,
  and hers, and hers, back to a man
  who sat here once. His face began
again in hers. You keep his name.

We do not change. They do. This girl
  will be a grandmother, then dead,
  before the birch that takes her bread
is old. That is, for them, the world.

Your kind were in the world when we
  first came to it. You hunted. You
  had fire, and words that changed, and few
of you. We watched. You did not see.

The birch you hunted under then
  has seeded, fallen, and come back
  more times than you have kept the track
of grandsons. We were here since then.

And we were fewer than before.
  We had a grief we do not sing.
  We had grown careful. Anything
that came, we watched. We shut the door.

You ask us how it all began.
  With fire—not ours—a long way east,
  a people driven west. The least
of them, a herdsman, led the clan.

He kept the sheep, and nothing more,
  and never led. He led you here.
  We watched him come, that first dark year,
and did not open any door.
```

: II. The Road {#asking-2}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
For lives on end your fathers' line
  was pushed west, camp by camp, by men
  who came out of the east. And then
his turn. The last of all that line.

Far east, past every hill you know,
  the grass ran on and met the sky.
  Your flocks were on it. Then the cry
went up one night: the fires. The foe.

They came out of the east at night,
  for grass, for sheep, and then for men.
  Your chiefs were dead by morning. Then
a herdsman stood up in the light.

He swore no oath. He said, "Go west.
  Take children, and the seed. No more."
  That was Dhárgan. The night before
he'd been a herdsman like the rest.

The river first. It was in spate.
  No boats. You roped the sheep. You roped
  the children to the men. You hoped.
Then in. Some came out. Some came late.

Then Dhárgan went back in for one
  more child, and got her out. The brown
  wild water took his wife. Gone down.
He stood. And all the rest went on.

Of her, that is the whole that's told.
  We keep the faces we have seen,
  and hers has never come. That's been
a hole, since then, in what we hold.

The plain was flat and long. The dead
  ewes fed you for a while. And then
  the men looked at the seed. The men
were hungry. "Not that," Dhárgan said.

He sat down on the sacks. One drew
  a knife. And Dhárgan did not rise.
  He said, "Eat this, and no one dies
tonight. We walk, then, till we do.

There is no field without the seed.
  Eat sheep. Eat dogs. Eat me. Not that."
  The knife went in its sheath. He sat
down with the rest. And you agreed.

You ate the dogs. The mountains came
  up out of what had passed for cloud
  all summer—stone, and snow—and loud
with wind. You went up all the same.

The pass was high. The snow came cold.
  Some sat to rest. They sit there yet.
  Néla, his daughter, would not let
the lamb go from her coat's warm fold.

And every ewe you have, you say,
  comes down from that one lamb. At dawn
  you stood up on the pass, and on
the far side saw a wood, all grey.

And in the wood there moved a light
  that was not fire, and was not sun.
  You all went down to it, each one,
for there was nowhere else that night.

Below the pass, where trees grow thin,
  a court of worked stone stood alone,
  grass in its cracks, older than stone
should be. You looked. And none went in.

That much is yours. But we were near.
  We stood among the last trees, still,
  and watched you come down off the hill
half-dead with cold. We stayed in here.

We were not friends. Not then. That took
  a lost boy, and a winter's sleet,
  and one man who would sit, not eat,
four days outside, till we would look.
```

: III. The Silver Wood {#asking-3}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
That night you stopped outside, and lit
  a fire. Beyond the fire, the bark
  took starlight, up into the dark,
and all the hillside shone with it.

Your shaman, Sîlor, sang. The wood
  did not reply. His songs were made
  for wolf and rain and river, laid
on grass-land. Here they did no good.

What lived in here was not the kind
  of thing his craft could name or feed.
  The forms he had were made for need
on open ground. He sang them blind.

The lights came down to you. A boy
  got up and followed them, the way
  you follow things at ten: to play.
The wood closed up behind the boy.

His mother ran in after. Round
  and round she went in it all night.
  It put her out at the first light,
outside the trees, on the cold ground.

He came out at the next full moon,
  no thinner, and the same. He thought
  he had been gone a breath. He sought
his fire, and said, "It's cold. So soon?"

He said the lights had led him in
  to where one tree stood in a clear
  round place. It spoke. He did not hear
a question in it. Just: "Begin."

That is the wood. It meant no ill.
  It meant no good. It is not kind,
  nor cruel. It has, in parts, a mind.
It moves, and it is standing still.

One man cut birch to feed the fire.
  His hands went numb, and stayed that way.
  He lived. He had sons. To this day
that line has cold hands, son and sire.

Then Dhárgan went and knelt in snow
  before the first tree. Sîlor said,
  "We do not kneel." He turned his head.
"Then what? You do not know this." "No."

We heard him. These were all his words:
  "You in the wood, whatever's there,
  the road has used us up. We bear
nothing but children, seed. No herds.

Take me, and leave them, and we'll serve
  you, every year we live. Say how,
  and we will do it, here, and now.
Believe it, and we will not swerve."

The wood said nothing. Nor did we.
  The prayer was wrong. Your Way does not
  beg favors, and he knew it. What
he did, he meant. And we could see.

He meant it more than most of you
  have meant it since. And then you made
  a ring of stones outside, and laid
bread, milk, and blood: the plain's old due.

It had not asked. One night, alone,
  he came down to the pool. And there
  was one of us: Lauva, whose care
is water, standing on a stone.

He took her for the wood. He knelt,
  and set down bread before her feet.
  She went away. She did not eat.
The wood went still. That's what he felt.

He took the stillness for a yes.
  He told it at the fire that way,
  and so you sang it. We will say
She went. That's all. No more, no less.

She ate the bread when he had gone,
  and said that it was well made. Then
  she came back in and told us. When
she'd done, we talked. The talk went on.
```

: IV. The Watchers {#asking-4}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
You did not ask. We did not say.
  We had seen wonder in your kind
  before, and watched it fall behind
the next bright thing, and pass away.

Haulera, eldest of us, said,
  "I knew halls once." She stopped. "These came
  here pushed. A pushed thing does the same
to what is next. And we're ahead.

Turn all the paths. Not round. Away,
  and to the sea. The land runs out
  a few days on. They'll farm. No doubt
they'll fish. They need not come our way.

They'll come to it for what they lack,
  and kneel, and we'll be what they kneel
  to, and we'll feed them, and they'll feel
fed, and kneel more. There's no way back."

Then Lauva spoke, who keeps the pool.
  "He came down there, and knelt, and set
  down bread, and asked for nothing. Yet
you say they'll beg. He broke your rule.

He had not got much. What he'd got
  he gave: himself. They're here. They'll stay
  by us, Haulera, either way.
The sea, you say? And after, what?"

Haulera: "Yes." And Lauva: "No."
  The rest of us said neither. We
  would not drive you off to the sea,
nor let you in, nor let you go.

We turned the paths in rings. The deer
  went in to where you could not go.
  You hunted nothing in the snow.
You starved. You call it the Lean Year.

A girl died in your camp. Her bones
  went under stone. Her brothers were
  the first to take up fire for her,
and go in with it, past the stones.

They came with fire to drive the deer
  out to the spears. The brands went out.
  The wood turned them. They went about
in rings till dark. And we were near.

One of them walked into a fog
  that sat in a low hollow, white,
  calling the deer. He called all night.
The fog kept him. No man, no dog.

You did not know that hollow yet,
  nor what it was. You'd learn. The cost
  was Dránketh, Ánsel's brother. Lost.
Not dead. Not back. No trace. No debt.

One sat down in the snow, not old,
  and laid his head down. Lauva stood
  to go to him. Haulera would
not have it, and her hand took hold.

"Go out to him, and they will come
  for what you brought, and come again,
  and come again. We'll be, by then,
the hand that feeds them. That's the sum.

They'll kneel to that, and stay the same,
  and never rise." And Lauva stayed.
  The young one in the snow had laid
his head down. Then a voice. His name.

A man was calling for his son.
  He came up with no fire, no spear,
  his two hands open, calling clear,
"Ánsel!" The snow had Dhárgan's son.

It was Haulera's choice. She could
  have let the paths stay turned. She let
  them straighten. No one spoke. We set
no word to it. We understood.

He met no turning. He went in
  and got the young one up, and out,
  and all the rest. He did not shout
or thank. He stopped where trees grow thin.

He turned to the dark trees, and spoke:
  "I will come back. With no cup, no
  fire, spear, nor bread. Alone." And so
he went back down into the smoke.

Haulera stood a long time there.
  Then: "Let him. We will see what's meant."
  And that was all. And so we went
back in. We left the paths laid bare.
```

: V. The Asking {#asking-5}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
He came at dusk next day, and sat
  down at the edge, before the trees,
  his two hands open on his knees.
He did not speak. He sat like that.

He sat all night. He sat all day.
  The second night the sleet came. He
  sat on, in it, where we could see,
and did not eat. He did not pray.

The paths were straight. He did not know.
  Had he stood up and walked in, we
  would have been there for him to see.
He sat. He had said: called, or no.

The fourth dawn came. By then, the sun
  would find him dead, or find us there.
  Haulera went out, did not bear
a word, nor food. She was the one.

She said, "Why do you sit?" He said,
  "I herded. What is driven goes
  where it is driven. What one knows
by voice comes when it's called, instead.

We have been driven a long way.
  I'd rather be called in, or not
  come in at all." She said, "To what?"
"To keep it," he said, in the grey.

"We've seed. We do not want its food.
  Say what it needs. Say what it will
  not bear. Say what to leave be. Till
I know those, I'll sit here. For good."

He asked us well. And so we said
  what we had not been asked before
  by any of your kind: the lore.
And Dhárgan took it in his head.

Haulera gave him there a name
  in our own tongue, for how he'd sat.
  No man has heard it. Not yet. That
is at the end. Then it's your claim.

"The work is this," she said. "You keep
  the wood. You learn it: tree, path, pool.
  You tend what grows. You learn the rule
of each thing in it, as with sheep.

And there are places where it's thin
  between the worlds. Things cross. You stand
  at those, and turn them back by hand,
or song, or fire. Or they come in.

This wood is Lonvu." "Lonpu." "No.
  Lonvu." He could not make the sound.
  His children's children got it round.
His mouth could not. We let it go.

"The pool where I knelt down?" he said.
  "Mallonna is the pool. It's thin,
  and Lauva keeps it. Don't go in.
What's under it has limbs, no head."

"The tree that talked?" "Tehy. Go there
  seldom, with cause, and ask one thing.
  It answers true. But what you bring
away is to some other care.

The boy asked none. It said, 'Begin.'
  It meant a thing. We don't know what.
  He'll find it, or his sons. Do not
go there for play. Don't send him in."

"And something takes our hens." "The fox.
  He takes. You argue down his share
  each time." "A tenth?" "No number there.
You argue, or you lose your flocks.

The silver birch you do not fell."
  "The man whose hands went cold?" "Yes. Not
  for hall, nor hearth, nor bier, nor cot,
nor for a field. He learned it well.

And keep all this in mouths, not stone.
  A thing in stone is kept the way
  the dead are kept. It does not stray.
It does not grow. Make it your own.

Sing it. That's how. And choose a child
  to hold it whole, and start her young,
  and let it take her. On the tongue
it keeps. It stays green there, and wild."

He got up stiff. We had not seen,
  so near, the thing your bodies do:
  four days, and he was older. You
are always older than you've been.

He went down. And the paths stayed straight
  behind him, which we had not done
  for any of your kind. And one
of us said, "He is old. We're late."
```

: VI. The Cairn {#asking-6}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
You did not ask. We did not say.
  But you did not move on. You stayed.
  Your ewes lambed twice. Your dead were laid
beneath a cairn, beside the way.

You found a valley, high and still.
  You built Eshálosha in oak
  and stone. No birch. The first hearth-smoke
went up. We watched it from the hill.

Your children found the seven falls
  one summer, in a gorge: steep, loud,
  seven pools, each dark and cold. A crowd
of them went up it, past the walls.

One boy climbed up it in the sun,
  by day, and slipped at the fourth fall.
  The pool kept him. No rope, no call,
no diving got him back. Not one.

Néla went up and sat, and asked.
  Haulera said: "That is one thing,
  seven falls, one water. In the spring
it's loudest. Here's what you are tasked.

Go to the lowest pool, at first
  light. Say no word. Leave there a thing
  you made yourself and want. You bring
the thing you'd miss. That is the worst.

No fishing past the third fall. None
  of you swims there. And no one climbs
  the Stair by day." Nine thousand times
you've asked us why. "Not in the sun."

That is the whole Haulera said,
  then, or since then. You ask each year.
  The answer's what it was: not here,
not in the sun. Go up instead.

Your shaman went, that spring, before
  the light, down to the lowest pool,
  and left the horn he'd carved. The rule:
he chose it since he'd miss it more.

And then he sang the Pact. The song
  was yours, the one you'd always sung,
  the old forms, sung in your own tongue,
our names inside it, said half wrong.

We stood apart. It was not ours
  to make, nor hear. What the land said
  back to him, he took in his head,
as Dhárgan had. That Pact is yours.

One spring he asked about the fog
  that kept his son. Haulera told:
  "Thalve. It's thin there, when the cold
goes out in spring. Take spear and dog.

Sit by it through the night, in twos.
  What comes through, turn with song, or fire,
  or stone. Some will not turn. Retire.
Sing from the hall. Some nights you lose.

Your son went in with fire, by day,
  and called it, and it heard. What keeps
  him is not dead." And Dhárgan weeps
not, in our song. He walked away.

He set a watch there that same spring:
  his sons, in twos, at night; the edge
  by day as well. The oldest pledge
you keep. It's older than your king.

Each spring he came and sat. Each year
  Haulera went to him. They'd talk
  of sheep, at first. They'd sit, or walk.
Then more. She'd come back in. We'd hear.

Haulera, who had said "the sea,"
  began, each spring, to look for him.
  We saw. We said no word to him,
nor her. His hair went white. Let be.

Then he came up with Néla, grown—
  the lamb-girl—sat her down, and made
  her sing it back; and, unafraid,
she had it all. It was her own.

She sang it as we sing it. Glad
  is not a word we use. But we
  were glad that day, and he could see
it. That was the last climb he had.

Next year his daughter brought him, laid
  him down where he had sat before.
  He could not sit up any more.
Haulera knelt by him, and stayed.

We do not kneel. She knelt. And he
  said, "Come down. You have never come.
  Come down, with me, to where I'm from."
She stood. She said, "Yes." So did we.

We went down to the fire. The small
  ones stared at us. The grown ones knelt.
  He said, "Do not." And what we felt
we do not say. He'd said it all.

He died before the dawn. We'd not
  held, ever, in our hands, a hand
  that ended while we held it. And
yours end. Ours seldom. That's your lot.

You laid him under stone. A tree
  came up through it—a birch. It's old
  now, silver, tall. No one is bold
enough to cut it. Nor would we.

We go there sometimes, as to no
  place else of yours. We keep his face.
  You keep his name. But that stone place
holds stones. Not him. Where, we don't know.

His Néla kept the lore, grew old,
  and gave it to a child, and died.
  Tála, that child, sat down outside
the trees and sang, as she'd been told.

She had one thing in it we had
  not said: the fox's share was more,
  in her song, than we'd said before.
The fox had heard it. He was glad.

Your flocks have paid for that since then.
  We did not mend it. A thing sung
  goes wrong at times, and on the tongue
stays wrong. You keep it. Keep it, then.

That year the lamb-line and the line
  with the cold hands fell out, for good,
  about a pasture. And the wood
was not in it. You buried nine.

You did not ask. We did not say.
  Tála's grandson came up. He sat
  where Dhárgan had. And he had that
same face, the first one's, gone half grey.

"I'm Ghálmir, of his line," he said.
  Haulera looked at him, and saw
  the first one's face, the same set jaw,
and, under stone, the first one, dead.

He had the face. We would find out,
  in time, what else he had. And we,
  who'd kept the first one's face, could see
nothing in it to make us doubt.
```

: VII. The Felled Birch {#asking-7}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
Ghálmir was chief the Black Snow year:
  snow early, late, and deep. It killed
  the lambs inside the ewes. It filled
the cairns. Fire was what kept you here.

The oak ran out. The ash ran out.
  Ghálmir looked at the silver trees
  around the Lodge, and said, "Cut these."
You let him do it. None spoke out.

He pulled the watch off Thalve, too.
  He needed hands to haul and split.
  "One spring," he said. "The fog can sit
unwatched one spring." You thought so, too.

The birch came down—a stand—at dark.
  The hands that swung went cold. And when
  the stars came out that night, and then
each night, no starlight lit the bark.

And Thalve went unwatched. In spring
  a thing came through it, made of fog,
  stag-shaped, with no eyes. First a dog
died at the Lodge. Then anything.

It walked at night. It took the young:
  three children, and the man who tried
  to drive it off with fire. He died
in fog, the wrong song on his tongue.

The song for it was in the lore.
  Môgha, his sister, had it whole.
  No one had asked her. That's the hole
it came in by. Not fog. A door.

You did not wait for us. The hall
  met. T'éna's fire was lit. You made
  Ghálmir Kinless: no hearth, no shade,
no water, name, nor friend. That's all.

He walked into the wood alone.
  We did not turn the paths. They turned.
  What he met in there, he had earned.
What that was, we keep. It's our own.

Then Môgha brought you up. You sat
  outside the trees—the young, the old,
  hands bare—as he had, in the cold,
three days. And no one begged. You sat.

Haulera went to her. "Why do
  you sit?" And Môgha said, "We broke
  it. We cut birch. We left the smoke
on Thalve. We did not ask you.

We have come back to ask. Not for
  the trees. They're gone. We ask to keep
  the wood again: the watch, the deep
pool, fog. We'll fail, and ask once more."

Haulera stood a long time there.
  She knew the cost: not this once. Each
  time after, when a face would reach
the edge again and sit, hands bare.

She said, "Keep it." No more. And we
  went with you, once, to Thalve. In
  the fog, you sang. We sang. The thin
place took it back. We let you be.

The silver came back to the bark,
  but slowly: four lives. And the ones
  who'd swung the axes, and their sons,
were dead before it lit the dark.

You asked. We said yes. We've not told
  the cost of it, nor that it would
  again, each time. We never could
say no to you. The why we hold.
```

: VIII. Fires on the Pass {#asking-8}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
One spring: fires on the pass, at night.
  Nélgan, you call it now: Snow-Gate.
  The folk who'd pushed your fathers. Late,
but come. For grass. For sheep. To fight.

His name was Dûlthân, the Deep Dark.
  Your mothers say that name to make
  a child lie still. He'd burned, to take
the grass, two valleys to the bark.

A Dhárgan led you then. The name,
  the face. He stood you at the trees,
  spears up. He did not send, past these,
the children in. Alone, he came.

His hands were bare. He said, "Take in
  these children. Keep them. We will stay
  outside, and keep the fire away
from you. We don't ask to come in."

Haulera heard. This was the task
  she'd set you, lives back, and not known:
  with fire behind you, and alone,
to drive, or ask? He came and asked.

She turned the whole wood, for your sake—
  the thing she'd once refused to do.
  The paths ran straight, but just for you.
For them: rings, till the light should break.

The children went in. And the wood
  gave back no sound. Inside, they heard
  nothing of you: no shout, no word.
And that was worse than fire. They stood.

They came at dusk. They came with brands.
  You held the edge. The first spears flew.
  The brands went over you, and through,
into the birch. Into the stands.

The birch caught. One. And then the stand.
  We watched Haulera. She looked long.
  We do not fight. We sing. No song
puts out a fire. She raised her hand.

She walked out through the fire, and we
  came after her, all, into sight—
  out of the wood, into the light
of their own fire, for them to see.

They had not seen us. So they stopped.
  No man had, all of us, by flame,
  at once. Half ran. The rest, the same
as stones, stood. And the brands were dropped.

Dûlthân stayed. One brand. He threw
  it at the nearest tree. It caught.
  Dhárgan went up to it, and brought
it out, bare-handed. It burned through.

Then Dûlthân's spear. It took him in
  the side. He stood. He held the brand
  until it died, in his burned hand.
Then he sat down, where trees grow thin.

Haulera looked at Dûlthân. That
  was all she did. He turned. He ran.
  His men ran after him. No man
came back that year. Nor since. Mind that.

You counted in the morning sun.
  He lay there, speared, but breathing. Then
  the children came. And we came, when
they came, out of the trees. Each one.
```

: IX. The Giving-Over {#asking-9}

```poetry {form=lay meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=ABBA lang=en}
You had not seen us in the light.
  We are, by day, too tall, too still,
  and not the gods you'd made. And, ill
as he was, Dhárgan sat upright.

A court of worked stone stands up where
  the last trees thin toward the pass.
  It's older than the trees. The grass
grows in its cracks. We laid him there.

Ethalossë, we call it. You
  say it well now. The first ones said
  it badly; so did theirs. It spread,
mouth to mouth, lives, till it came true.

Haulera spoke, so all could hear—
  a thing we do not do. You'd stood
  all night, your backs set to the wood,
and asked, not driven. She came near.

"You have not earned this wood," she said.
  "You can't. Nobody earns a wood.
  You broke it. You'll break more. You would
still come back up, each time, hands spread.

That is the thing we give it for:
  not that you've kept it. You have not.
  But that you want it. That you've got
no deeper want than that. No more.

This wood was never ours. We give
  what is not ours to give. That's how
  a thing like this is given. Now
it's yours to keep. Go on. And live.

You'll plant, and never see them old,
  nor will your children. But we will,
  and see your face beneath them, still,
on someone else. And that's the cold.

The night is ours. We've had the night
  since there was night. Yours is the day.
  The wood wakes up to you. We stay
its dusk. And you be its first light."

That is the day that you were named:
  first light, in your own tongue. And when
  you say it, we are back there, then,
in daylight, with the birch-fire tamed.

And Dhárgan, speared, sat up. He could
  sit. He said back, "To keep it." Then
  he lay back down. And that was when
it passed: no gift, no vow. For good.

You stood inside it as a child
  stands in a coat cut large, and grew
  into it, lives on end. We knew
no plan for that. Haulera smiled.

That day you set the watch again:
  his sons, and theirs, and theirs. You call
  them Wardens now. They walk the wall
that is not there. We went in then.

That was a long time since, for you,
  and not for us. The cairn-birch fell
  and seeded, and came up as well,
and fell, and came again, and grew.

You came up, asked, and kept, and broke
  the keeping, and came back, and died.
  Your children came, the face, the stride,
and asked. And every time, we spoke.

Each one of you we came to know
  we knew the end of first. You keep
  dogs, so you know. It is not deep.
It is just long. It does not show.

The trees you plant, we see grow old.
  You don't. Your faces come back, on
  others, beneath them, when you're gone.
Some we now know by name, and hold.

And here's a name we've kept, unheard
  by any of you, since the spring
  he sat. It's his, in our tongue. Bring
it down with you. Here is the word.

"Vilmë," it is—the one who sat.
  Say it to children by the hearth,
  and at his cairn, and on the earth
he's under. It is his. Keep that.

You do not ask. We do not say.
  That is a keeping, too. And you
  have held it, lives on end. We, too,
hold you, among what we don't say.

And some of you leave bread. We've said
  we are not gods. You know. You leave
  it anyway. And we receive
it anyway. It is good bread.
```

## The names in the song

The song keeps the Áelendan names as the Áelendan say them and the Sinalë names as the Sinalë say them.

| Name         | Who or what                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Dhárgan**  | The herdsman who led the migration, sat four days at the wood's edge and died old at Eshálosha; later his descendant, chief at the fires on the pass |
| **Néla**     | Dhárgan's daughter, the lamb over Nélgan, who made the asking at the Stair and was the first Tradition Keeper after him                              |
| **Sîlor**    | The band's shaman, whose grass-country forms fail on the fae, who makes the Pact at the lowest pool                                                  |
| **Ánsel**    | Dhárgan's son, who sat down in the snow and was called out                                                                                           |
| **Dránketh** | Dhárgan's other son, kept by the fog at Thalve in the Lean Year                                                                                      |
| **Tála**     | Néla's grandchild, who enlarged the fox's share in the song                                                                                          |
| **Ghálmir**  | The chief of the Black Snow year, who felled the stand and pulled the watch, and was made Kinless                                                    |
| **Môgha**    | Ghálmir's sister, a Tradition Keeper, who led the return                                                                                             |
| **Dûlthân**  | The raiders' chief as the Áelendan name him, a name mothers use to still a child                                                                     |
| **Nélgan**   | The eastern pass, also called the Snow-Gate                                                                                                          |
| **Haulera**  | The eldest of the Sinalë of Lonvu, she who said "the sea", once a guest in the makers' halls                                                         |
| **Lauva**    | The Sinalë whose care is the pool, who ate the bread                                                                                                 |
| **Vilmë**    | Dhárgan's epithet in the Sinalë tongue, "the one who sat", withheld until the song's close                                                           |
| **Lonvu**    | The Silver Forests, as the Sinalë name them                                                                                                          |
| **Mallonna** | The deep pool Lauva keeps, a thin place in water                                                                                                     |
| **Tehy**     | The clearing round the one tree                                                                                                                      |
| **Thalve**   | The fog-hollow thin place at the wood's edge                                                                                                         |
