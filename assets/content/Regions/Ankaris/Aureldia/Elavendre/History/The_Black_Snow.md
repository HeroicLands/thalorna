The Áelendan will cut an oak, an ash, a pine or an alder, and they do not cut a silver birch that is standing. The reason is a chief's mistake, and the people do not tell it as a story about him. They tell it as a story about the winter.

## The Winter

Snow came early that year and late, and it was deep. It killed the lambs inside the ewes and filled the cairns, and fire was the one thing that kept the people at [[place-eshalshldg|Ésalosa]]. The oak ran out, then the ash. The chief was **Ghálmir**, Tála's grandson, who bore the face of the first Dhárgan, and he looked at the silver birch standing above the Lodge and said that it should be cut. The people let him. Nobody spoke against it.

He also took the watch off the fog-hollow, for he needed hands to haul and split. The fog could sit unwatched for one spring, he said, and the people thought so too. The birch came down in a stand at dusk, and the hands that swung the axes went cold. After that night no starlight lit the bark of the trees that remained.

## The Thing in the Fog

In spring something came through the unwatched hollow. It was made of fog and shaped like a stag and had no eyes. The first thing to die at the Lodge was a dog. It walked at night, and it took three children, and then a man who went at it with fire and the wrong song on his tongue and died in the fog.

The right song was in the lore, and it was held whole by the chief's sister, **Môgha**, a Tradition Keeper. Nobody had asked her. The Áelendan say that this was how the thing came in: not through the fog but through a door the people had left open themselves.

## Kinless

The hall did not wait for the Sinalë. It met at the hearth of [[lore-tenaspr|T'éna]] and made Ghálmir Kinless: no hearth, no shade, no water, no name and no friend. He walked into the wood alone. The Sinalë did not turn the paths against him; they turned of themselves. What he met in there he had earned, the Sinalë say, and they keep what it was.

## The Return

Môgha brought the people up to the wood's edge in the cold, the young and the old with their hands bare, and they sat three days as Dhárgan had sat, and nobody begged. Haulera went out to her and asked why they sat. Môgha said that they had broken it, that they had cut birch and left the smoke on the hollow and not asked, and that they had come back to ask. They did not ask for the trees, which were gone. They asked to keep the wood again: the watch, the deep pool, the fog. They said they would fail, and ask once more.

Haulera stood a long time. She saw the cost: it would not be this once, since each time after, a face would come to the edge and sit with its hands bare. She said, "Keep it," and nothing more. The Sinalë went with the people once to the hollow, and in the fog they sang together, and the thin place took the thing back.

The silver came back to the bark of the birch that were left, slowly, over four lives. The men who had swung the axes and their sons were dead before it lit the dark.

## What It Left

- **The birch law.** No standing silver birch is felled in Áelendan country, ever, by anyone, for any reason, and the village of [[place-sourimael|Sóurimel]] inside the Silver Forests keeps it as strictly as the tribes do.
- **The Kinless.** The sentence of Kinlessness is passed by a tribe's hall to this day. It is the worst the Áelendan have.
- **The Felled Stand.** The stumps of the cut birch stand in a ring at the margin of the Silver Forests nearest the Lodge, and the young Wardens are brought to see it ([[place-silvrfrsts|The Silver Forests]]).
- **The pattern.** Failure, return and asking, and a yes that does not name its price, is the shape of every Áelendan telling after this one.
