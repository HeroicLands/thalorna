Where the envoys' road to [[place-eshalshldg|Eshálosha Lodge]] skirts the outer margin of the [[place-silvrfrsts|Silver Forests]], a long ring of standing stones runs through the trees on the uphill side of the road. The stones are waist-high, grey and grown with moss, and each is cut on its outward face with the same mark, a closed line with a single gap. Some are banded round the top with silver. The ring is too wide to see across: it encloses a fold of silver-barked wood, a stream, and a clearing at its heart that the moon finds when it finds nothing else. The [[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan]] call it _Pálmirno_, a ring of standing stones, and the [[affiliation-alndnwrdns|Wardens]] call it the **Hunter's Ring** when they speak of it to anyone else.

## The Hunter

The ring holds the ground of a fae being the Wardens name only _súldinno_, a hunter in the wood. It is not one of the seated spirits of the forest, and it does not ask, bargain or take a toll. It hunts. Within the stones it may take whatever walks there, and outside them it may take nothing at all, and it keeps that rule exactly.

Nobody living has seen it plainly. Those who have come close describe a tall shape among the trunks at the edge of the eye, the sound of footsteps that keep pace with their own and stop when they stop, and a voice from above that belongs to someone they know. It takes one at a time, and it takes the one who is alone: the man who steps aside to drink, the sentry at the far side of the fire, the sleeper behind a barred door. What it takes it carries to the clearing and hangs above the ground in woven branches, and some of what hangs there is still alive. Among the roots below lie the bones of what it took before.

It wears what it takes. A hunter caught in the ring is seen again, walking at dusk in his own cloak and calling his companions by name, and a companion who goes to him is the next one hanging.

## The Stones and the Lodge

The stones are the bound of its ground. A mark-stone standing upright closes the line on either side of it, and the space within arm's reach of an upright stone lies outside the hunt. The Wardens' word for such a bound is _thîruthrima_, the bound of a hallowed place, and a Warden teaching the young says it as a rule: nothing walks alone in the ring, and nothing runs from it but toward a standing stone.

Who set the stones is not told. The Wardens' songs say only that they were set when the hunter was given its ground, and they do not say by whom or to what end. At the ring's lower edge, by the road, stands a long timber lodge with a stone hearth, built for the line of _thîdharand_, the keepers of the boundary marks, who walked the ring each season, renewed the cut marks and kept the silver bright. The line ended, and the lodge stands empty, as the Áelendan say of a lodge whose kin are gone. The keepers carved the inside of its walls with the ring: every stone in its place, each with its mark, and the clearing at the centre drawn as a tree with figures in it.

The Wardens walk the ring's outside each season and do not go in. Lowland poachers, who know nothing of the stones, use the lodge as a hunting camp, since the ring holds deer that nobody else hunts.

## When the Stones Fall

A fallen stone opens the line. While the ring is whole the hunter's ground is the fold inside it and no more. When stones lie overturned the ground has no edge, and the wood near the ring begins to fold toward it: a path walked out of the ring comes back to it, a blaze cut on a tree turns up ahead of the walker, and every way through the trees leads inward to the clearing. The hunter follows its prey outward as far as the opened line allows, and the farther the gaps run the farther it walks.

Silver is the usual reason a stone falls. The bands round the stones' heads are worth prying off, and a thief who sets his bar under one finds the stone comes up with it.

## Who Knows, and What They Say

- **The Wardens** of the Silver Forests know the ring, its rule and its songs, and keep the songs' last verses to themselves. They escort travellers along the road below it and do not let them stop.
- **Sóurimael** tells its children that the deer in the stone ring belong to someone. Its charcoal-burners leave the road a mile short of the lodge and rejoin it a mile past.
- **Poachers out of the lowland** know the lodge as a good camp with fat deer near it and a run of bad luck that nobody explains.
- **The Bardic Colleges** have a song of a man followed home from the wood by his brother's voice. It is sung without its last verse.

## Reaching It

The ring lies a day's walk along the envoys' road from the last lowland steading, inside sacred territory, on a road the Wardens watch. A party travelling the road under Warden escort passes the lodge in daylight. A party travelling without leave meets a Warden before it meets the ring.
