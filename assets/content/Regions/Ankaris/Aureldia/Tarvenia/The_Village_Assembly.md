Across [[place-tarvenirgn|Tarvénia]] the free householders of a village meet to settle what concerns them all, and they call the meeting the _dilkorla_, from _dil_ (a vote) and _korla_ (a yard): the yard of the vote. It is the oldest government a Tarvenan knows and the one he means when he says that nobody tells him what to do without asking. A matter between neighbors goes to it before it goes to the lord in the [[lore-tarvenanclt|Tarvenan]] reckoning, and a village owes a free man a voice in it.

What follows is the account a householder in a valley of [[place-asturath|Asturath]] gives of the assembly, as his father gave it to him: who stands, who speaks, how many times a thing is asked, and what a man who stood there owes afterward.

## Where and When It Meets

The assembly meets in the open, at the place it has always met: the square of a town, the yard before a shrine, the ground round a village's _goitsa_ (oath stone), the harbor wall of a fishing town. Where a village has an oath stone it meets beside it, because what it settles is said there.

It meets on its own day and when it is called. Most villages keep an assembly day after the autumn fair, when the men home from contracts are home and the harvest is counted, and another before the spring drive or the sowing. Between those days any householder may call it by going round the doors, and it meets on the next morning that is not a feast. A lord's steward may call it too, for a matter that touches the lord. Nobody calls it in the dark, and nothing said after sundown binds.

## Who Has a Voice

The voice belongs to the household, and the household's free head gives it: a householder who bears arms, keeps a holding and owes the ban. A man who holds no house speaks through the house that keeps him. A sworn man of a lord's household speaks through his lord's steward, if at all.

The household's voice does not go abroad with its man. While a man is under contract three hundred miles away, the woman who holds his holding holds his voice, and she stands in the assembly for the house. In most valleys of the mountains half the voices in a spring assembly are women's for that reason, and nobody remarks on it. The returned man takes his place again at the autumn assembly, and a returned man who has done well is given a seat at the fire as well as a voice.

The unfree and the banished have no voice. A stranger, a guest or a _palvo_ (a free man living in another man's town) may be heard if the assembly asks to hear him, and he says what he came to say and stands back.

## How a Matter Is Argued

The assembly has no officer of its own, no chair and no clerk. Whoever called it says what it is called for, and the matter is argued round until it is settled. A speaker who wants to be heard stands on whatever the village stands on: the steps of the well, the edge of a cart, the foot of the stone. The old men speak first by custom and the young men speak longest. Nobody is interrupted while he stands; a man who wants to answer waits until the speaker steps down.

A priest, a wise-woman or a lord's steward speaks as anyone else does, from the same step, and is answered the same way. In a town where the lord keeps a steward and a notary, as at [[place-durgalis|Dúrgalis]], the steward speaks for the lord and the notary writes; neither of them decides.

## How It Decides

There is no count of hands. When the argument has run its course, the one who called the assembly asks the matter aloud as a single question, and asks whether any house says no. If a house says no, the argument goes on. The matter is asked a second time, and a third. At the third asking a house that still says no may stand off: its householder steps back from the stone and out of the assembly, and says so aloud.

A matter is carried when it is asked the third time and every house that remains says nothing against it. A house that stood off is free of what the assembly decided, and it gives up its share of whatever the decision brings: the common grazing, the share of the levy's spoil, the help of its neighbors with the thing decided. Standing off is rare and long remembered, and a house that stands off twice in one generation finds its neighbors slow to come to its door.

## What Binds

What the assembly decides is said aloud at the stone, by the one who called it, in the hearing of every house that stood. That saying is what binds. Every house that stood in the assembly and did not stand off is bound, and so is every house that was called and did not come, because a house that was asked and stayed home has said nothing against it. The village keeps the decision in its memory and its witnesses, which is to say in every head that heard it. Where there is a notary he writes it down as well, and reads it aloud when it falls due.

A decision binds the village and nobody above it. The assembly can share out the common grazing, set the days of the drive, count the fields the hail took, judge a quarrel over a boundary, a strayed beast or a debt between neighbors, decide what is shared in a lean year and buy a contract in the village's name. It cannot judge its lord, bind another village or touch the lord's rights. A matter it cannot settle goes to the lord, who judges it in his hall before witnesses. A broken oath goes to the [[affiliation-ordrsldwrd|Order of the Sealed Word]].

A householder who breaks what the assembly bound him to has broken his word at the stone, and the village answers him as it answers any broken word. It begins with his neighbors' silence, goes on to his share withheld, and ends, for a thing done twice, with his name taken to the lord.

## Where It Differs

Every town keeps its assembly its own way, and the bones are the same.

- At **Dúrgalis** the free men of the town and the hill farms meet in the square under the castle as the [[affiliation-cncldrgls|Council of Dúrgalis]], the lord's steward speaks for the castle, and the castle's notary writes and seals what the assembly agrees.
- At **Thórgila** the town's meeting of its free men is the [[affiliation-cnclthrgl|Council of Thórgila]], and its one office of note is the horse-judge of the autumn fair.
- At [[place-ezkoba|Ezkoba]] the assembly is the boat-owners', and it meets on the harbor wall after the boats are in.
- At [[place-ardalis|Ardalis]] it is the miners' and the smiths', and it orders the forges on the stream.
- At [[place-nebralis|Nebralis]] the free men of the town and its country keep the [Freemen's Assembly]{.name data-type="affiliation/governmental"} beside a council the harbor houses are taking hold of.
- At [[place-tsaberis|Tsaberis]] the town's householders keep the fold and the market in an assembly of their own beside the shepherds' council and the king's Drove-Warden.

The [[affiliation-biltzarrath|Biltzarrath]] of Eskarath and the [[affiliation-consejarath|Konsezarath]] of Valthari are not village assemblies. They are the governing councils of a free territory and a free city, and they keep their own law.

## History {#history}

The assembly is older than the kingdoms, and no Tarvenan account says when it began. The valleys of Asturath kept it through the centuries when the legions held the lowlands, and in [[place-leonrik|Lyenrik]] the imperial legates let the village assemblies settle the villages' quarrels so long as the grain came in ([[lore-lowlandstaken|The Lowlands Taken]]). The councils of the free towns grew out of it, and the High Kingship itself is an election among lords who each answer, at home, to a village that asks a thing three times.
