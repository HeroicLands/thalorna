## Overview

The Sea-Law is the commercial law of [[affiliation-calypsa|Kalipsa]]: the law of ships and shares, cargoes and freight, bonds and losses, crews and wages, and the duties a master owes to the owners and the owners to the master. It has no single text. It is kept as a dense and consistent oral tradition by the city's professional arbitrators, a small caste who learn it by recitation, recite it at every hearing, and are prickly about the difference between what the Sea-Law says and what a party would like it to say.

Its verdicts are given at [[place-sealawhall|the Sea-Law Hall]] on the quay, and they bind nobody outside the city. In practice they are honored across half the northern shipping world, because a captain or a house known to defy one finds that no syndicate in Kalipsa will write its bonds and no Kalipsan factor will carry its goods.

## What It Holds

The Sea-Law is a large body of particular rules, and every one of them rests on a few principles that every Kalipsi grows up knowing.

**A ship is shares.** A hull is owned in shares, often by several houses at once, and every share carries its part of the profit, the cost and the loss. A share is sold, pledged or inherited like land, and the census of the [[affiliation-captnscncl|Captains' Council]] counts a house's shares as tonnage.

**The master answers for the voyage.** Once a hull leaves the harbor its master's word governs it, and once it returns he answers for every choice he made. A master who sails against an owner's written order bears the loss himself.

**A loss is shared by those it saved.** Cargo thrown overboard to save a hull is paid for by every owner of hull and cargo in proportion to what was saved, and the arbitrators reckon the shares.

**A bond is paid unless the loss was the master's doing.** A syndicate that writes a bond on a hull or a cargo pays its full sum on a loss at sea, unless the arbitrators find that the master caused the loss by a choice the Sea-Law forbids: sailing an unseaworthy hull, overloading it, refusing a pilot where the city requires one, or sailing it where the bond did not run.

**A guest is not robbed.** A stranger who has eaten at a Kalipsan table, or been given a berth in the harbor, is under the city's protection, and a wrong done to him is judged twice as heavily as the same wrong done to a citizen. This is the rule the city's reputation rests on.

## How It Is Kept

The arbitrators keep it. A young arbitrator learns the Sea-Law from a master arbitrator over seven years, reciting the rules and the verdicts that shaped them until he can give both back word for word, and the master arbitrators examine him before he may hear a case alone. There are rarely more than twenty arbitrators in the city at once, and they are paid by the parties to each hearing in equal shares, so that neither side pays more for the verdict.

A hearing is held before three arbitrators, each party choosing one and the two choosing the third. The parties speak, the witnesses speak, and the arbitrators recite the rules that bear on the case before they give the verdict. A verdict once given is recited ever after with the rules, and the oldest arbitrators can recite verdicts given before any house now on the Council held a seat.

Only the Captains' Council may change a rule. It does so rarely, by a vote at a sitting, and the arbitrators then recite the rule as the Council changed it. The Council has never voted to write the Sea-Law down.

## What It Does Not Do

The Sea-Law is a law of trade. Theft, violence and murder on the island are tried by the city's own courts under the Harbormaster's officers, and treason and war are the Council's. A Sea-Law hearing decides who owes what to whom.

It also has no force outside the city except reputation. An Élavendren court at [[place-sarmelis|Sarmelis]] owes it nothing, and a foreign house that refuses a verdict is answered only by the city's refusal to deal with it.
