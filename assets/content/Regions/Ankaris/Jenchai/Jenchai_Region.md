The Jénchài Region is the eastern archipelago of [[place-ankrscntnnt|Ankaris Continent]]—a ring of nine mountainous, volcanic islands separated from the [[place-tantsouregin|Tāntsou mainland]] by a treacherous strait. It is dominated politically by the sovereign empire of [[affiliation-jenchaiempr|Jénchài]], which the [[affiliation-tantsouempr|Empire of Tāntsou]] has for a thousand years refused to recognize—considering the archipelago a rebellious province rather than an independent state. The islanders return the compliment, viewing themselves as heaven's favored inheritors of the Celestial Mandate that Tāntsou, in their telling, has long since forfeited.

Where the Tāntsou Region is a continental civilization of imperial bureaucracy and ordered plains, Jénchài is its mirror-opposite: maritime, feudal, warrior-aristocratic, and shaped at every turn by the discipline of life on steep, narrow islands.

## Geography

The archipelago takes its name—"Nine Mountains"—from the nine principal islands, each dominated by a volcanic peak that serves as both geographic center and cultural emblem. The great mountains rise straight out of the sea, their shoulders terraced for rice and their summits kept for shrines. Between them, coastal plains are narrow but intensively cultivated; interior valleys hold forests of cedar, pine, and sacred bamboo.

The strait separating Jénchài from the mainland is the archipelago's single most important defensive feature. Notorious for sudden storms and shifting currents, it has repeatedly wrecked Tāntsou invasion fleets and has shaped the islanders' attitude toward the sea as something closer to reverence than mastery. Shrines to the storm-aspect of the [[affiliation-tengwouronglei|Celestial Order]] stand at every significant headland.

Beyond the Nine, scores of smaller islands and islets stretch out in braided chains toward the open ocean. Many of these lesser islands are uninhabited or only seasonally occupied by fishermen, smugglers, and hermit-priests. The volcanic activity that built the chain is still active: eruptions, hot springs, and periodic earthquakes are part of ordinary Jénchài life and are interpreted theologically as heaven's continuing attention to its favored land.

The climate is temperate with a sharp maritime character: wet summers, mild winters in the south, and harsh winters in the north where the outermost islands feel the ocean's teeth. Typhoons arrive every summer and are read as divine warnings, tests, or blessings depending on the year and the Great Lord's fortunes.

## Peoples and Culture

The Jénchài are descended from Tāntsou stock—their language, script, calendar, and caste categories still echo the mainland—but a millennium of separation has produced an unmistakably distinct people. They are, on average, harder-edged than mainland Tāntsou: a society that lives on narrow terraces between steep mountains and dangerous seas has little patience for administrative abstraction. Physical discipline, personal loyalty, and refined aesthetic practice are the virtues the culture prizes above all.

Culturally, Jénchài is a civilization of great paradox. It has produced some of Thalorna's most exquisite poetry, calligraphy, ceramics, blade-work, tea ceremony, and garden design; its retainers study these arts as spiritual practices. The same retainers, without contradiction, can order a village burned in the afternoon. This coexistence of refinement and brutality is not a tension for the Jénchài—it is the essence of what it means to have kept the Mandate while the mainland lost it.

The **caste system** inherited from Tāntsou still organizes formal society, but it has been bent around feudal reality. The warrior lords dominate completely; the [[lore-wanduo|Wánduo]] sorcerers, so central to mainland power, appear here only as subordinate retainers in individual lords' service. Personal names are granted more freely among the retainer classes than on the mainland—a mark of service rather than exceptional achievement.

The Jénchài practice **Ruìsing**, a form of ritual suicide distinct from Tāntsou's [[lore-tseising|Tséising]]. It is not merely an act of atonement but a versatile tool of communication: it can express protest, preserve a secret, demonstrate loyalty to a fallen lord, or simply assert that honor outweighs life. It is taken seriously enough that a lord ordering a subordinate's Ruìsing stakes his own reputation on the judgment.

## Religion

The islands' faith is a divergent form of the [[affiliation-tengwouronglei|Celestial Order]]. The broad pantheon—Celestial Dragon, Jade Sovereign, Minister of Storms, Minister of the Moon, the [[lore-fvlmntlrds|Five Elemental Lords]]—is recognizable, but interpretation has fragmented along regional and domainal lines. Coastal provinces elevate the storm and sea aspects; mountain provinces emphasize earth and metal; island shrines host cults specific to their locality. Each Great Lord claims some portion of heavenly favor for his own line, and the [[lore-mandatehvn|Mandate of Heaven]]—which in Tāntsou is held singularly by the Emperor—has fractured into competing local claims.

The ceremonial emperor on Jénchài serves this fragmented theology well: his role is to hold the symbolic center while the Great Lords pursue their regional pieties.

## Politics and Power

Real authority lies with a handful of **Daozhuo**—Great Lords—who rule their provinces as effectively independent fiefdoms, each with its own army, justice, taxes, and foreign policy. Below them sit the **Shuìzhuo**, lesser lords who pledge personal allegiance in exchange for land. The **Rongléng Téngzhéi** ("Son of Heaven of Ten Thousand Dawns") sits at the ceremonial apex, distributing honor but commanding nothing.

The current balance rests on three or four roughly equal Daozhuo, none willing to risk open war against a coalition of the others. Every generation or so this balance collapses into open civil war before a new equilibrium emerges. See [[affiliation-jenchaiempr|Jénchài]] for the detailed political structure.

## Trade

Jénchài exports the finest blade-work in the eastern world, along with sea-salt, dried fish, lacquer-ware, silk, ceramics, and the refined luxury goods of the Great Lords' workshops. It imports mainland jade, tea, bronze, and silver; and, through the long-distance eastern trade, it reaches the [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyaran]] coast and even (more rarely) the ports of [[place-xerathia|Xerathia]].

Each Great Lord controls trade within his own domain; the emperor does not. The result is a patchwork of harbor policies, tolls, and trading-house alliances. Merchant lineages who maintain good relations with multiple Daozhuo are among the wealthiest commoners in the archipelago, though their formal caste status keeps them socially subordinate to even the lowest retainer.

**Naval power** is Jénchài's greatest economic and military asset. Island geography forced the development of a maritime tradition the mainland never matched: Jénchài warships are faster, more maneuverable, and better-armed than their Tāntsou counterparts, and Jénchài sailors are the finest in the eastern seas. The distinction between merchant ship, warship, and pirate ship is less rigid here than in almost any other region of Thalorna.

## Relations with Neighboring Regions

The **west**, across the strait, is [[place-tantsouregin|Tāntsou]]—the rejected motherland. Raids along the Tāntsou coast are a standing institution of Jénchài policy, and the Great Lords openly claim sovereignty over coastal territories that were once part of their ancestral province. The Empire treats this as piracy and insurrection; Jénchài treats it as the rightful reclamation of lands heaven intended for them.

To the **south**, Jénchài trading ships reach [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]] and sometimes Xerathian ports, participating in the wider eastern and Vylarian commerce from a safe distance—the archipelago is far enough east that no western power has ever seriously projected military force against it.

To the **north**, the archipelago's outer islands trail off into frozen seas the Jénchài rarely cross. What lies beyond is mostly legend.
