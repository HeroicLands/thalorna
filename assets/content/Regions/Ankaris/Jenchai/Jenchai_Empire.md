Jénchài is a realm of mountainous volcanic islands east of the [[affiliation-tantsouempr|Empire of Tāntsou]], off its south-eastern coast and separated from the mainland by a treacherous strait. The islanders call their land **Tēyan Jenjai**, the Nine Mountains Above the Clouds of Heaven, and hold themselves above every other people, the western mainland first among them; in daily speech the name shortens to **Jenjai**. The Nine Mountains stand on two islands: a great island carrying eight of them, and a lesser island carrying the ninth. Once a province of the empire, Jénchài broke away over a thousand years ago in a bloody war of secession and has since developed its own fiercely independent culture. The empire still refers to Jénchài by the dismissive name **Chàiwōng**, "Kingdom of Mountains and Seas", and has never formally recognized its sovereignty, considering it a renegade province in open rebellion.

Jénchài's culture is shaped by its Tāntsou origins—the caste structures, the written script, the religious calendar, and the broad outlines of the Celestial Order all echo the mainland. But a millennium of separation has warped these shared roots into something distinctly Jénchài, and the islanders are deeply proud of what they have become.

## The Emperor of Ten Thousand Dawns

Jénchài maintains an Emperor—the **Rongléng Téngzhéi** ("Son of Heaven of Ten Thousand Dawns")—who claims an unbroken line of descent from the last imperial governor who led the secession. The Emperor performs elaborate seasonal rituals, receives foreign ambassadors in his palace above [[place-lengwou|Léngwou]], and serves as the ceremonial heart of the nation. His court is a pageant of ancient protocol and exquisite aesthetics.

In practice, the Emperor commands almost nothing. He has no standing army, controls no territory beyond the imperial capital district, and depends on the great lords for his household's upkeep. He is nineteen years old, styled **Wéiléng** in his reign, and was enthroned in 712 AF at eleven, when his father abdicated as the price of the peace that ended the War of the Empty Seat. The court's real function is as a stage for the lords to compete for prestige and proximity to divine legitimacy—being named Celestial Minister or receiving a court title carries immense social weight, even though the titles confer no actual authority. The Emperor's power lies entirely in this ability to grant or withhold honor, a currency the warrior lords value above gold.

## The Great Lords

True power in Jénchài resides with the **Daozhuo** ("Great Lords"), who each hold one of the Nine Mountains as an effectively independent fiefdom and rule it as a king rules his kingdom. Each Daozhuo maintains their own army, administers their own justice, collects their own taxes, and conducts their own foreign trade. They owe formal allegiance to the Emperor but treat this obligation as ceremonial—a matter of face rather than submission.

Below the Daozhuo sit the **Shuìzhuo** ("Lesser Lords"), minor feudal holders who pledge loyalty to a Great Lord in exchange for land and protection. This cascading web of personal oaths and obligations holds the realm together—barely. Feuds between the Great Lords are constant, ranging from trade wars and border skirmishes to full-scale campaigns that can engulf entire provinces. The Emperor occasionally attempts to mediate, but his rulings carry weight only when backed by a Great Lord willing to enforce them.

The current balance of power rests on three or four Great Lords of roughly equal strength, none willing to risk open war against a coalition of the others. This equilibrium is fragile, and every generation or so it collapses into a period of open warfare before a new balance emerges. The last collapse was the War of the Empty Seat, 709–712 AF.

Eight houses hold the eight domains on the great island and the lesser. Each domain is named for its house, and each house for a feature of its mountain:

The houses sort into three blocs. **The Strait** is Shuiwōjai, led by Shuiwōjai Henzo, and Rīnjai sworn to him; together they muster 2,600 men and 85 ships, and they want Henzo named Téng Ja. **The imperial loyalists** are Hojūjai, whose lord is the widow-regent Hojūjai Rēwei acting for her son Hojūjai Zīja, aged fourteen, and Mūwōjai; they muster 2,500 men and 20 ships, and want the office for the Hojūjai heir when he comes of age, or for nobody. **The Independents** are Wōjai, Zenjai, Wējai and Tsuijai, mustering 3,970 men and 55 ships; each has its own price, and Zenjai or Wōjai joining either bloc ends the balance.

The eight domains and the imperial district together hold the 500,000 people of the realm, assessed at 346,000 bales of rice a year, the shortfall of grain made up by fish, barley and millet.

A **bale** is rice enough to feed one adult for a year, and it is the unit of every lord's roll. Each village is assessed in bales once and pays in kind, in rice or in whatever its shore, forest or mine yields. Whoever holds the village—the lord's granary, a retainer's fief, the court or a summit shrine—takes four parts in ten, and the village keeps six. Each village also owes thirty days of labor a year on the castle, the roads and the lord's fields. Its headman answers for the whole assessment, so a shortfall is the village's debt. Each lord raises his Tsàihuo soldiers from his castle town and villages at two and a half men for every hundred bales assessed, except Zenjai, whose northern levy is four.

## The Court and Its Poverty

The Emperor does not leave the Dragon's Mountain. The imperial person may not set foot beyond **Zojai**, so the realm comes to him: every lord's envoy, every petition and every bale of rice. He can go nowhere to collect, and this is the hinge of his poverty. The imperial district is Zojai and the capital on its lowest slope, **[[place-lengwou|Léngwou]]**, the Dawn Court, a town of 12,000 below the Emperor's palace, in a district of 20,000 people assessed at 6,000 bales. Foreign ambassadors come as far as Léngwou, and the upper slope is closed to them.

The court's nominal income is 6,880 bales a year: four parts in ten of its own district's assessment, plus four parts in ten of eight **court estates**, one village in each domain whose share is owed to the Emperor. Each Daozhuo collects his court estate through his own granary and forwards what he chooses. In 719 AF the court received 4,100 bales against the 9,000 it spends, and the Retired Emperor's household in Léngwou is part of that cost. Silver from Shuiwōjai has covered the gap for three years, and the court is in his debt. The court's strongroom stands at Tsaimū in Mūwōjai ground, in the keeping of that house's Weijū.

The Emperor grants four things, and each costs him nothing:

- **The patent of the mountain.** Each Daozhuo holds his mountain by a patent the Emperor issues on every succession. The Emperor cannot enforce it; a lord without one holds by force alone and cannot call on any other lord's duty.
- **Court dignities.** Celestial Minister, Caste-Registrar and Censor of Purity are titles conferring precedence and no function. Omen-Reader and Keeper of the Sacred Texts remain working posts of the Tānchéi at court.
- **Precedence at the Dawn Rite.** The New Year rite on Zojai's summit is attended by the envoy of every lord, and its seating order is the year's political map.
- **The office of Téng Ja.**

## Téng Ja

**Téng Ja**, Heaven's Blade, is the Emperor's sword-arm: the holder of the force the Emperor has none of. The Emperor confers the office on a Daozhuo who has brought enough of the others to heel, and an Emperor who refuses a lord with the realm in his fist does not refuse long. The holder speaks for the Emperor to the other lords, keeps the peace between them by force, and receives the rice of the court estates to forward, which makes the Emperor dependent on him. The office lapses with its holder and is not inherited.

Since 709 AF it stands vacant. The last Téng Ja, Hojūjai Henwo, held it from 693 AF until his death, when his son was a child; Shuiwōjai and Wōjai pressed the court not to confer it on a regency, and the war that followed ended in the present balance. The race for the office is the realm's central contest.

## Honor and Pride

Personal honor is the organizing principle of Jénchài society, more deeply embedded even than in Tāntsou's warrior caste. A lord's word is sacred; broken oaths are remembered for generations and can poison alliances centuries later. Insults—real or perceived—demand satisfaction, and the challenge and the duel each follow a fixed form.

The Jénchài use the mainland's word for the ordered death, **Ruìsing** ("Ritual Death"), for every ritual death, ordered or chosen, and it carries a different weight on the islands. Where [[lore-tseising|Tséising]], the mainland's chosen death, is primarily an act of atonement or defiance, Ruìsing in Jénchài can express protest, preserve a secret, demonstrate loyalty to a fallen lord, or simply assert that one's honor is more precious than one's life. A lord who orders a subordinate's Ruìsing wields that power carefully, knowing it reflects on his own judgment as much as on the condemned.

## Relationship with Tāntsou

Jénchài's attitude toward the empire is a tangle of resentment, cultural debt, and fierce superiority. The islanders acknowledge their Tāntsou heritage—they could hardly deny it, given the shared script, religious framework, and caste echoes—but insist that they have purified and elevated what the mainland corrupted. They view the empire as bloated, decadent, and spiritually fallen, a civilization that lost the Mandate of Heaven centuries ago and is too proud to admit it.

The core of this belief is religious: the Jénchài hold that when their ancestors broke away, heaven itself blessed the act. The original secession is framed not as rebellion but as a divine calling—the gods withdrew their favor from the empire and bestowed it upon the Nine Mountains. The Emperor's unbroken lineage is offered as proof: where Tāntsou's imperial line has been interrupted by conquest and civil war, Jénchài's has endured. To the islanders, this is evidence that they are heaven's chosen people, and the mainland is a fallen realm.

This belief fuels both their pride and their aggression. Jénchài's superior navy raids the empire's southern coast, and the Great Lords openly claim sovereignty over coastal territories that were once part of their ancestral province. The empire views this as piracy and insurrection; Jénchài views it as the rightful reclamation of lands that heaven intended for them.

## Divergence from Tāntsou

A thousand years of separation have produced meaningful cultural differences:

**Caste system:** Jénchài inherited Tāntsou's caste structure but has bent it around its feudal reality. The warrior lords dominate completely—there is no equivalent of the Tāntsou balance between Zhazhò, Wánduo, and Wòhou. Magic-users exist but are subordinate to their lords in a way that even the Wánduo's chattel status doesn't quite capture; in Jénchài, a sorcerer is simply another retainer, not a separate pillar of power.

**Religion:** The Celestial Order is recognizable but has fractured into regional variations. Each Great Lord's domain tends to emphasize the deities that suit their territory—coastal lords venerate storm and sea aspects, mountain lords emphasize earth and metal. The Mandate of Heaven is interpreted locally: each Great Lord claims some measure of divine favor for their own line, and the summit shrine of each mountain, kept by the domain's Tānchéi, supports the claim. The Emperor's is the one claim to the whole Mandate. An eruption is read as heaven's attention, so an eruption in a disputed year is a verdict on the house beneath the mountain.

**Aesthetics and refinement:** Where Tāntsou's culture prizes cosmic harmony and bureaucratic order, Jénchài has developed an intense aesthetic sensibility—an almost spiritual devotion to beauty in craft, poetry, swordsmanship, and the rituals of daily life. Tea-craft, flower arrangement, garden design and the forging of blades are all elevated to spiritual practices, and the same lords who keep them wage war without restraint.

**Namelessness:** Jénchài adopted the Tāntsou convention of names as earned honors, but the practice has evolved. In Jénchài, warrior names are granted more freely among the lord and retainer castes—personal names are a mark of service rather than exceptional achievement. The lower castes remain unnamed, as in Tāntsou.

## Naval Power

Jénchài's greatest military advantage is its navy. Island geography forced the development of a maritime tradition that the empire, with its continental focus, has never matched. Jénchài warships are faster, more maneuverable, and better armed than their Tāntsou counterparts, and the island's sailors are among the finest in the eastern seas. The Great Lords maintain their own fleets, and naval raiding—both against the empire and against merchant shipping—is a significant source of wealth and prestige.

## Outside the Ladder

The Nòutsou are slaves and outcasts, the caste that is not a caste, whose members may hold no name the law recognizes.
