## Overview {#overview}

Evod is flat, white and enormous. From the dune crest on its southern rim the salt runs to a horizon forty miles away, so even and so bright that a rider shades his eyes at noon and cannot judge his distance to anything. Along the old shoreline, a day's ride round the rim, a chain of stepped mounds stands against the sky, each a pyramid of baked brick with its flanks eaten by wind, and between the mounds the canal banks run straight out of the dunes and straight under the salt, as if the people who dug them expected the water to be back by the next harvest.

This was the lake of [[lore-basinbldrs|the Basin Builders]], who farmed its shores for the better part of a thousand years and left when it failed, around 1700 BF. The salt is what the water left. In the [[place-hskrrgn|Ghoshiqor]], the sand range, it is the only thing worth cutting, and the tribes of the nearest wells cut it in slabs a yard long and trade it west for grain.

## Layers {#history}

[[lore-basinbldrs|The Basin Builders]] had cut their canals and raised the stepped mounds along this shore by 2600 BF. The lake fell over some five centuries from about 2200 BF, and by about 1700 BF the basin was dry and its people gone. [[lore-waterunder|The Buried Water]], the Oroqidur star-song, keeps the tribes' account of the water going down. By the time the tribes came to the sands the basin was salt. The tribes of the nearest wells have cut it since, the [[affiliation-khldrtrb|Khilodur]] on the southern rim and the younger horde of the [[affiliation-mrjlrtrb|Mirjolur]] on the western, and they have quarrelled over the western cuttings as long as either has cut there.

## What the Tribes Believe

The tribes hold that the lake did not dry up. It went down. They teach it to their children in one sentence: the water went to ground to find its people, and it will come up again where the last of them is buried. Slab-cutters will not work the middle of the flat, where they say the water is deepest below, and none will sleep out on the salt.

## Danger

The crust is the danger. Over most of the basin it holds a camel, but over the old canals and the deep center it is a skin an inch thick laid over brine and black mud, and a loaded string that crosses it breaks through one beast at a time. Mirage fills the air from mid-morning, and a rider can steer for a well that is not there; a party without a guide has walked off the flat in the wrong direction and met no water for five days. On the dunes of the old shore the Duneclaw hunts the edge of the white, striking from the lee of a dune at anything that crosses between the salt and the sand.

## Treasure

The mounds are the prize, and they are not empty. Glazed tiles in blue and green, nowhere else made in the waste, come out of the lower courses. Sealed jars of gray clay are found whole, some holding seed, some oil, and a few a black paste no one has dared taste. The salt itself is worth the labor: a slab-cutter's wage in a good season is more than a caravan guard's, and a party with a dozen camels and a week of patience can carry out a fortune in white stone.

## The Stair under the Crust {#present}

The slab-cutters work the salt as they always have, and none of them works the middle of the flat or sleeps out on it. This season a salt-cutter's son, working a canal bank half a day from the nearest cutting, put his pick through the crust where it should have been mud and broke into a dry stair going down. He covered the hole with his shirt and told his mother, who told the tribe's elders, who have told no one outside. The boy is the only person alive who can find the place again, and he is eleven. His father wants to know whether he was lying.

## Hooks {#hooks}

- **The boy who can find it.** The tribe's elders want the stair opened before anyone outside hears of it, and the only guide they have is eleven years old.
- **Lying or not.** The boy's father wants to know whether his son broke into a stair or into a story, and the boy is the only one who can show him.
