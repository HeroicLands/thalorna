For eleven months the place is a ring of black stones round a deep well, a few hundred yards of trampled gravel, and a scatter of bones. Then the spring fleet comes in from the west and the autumn fleet from the east, and in ten days the empty ground becomes a city of felt tents and rope corrals that holds fifteen thousand people, four thousand camels and every kind of silver anyone has ever struck.

**Qìso**, "the price" in [[skill-sowideslng|Sowides]], is the market-camp of the central [[place-hskrrgn|Hosikor]], about halfway across the [[place-khzryndsrtrgn|Khazryn]]. [[lore-celestialroad|The Celestial Road]] reaches it on about the sixty-fifth day out from the March, and the southern spur to the [[place-swoasisbelt|Tellumel]] leaves it here. Most goods that cross the waste change hands at Qìso instead of going on whole, and the price of water is the highest on the road.

## The Camp

The camp has no wall and no houses. Its heart is the well, the deepest on the road, lidded in stone and watched day and night by riders of the tribe that holds its water; round it, in rings, lie the tents. Western factors pitch on the west side, Tānvüri and Vedyari on the east, the Tellumi and Ātárzád houses on the south, and the furs of the north come in on the north. Tribesmen camp in the gaps. A trader finds his own people by the color of the cloth over the tent-pole.

For ten days the camp is the busiest place for a thousand miles in any direction. Silk and tea are weighed against silver, porcelain is unpacked and counted shard by shard, horses are sold by the string, and spices and medicines pass through a dozen hands before they leave. Camels that crossed from the west are sold for camels that will cross to the west. A caravan from the March sells its cargo here, loads Tānvüri silk bought from an eastern string and turns back, with its master having crossed only half the road.

## Who Keeps It

No polity does. The tribes whose ranges meet here hold a moot, the jutjen, before each season. Their chiefs, the dowek, swear a peace on the well-stone, and the peace holds for the ten days of the market. In the camp no blood is shed, no weapon is drawn, and a quarrel is carried to the moot and priced. A man who breaks the peace is killed outside the ring by the tribe he wronged, and the tribe that holds his water pays the blood-price.

The moot sells everything it can: stands, water, grazing for the beasts, the services of its riders, and its word. Its clerks keep tally with knotted cords and remember more than any ledger holds.

## Dangers

Thieves work the camp, and the moot's law is severe: a man caught stealing a camel loses his hands. Giant camel spiders feed in the refuse pits at the rim, and a careless trader walking out to relieve himself in the dark has not always come back. The two blood-fields near the road keep every sensible caravan on the track as it approaches and as it leaves.

## Hooks

- A Byzarian factor and a Tānvüri factor each believe the other has bought the entire spring supply of a single dye. Each approaches the party with a request for a quiet look at the other's baggage.
- A dowek of the moot is dying, and two of his sons are bidding against each other for the right to speak for his tribe.
- A well-rider has been found dead in the ring, and the moot must find the killer before the peace breaks and the market empties.
