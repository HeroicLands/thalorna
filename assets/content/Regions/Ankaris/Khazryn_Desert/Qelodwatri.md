Two days north of the road, where the gravel of the [[place-hskrrgn|Khoshiqor]] runs up against the foot of a black mesa, there is a ring of twelve palms around a well. The tribe that owns it, the **Küüyöyür**, "the arrow people", call it **Kelödvootri**, "the cold well", because the water drawn from it is cold at noon in high summer. It is a [[lore-ownedoasis|bödek]], owned water: the tribe's reserve for a dry year, visited in its season and empty the rest of it. A horse skull on a pole faces the approach, the tribe's sign is cut high on the oldest palm, and a flat stone lies over the well-mouth.

The Küüyöyür are a small tribe of the sand range. They graze far to the west for most of the year and send a water-warden and two riders to the well at the end of each dry season to clean the shaft, count the palms and see that no stranger has drawn.

## What Lies Beneath

The spring is older than the tribe and older than its palms. Under the well-mouth the shaft drops into a cistern cut from the rock by the builders of the [[lore-towercities|Tower Cities]], who found the spring and roofed it: a long hall of black water with ledges round it, a second drawing-basin cut into the north ledge, and an overflow channel that carries the excess out under the palms, where it waters their roots. A shutter of cut stone drops into a slot at the channel mouth to close it, worked from the ledge by a bar and a counterweight. A narrow passage of steps runs from a cleft in the mesa foot down to the ledge, so that the cistern can be cleaned without lowering a man on a rope. The wardens use it once a generation, to clear the silt from the sump.

The builders carved the walls of the passage and the face of the ledge. The carvings show figures with jars, and every figure is pouring water back into the pool before it draws. A figure with long arms lies under the ledge in every panel, and the carvings show nothing of its face.

## What the Owners Say

Asked about the stone on the well, the Küüyöyür say what every tribe says of a lidded well: a [[being-qelodud|kelödüd]] lives in it. They have said so for longer than anyone in the tribe can remember. Their grandmothers poured the first draw of every season back into the shaft before they filled a skin, and called it letting the well drink first. The habit lapsed when the stone went on, since nobody draws from a lidded shaft but the warden.

The thing in the cistern is not a kelödüd. A kelödüd has no hands, does not come up into the open where the wind blows, and does not return what it takes. What lives under Kelödvootri is a [[lore-dreadspawncrtr|dreadspawn]], and the tribe has a word for it that it does not use in front of strangers: **Bödeküd**, "the one who owns the water".

## The Bödeküd

Nobody has seen the whole of it. What has been seen is arms: pale, wet, jointed in more places than an arm should be, and longer than three men laid end to end, ending in hands with long smooth fingers that leave prints with no lines in the palm. It hangs beneath the ledges with its arms spread over the pool and its body somewhere under the stone.

Its signs are these:

- A rope that goes slack in the shaft and comes back up coiled, wet, and tied off in the knot of whoever lowered it.
- The sound of a rope wound slowly up a shaft that has no rope in it.
- Handprints round the well-ring and the palm roots, which dry in the sun to a white crust that smells of standing water.
- A tether, a lead or a cord drawn back across the sand toward water, steadily, with nothing visible at the far end.
- A beast or a man taken without a sound, and later found under the water, bound with his own rope to a stone.

It keeps what it takes. Prey is weighted under the water, or tied in the air pocket beneath its ledge, and a man taken by it may live for a day or two in the cold and the dark before he drowns. It hunts in the open only at night, by the overflow channel and the palm roots, and the dry wind of noon drives it deep. A sudden heat makes it go still, and a pool fouled with its blood is bitter for a season. It does not take from one who has poured water back into the pool before drawing, for as long as he stays at the pool, and why it keeps that courtesy is not known.

Whether the builders dug down to something that was already there, or whether a maker set it in the spring to keep the water for them, nobody can say. The tribes of the Khoshiqor say they hold the wells of the road in trust until the owners return.

## Reaching It

Kelödvootri is marked on no map and lies off every caravan track. A string blown off the road by a dust storm in the Long Dry, west of [[place-qisomrktcmp|Qiiso]], can reach it by the stars if its guide knows the mesa, and a guide who knows it has usually heard why the Küüyöyür keep a stone on it.
