Two days north of the road, where the gravel of the [[place-hskrrgn|Khoshiqor]] runs up against the foot of a black mesa, there is a ring of twelve palms around a well. The tribe that owns it, the **Kuuyoyur**, "the arrow people", call it **Kelodvootri**, "the cold well", because the water drawn from it is cold at noon in high summer. It is a [[lore-ownedoasis|bodek]], owned water: the tribe's reserve for a dry year, visited in its season and empty the rest of it. A horse skull on a pole faces the approach, and a flat stone lies over the well-mouth with a weight on it.

The tribe's sign is cut high on the oldest palm, where a camel cannot rub it out. Beneath it is an older carving, half grown over by the bark: a figure tipping a jar into a pool, and below the pool a long shape with its arms spread. The Kuuyoyur did not cut it, and they cut their own sign above it rather than across it.

:::secret {title="The older carving"}
The lower carving is an owner's mark, cut in the same sense as the tribe's sign above it: it names who owns the water. Whoever held the spring before the Kuuyoyur copied it from the builders' panels in the cistern, and the Kuuyoyur kept it out of a habit nobody now explains. On a bodek the cut palm names the owner, and this palm names two. The lower is the elder.
:::

The Kuuyoyur are a small tribe of the sand range. They graze far to the west for most of the year and send a water-warden and two riders to the well at the end of each dry season to clean the shaft, count the palms and see that no stranger has drawn. Once a generation the warden goes down to clear the silt from the bottom of the shaft, and the tribe does not say how he reaches it.

## The Palms and the Water

The ground among the palm roots stays damp through the dry season, though no channel runs on the surface. Among the roots of the palms nearest the mesa a hole about a forearm wide opens into the dark, and in the quiet before dawn water can be heard running somewhere beneath it. The palms on that side are the greenest of the twelve. At the mesa foot, a few hundred paces off, a cleft in the black rock breathes cold air at noon.

:::secret {title="The hole among the roots"}
The hole is the mouth of the cistern's overflow channel, and it is the road the thing below uses when it comes up into the open. Wet furrows a forearm wide run from it toward the well and back on any night it has been out.
:::

## What the Owners Say

Asked about the stone on the well, the Kuuyoyur say what every tribe says of a lidded well: a [[being-qelodud|kelodud]] lives in it. They have said so for longer than anyone in the tribe can remember. The tribe's grandmothers poured the first draw of every season back into the shaft before they filled a skin, and called it letting the well drink first. The habit lapsed when the stone went on, since nobody draws from a lidded shaft but the warden.

The tribes of the Khoshiqor have a saying for owned water in general: that they hold the wells of the road in trust until the owners return. Guides of the grass who cross the sand range repeat it at every bodek they stop at, and most of them drink from their own skins until the owners have come down to grant guest-right.

A guide who knows the mesa has usually heard that the Kuuyoyur keep a stone on Kelodvootri, and some say among themselves that a kelodud is not all that lives there.

## Reaching It

Kelodvootri is marked on no map and lies off every caravan track. A string blown off the road by a dust storm in the Long Dry, west of [[place-qisomrktcmp|Qiiso]], can reach it by the stars if its guide knows the mesa. The next water is three days off across open sand.

# The Truth of Kelodvootri {#truth .secret}

**The cistern.** The spring is older than the tribe and older than its palms. Under the well-mouth the shaft drops into a cistern cut from the rock by the builders of the [[lore-towercities|Tower Cities]], who found the spring and roofed it: a long hall of black water with ledges round it, a second drawing-basin cut into the north ledge, and an overflow channel that carries the excess out under the palms, where it waters their roots and comes up at the hole among them. A shutter of cut stone drops into a slot at the channel mouth to close it, worked from the north ledge by a bar and a counterweight. A narrow passage of steps runs from the cleft in the mesa foot down to the ledge, so that the cistern can be cleaned without lowering a man on a rope, and it is the way the wardens go down. Halfway down the passage a slab sits in a groove and slides aside on a dry shelf cut above the highest water the spring has ever reached. The shelf holds the Kuuyoyur's reserve for a ruinous year: silver in sealed jars, strings of turquoise, a cord of the tribe's passage tokens, and two fine swords in oiled cloth, the price the tribe was paid to end a feud four generations ago. A [[being-bnscrpn|bone scorpion]] keeps to the shade at the mouth of the cleft in most seasons, and the wardens pass it in the last hour before dawn, when it is slowest.

**The carvings.** The builders carved the walls of the passage and the face of the ledge. The figures in them carry jars, and every figure pours water back into the pool before it draws. A figure with long arms lies under the ledge in every panel, its arms spread beneath the pool, and the carvings show nothing of its face. The builders knew what was under their water, kept its courtesy, and drew.

**The Bodekud.** The thing in the cistern is a [[lore-dreadspawncrtr|dreadspawn]], not a kelodud, and the tribe has a word for it that it does not use in front of strangers: **Bodekud**, "the one who owns the water". The Kuuyoyur use the word as a name and have long forgotten that it is a plain description. Nobody has seen the whole of it. What has been seen is arms: pale, wet, jointed in more places than an arm should be, and longer than three men laid end to end, ending in hands with long smooth fingers that leave prints with no lines in the palm. It hangs beneath the ledges with its arms spread over the pool and its body under the stone, a pale bulk larger than a camel. It comes into the open only at night, by the overflow channel and the palm roots, and the dry wind of noon drives it deep. A sudden heat makes it go still, fire on its arms makes it draw them under, and a pool fouled with its blood is bitter for a season. It does not climb the passage into the dry air and does not follow anyone up the shaft into daylight.

Whether the builders dug down to something that was already there, or a maker set it in the spring to keep the water for them, nobody can say. The saying of the Khoshiqor tribes is, at Kelodvootri, a plain statement of fact: the owner never left.

**The owner's custom.** The Bodekud keeps the custom of [[lore-ownedoasis|the owned oasis]] exactly, as the owner of the water, and every act of it that looks like hunting is a step of that custom.

| What a stranger does                          | What the Bodekud does                                                                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| lets a rope down the shaft                    | takes the rope, and lays it coiled and wet by the well-stone, in the knot of the man who lowered it: the proof of what he meant                                         |
| draws water without leave                     | a beast for every skin drawn, taken from the beasts that drank that water, with any skin of it still hanging near the palms; the tribe's own warden is a stranger to it |
| draws, and has not paid                       | holds one of the party alive until the debt is paid                                                                                                                     |
| fights it, fouls the pool, or harms the palms | blood: it drowns the one who did it, if it can hold him, and weights him under the water                                                                                |
| pours water back into the pool before drawing | that is asking guest-right of the owner, and it is granted: the Bodekud does not touch him while he stays at the pool                                                   |
| gives a beast or a weight into the water      | it accepts the payment, one for each skin owed or each life it keeps, and lets the held go                                                                              |

It keeps what it takes. The beasts it collects stand alive in an air pocket beneath the south ledge, half in the water, tied to stones with good rope in the hitch of whoever lowered it, as an owner keeps a forfeit herd; the one it holds is tied among them, cold, and lives for a day or two before he drowns. Each night a debt stands, it winds the stranger's rope up the shaft and lets it fall again, one pull for each skin still owed, then a pause and the splash: the creak and pause of a bucket drawn, which carries through the lid and up into the palms. When a beast is collected, the count drops by one.

**Its signs.** These are what a visitor meets, and each is the custom at work:

- A rope that goes slack in the shaft and comes back up coiled, wet, and tied off in the knot of whoever lowered it, or turns up beside the well-stone.
- The sound of a rope wound slowly up a shaft that has no rope in it, always to the same count of pulls on any one night.
- Handprints round the well-ring and the palm roots, which dry in the sun to a white crust that smells of standing water.
- A tether, a lead or a cord drawn back across the sand toward water, steadily, with nothing visible at the far end.
- A beast or a man taken without a sound, and later found under the water or in the air pocket, bound with his own rope to a stone.

**This season.** At the end of this dry season the warden **Voghalkenod**, his wife **Numaqlun** and her nephew **Budaqtoliz** came to the well with three camels, and on the first evening they drew six skins on their own rope, as the tribe has done since the stone went on, and poured nothing back. The next morning Voghalkenod went down the shaft on that rope to clear the sump. The Bodekud held him. He fought it with his knife, and the custom answers a fight with blood: it drowned him and weighted him under the water beneath the south ledge, and his knife, dark with its blood, lies on the ledge above him. When it surged up the shaft after the rope, the old lid cracked across its middle and the crack ran on through the south ledge. The two riders laid a fresh stone over the shaft and chained it, and they have watched from the cleft since. The Bodekud has taken two of their camels, one skin each, and four skins stand owed. It has held nobody since the warden, because the riders keep to the cleft and have not gone near the palms after dark; the first person who has drunk the drawn water and comes among the palms after dark is the one it holds.

> [!TIP]
> Present the Bodekud as an owner, never as a hunter, even while the party takes it for one. It is unhurried and exact: it takes no beast it is not owed, it passes over any beast that drank nothing drawn, and it keeps what it takes alive. Describe each act plainly and let the pattern accumulate.

> [!WARNING]
> The winding is a count, and it is the clearest clue at the well. Keep the count exact on every night it is heard, and drop it by one each time a beast is taken. A GM who rounds it off or varies it for effect spends the clue.

**The tellings, weighed.**

- **The kelodud.** False. A kelodud has no hands, does not come up into the open where the wind blows, does not return what it takes and cannot tie a knot. The Kuuyoyur have called the Bodekud one for generations, and the name has let them forget what the other word means.
- **Letting the well drink first.** True, and more than a charm. The grandmothers who poured back the first draw were asking guest-right of the owner below, as the builders did in the carvings, and the Bodekud granted it every season they asked.
- **The wells held in trust until the owners return.** At Kelodvootri, literally true.
- **The guides' unease.** Well founded: the thing under the stone is not a kelodud. It is not a hunter either.
