## Overview

The water at Wemaq is the best on the whole road. It comes up cold through white sand into a pool the size of a threshing floor, and round it stand date palms thicker than a man's waist, their crowns so heavy with fruit that the trunks lean. The grass is knee-high in a country where grass means a good year. There is no skull-pole at the approach, no well-stone and no mark cut in a palm. No tent has stood beside it since anyone's grandmother.

**Wemaq**, "the dead" in [[skill-sowideslng|Sowides]], lies a short ride off [[lore-celestialroad|the Celestial Road]], and a caravan that comes within sight of it halts. The animals pull toward the water. The guide holds them back. Most of the string reaches the next well thirsty and a little ashamed, and does not ask about it.

## How It Was Made

About 312 AF the _orqwen_ who would lead [[lore-hndrdbnnrs|the Storm of the Hundred Banners]] came to the oasis at the head of the tribes already sworn to him, and the last tribe that refused to swear was camped at it. Some four thousand people were there, with their herds. The _orqwen_ put them to the sword over a morning and forbade anyone to lay them out. Their names were not spoken to the four winds. The tribe's dowek was made to watch.

The water was sweet that year, as it was every year, and the dates fell on the bodies. The next spring the dead were gone to a skin of bone in grass already knee-high, and the grass has never been less.

## What Grows

The ground at Wemaq is a quickening. Palms fruit twice a year. Seeds thrown out of a saddle in the evening are green shoots by the morning. A camel that drinks here comes away sleek and a little glassy-eyed, and a man who sleeps a night beside the pool wakes rested in his limbs and with the sound of horses in his ears.

The field is moderate. It kills nobody by itself, and it does not need to.

- **By day,** a person who lingers is overtaken by the battle's last hour. The grass seems to move against the wind, and there is a low wet noise from the reed beds like men breathing.
- **By night,** the dream comes to everyone who sleeps within sight of the palms: the dowek on his knees, the horns, the long line of banners coming through the trees. It is clear and it does not end on waking. People who sleep at Wemaq for three nights hear it in every quiet afterward.

A caravan short of water that draws here in daylight and moves off before dusk takes no lasting harm. A caravan that camps carries the sleepers home with a stare and a bad habit of flinching at hoofbeats, and some are never right again.

## How the Road Lives with It

- **Nobody camps.** Each string that draws water here fills its skins by day, rinses nothing and leaves before the light turns yellow.
- **The dates stay on the palm.** What falls is left to lie, and the guides will not eat the fruit. The sweetness is what gets into the dreams.
- **No tribe claims it.** A _bedaq_ is a tribe's property, and Wemaq is the water on the road that belongs to nobody. The tribes of the Hosikor say so quietly.

## What Is Still There

The gear of the dead is in the grass: saddle-trees gone grey, iron fittings in the shapes of buckles and bits, a pile of tent-poles that has gone to roots and put out leaves. The tribe's herd-marks are visible on a few skulls. Nobody has taken more than a handful of them. A stranger seen carrying something out is news that a Ruwel guide sells to the nearest tribes within the month.

## Hooks

- **Sweet dates.** A hidden-water guide at [[place-qisomrktcmp|Qìso]] has been selling jars of Wemaq dates to a Byzarian spice-buyer who thinks they come from a private grove. The buyer's cook has stopped sleeping.
- **The names.** The few who escaped were taken in by a neighboring tribe, whose elders still hold the names of the dead by heart and have never agreed to speak them at the oasis. Saying them would mean naming the _orqwen_'s part in it aloud, before every tribe on the road, and that tribe sells passage to all of them.
- **The caravan that camped.** A merchant's string stands at the oasis in good order, tents pitched and animals fat, and the drivers lie asleep and breathing. None has woken in four days. The tribe whose range it is has offered a price to anyone who will carry them out before dusk, in daylight, without lingering.
