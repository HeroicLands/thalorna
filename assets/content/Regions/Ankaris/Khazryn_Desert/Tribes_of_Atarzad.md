# terran_analog: "Israelites of the Exodus and Conquest—twelve-tribe nomadic-pastoral confederation, monolatrous fire-cult, covenantal land-claim, post-bondage founding narrative under a singular prophet, blood-feud with the indigenous urban polytheists whose territory they seized"

The **Tribes of Ātárzád** are a confederation of twelve allied tribes who hold four of the walled cities of [[place-swoasisbelt|Tellumel]], the oasis belt at the north foot of the Grazian Mountains, and the high ground around [[place-mountshofar|Mount Shōfar]]. They are newcomers—by the reckoning of their own griots, three generations have passed since the Conquest, and the forebears who came up out of bondage in the [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Aû'Khelâthu]] under the prophet **Mūshárā** and received the **Promise of the Tellumel** at the Unconsuming Flame are the stuff of recitation. By the reckoning of their adversaries, the [[affiliation-khzrncnfdrtn|Tellumi]], they are three generations of trespassers whose claim rests on theology rather than on right.

The Tribes are a power of one corner of the Khazryn, not of the waste. The steppe tribes to the north answer to no one in Tellumel, the wells and grazing of the open waste belong to the tribes that own them, and an Ātárzád caravan master buys safe passage there tribe by tribe like any other.

Both reckonings are correct. The Ātárzád do not deny that the Tellumi were here first; they deny that _first_ matters. The land was promised to the Flame-born by the Sacred Flame himself. To the faithful Ātárzád, that promise is more solid than any wall the Tellumi built before them and any deed the Tellumi kept after them.

## Origins: The Bondage and the Exodus

Every festival of the Ātárzád opens with the recitation of the founding, and a child who falters in it is made to begin again. By that tradition the ancestors of the Twelve Tribes were a single labor-caste in the temple-cities of Aû'Khelâthu, brought from somewhere to the east in a generation now beyond memory and held there for centuries as builders, brick-makers, and irrigation-diggers under the Gar-Aû's quarry-overseers. They worshipped Ātáröš in secret, in lamps kept burning behind hearth-stones, while the priests of the Khelâthi temples sought to drive them into the worship of [[affiliation-uqaa|Uqa'â]] and [[affiliation-thubai|Thubâ'i]].

The Exodus narrative is dense, much-elaborated, and central to Ātárzád identity. Its core: a prophet named **Mūshárā**, born to a labor-caste woman but raised in a minor noble household after some accident of fortune, was called by Ātáröš at a desert flame that burned without consuming its tinder. Ātáröš commanded Mūshárā to lead the Flame-born out of bondage and across the desert into a land that had been kept for them. Mūshárā confronted the Gar-Aû of the day, who refused; Ātáröš afflicted Aû'Khelâthu until the Gar-Aû relented; the Flame-born departed.

The route of the Exodus is by tradition divided into three legs.

The **first leg** carried the Flame-born northeast out of Aû'Khelâthu, across the narrow desert that joins the [[place-nrthrnfrtlrgn|Northern Fertile Region]] to the southern coast of the [[place-vylarianse|Vylarian Sea]], and up the eastern shore of that sea, opposite the islands of [[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Harad]]. The Haradian merchant-states of the time, recognizing both an opportunity and a moral obligation, shipped supplies across the strait and provisioned the host with grain, fresh water, and what Haradian chroniclers euphemistically call "discounted iron." Ātárzád law to this day forbids raiding into Harad, and the Ardashír merchant-houses maintain especially scrupulous trading relationships with Haradian counterparts in continued recognition of the obligation.

The **second leg** carried the Flame-born east-and-north through the **Dunhari uplands**—the long traverse of the high Dunhari plateau that runs between the [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Sultanate]] (whose southern marches the Ātárzád skirted with care) and the open [[place-dunharargn|Dunhari]] tribal interior to the south. The Dunhari sheikhs of the day were too occupied with their own internal feuds to oppose the migration; the Flame-born paid passage in temple-gold and proceeded.

The **third leg** was the **Wandering** itself—the long traverse of the great central deserts and the high-cold plateaus that stretch between the eastern Dunhara and the southern Khazryn. The original generation died in the dry country; a new generation, born free, completed the crossing and prepared to enter the land. By the time the Flame-born came down off the Stone Uplands past Mount Shōfar into Tellumel, those who had personally remembered Aû'Khelâthu were nearly all dead, and the people who would conquer the Tellumi were a wholly new generation—born free, raised in the Promise, hardened by the wilderness.

The Promise itself was given to Mūshárā at **Mt. Shōfar**, a black-rock peak at the southern edge of the Stone Uplands, rising over the eastern half of Tellumel, where Ātáröš revealed himself in a flame that burned in the open air on bare stone for three days and nights without any fuel and without diminishing. The covenant Ātáröš gave Mūshárā there is still recited in full at every Festival of the Promise:

> _I am Ātáröš, the Sacred Flame, who lit the first hearth and shall light the last. The Tellumel I give to you and to your seed after you, in perpetuity, for as long as a flame burns upon a Flame-born hearth. Walk before me with truth in your mouth and the kindling in your hand. Honor no other before me. The Tellumel is yours._

Mūshárā died on the slope of Mt. Shōfar, never having entered the choicest land himself. His tomb is the second-holiest site of the Ātárzád after the Unconsuming Flame above it.

## The Conquest

The Conquest of Tellumel—what the Ātárzád call the **War of the Twelve Years** and what the Tellumi call simply the **Catastrophe**—followed under Mūshárā's successor, the Dūkhen **Yáhōshí**. The Tellumi at the time were a confederation of seven independent oasis-cities, each governed by a hereditary princely house and a temple of Āhúrdáén, militarily competent but unprepared for what came at them: a tribally organized people who had been hardened by the wilderness, who fought as twelve allied units, and who believed without reservation that the cities they were attacking had been promised to them and that to fall in the taking of them was to die in the bosom of the Sacred Flame.

The Tellumi had stone walls, water-engineering, professional cavalry, and several centuries of written law. The Ātárzád had numbers, surprise, mobility, religious zeal, and the willingness to take losses no professional army would have accepted. Over twelve years the Ātárzád took, in order: **Zargandûr** (year 2), **Ashkarad** (year 5), **Kethramír** (year 8), and finally the Tellumi's holiest seat, **Oasis of Shirvan** (year 11), where the great spring gushes from the rock at the foot of the Mōrávar cliff and where the Tellumi traditionally held that Āhúrdáén had revealed the cosmic order to the desert peoples in the first age. The fall of Shirvan ended the war. The surviving Tellumi princely houses retreated to peripheral oases or fled to the [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Sultanate of Amradad]]; the Tellumi population that remained was reduced to a subject merchant-and-craftsman class within the now-Ātárzád cities.

That was about ninety years ago. The Ātárzád have held those four cities since, and they have taken no more: the other Tellumi cities still stand.

## The Twelve Tribes

The twelve tribes are descended (so the genealogies say) from the twelve children of **Ātarpāt the Many-Sired**, a patriarch of the labor-caste in Aû'Khelâthu who lived several generations before Mūshárā. Each tribe maintains its own genealogy back to Ātarpāt, its own dialect-inflection of the Ātárzādi tongue, its own particular tribal customs, and its own apportioned territory among the conquered cities and the ground around them.

The most prominent five tribes are:

- **Bāhrām**—the largest tribe, holding most of Zargandûr and the great pasturelands to its north. Traditionally the war-tribe; the Dūkhen has been chosen from Bāhrām in five of the last seven elevations.
- **Ardashír**—holders of Ashkarad and the gates where the caravan road from the steppe comes into the belt. The merchant-tribe; the wealthiest of the twelve and the most cosmopolitan, with substantial diaspora communities in Amradad and even in Haradian ports.
- **Khorshad**—holders of the central highland country and the slopes of Mt. Shōfar itself. The keeper-tribe; the Tōqēl (Keeper of the Unconsuming Flame) has by long custom always been a Khorshad.
- **Zarvān**—holders of Kethramír and the oases east of it. The scholar-tribe; their elders maintain the scriptural archive and the law-codes of Mūshárā.
- **Mihrān**—holders of Shirvan and the western fringe. The militant tribe, considered most uncompromising on matters of theology and most likely to support the strict-observance party in inter-tribal politics.

The remaining seven (Ráhmān, Vaspūr, Sāvash, Yazdānbar, Pārmīda, Dārvāz, Ardvī) are smaller, hold portions of the lesser oases or the herding-grounds between, and align variously with the larger five on inter-tribal questions.

## Government

The Ātárzád have no king. Mūshárā explicitly forbade the office at Mt. Shōfar—_"You shall have no king, for I, Ātáröš, am king of the Flame-born; if you raise a king over yourselves, he will eat your sons and your daughters and your fields"_—and the prohibition is taken with great seriousness. Several attempts have been made over the generations to elevate a particularly successful Dūkhen to permanent kingship; all have ended in repudiation, and the most ambitious such attempt (by the Bāhrām war-leader Ártáz the Bold, two generations ago) ended with Ártáz's repudiation and exile by the Council of the Twelve.

What the Ātárzád have instead is the **Council of the Twelve**, which convenes annually at **[[place-beitshofar|Beit-Shōfár]]** (the priestly seat at the foot of Mt. Shōfar) for a six-week session. Each tribe sends its Rēzhul (hereditary chieftain), the senior elders of its principal lineages, and its senior priest. The Council sets common policy on defense, taxation, the disposition of disputed territories, the adjudication of inter-tribal disputes, and the renewal of the Covenant.

In times of major crisis—full-scale war, drought, religious schism, or a particularly dangerous Tellumi uprising—the Council may acclaim a **Dūkhen** (Judge), a single figure granted broad temporary executive authority. The Dūkhen is by tradition always a prophet or a warrior of demonstrated capability, always selected after consultation with the Tōqēl (who interprets the will of Ātáröš through the Unconsuming Flame), and always serves only for the duration of the crisis. Twenty-three Dūkhen have been acclaimed in the three generations since the Conquest. The current generation has had none; the last died fourteen years ago after suppressing a Tellumi-led rebellion in the Shirvan district.

## Religion

The Ātárzád practice **monolatrous devotion to Ātáröš the Sacred Flame**. They are not—strictly speaking—monotheists; they acknowledge the existence of the other eleven gods of the [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|Āsháian pantheon]] as real beings, even powerful ones. But they hold that Ātáröš alone is the patron of the Flame-born, that Ātáröš alone gave them the Promise, and that Ātáröš alone will receive their devotion. To worship the others—Āhúrdáén, Khóršádrä, Mítharä, Zúráthrä, any of them—is, for an Ātárzád, a covenant violation: a betrayal of the agreement at Mt. Shōfar.

Several practical consequences follow:

- **No temples to other gods may stand on Ātárzád land.** The Tellumi temples in the conquered cities were systematically dismantled in the generation after the Conquest. The stones were used to build the great fire-temples of Ātáröš that now dominate the city centers of Zargandûr, Ashkarad, Kethramír, and Shirvan. Some Tellumi maintain that the foundations of the Shirvan fire-temple still incorporate the original altar-stone of Āhúrdáén, hidden in inverted position deep below the floor—a charge the Ātárzád priesthood denies and refuses to allow excavation to verify.
- **Imagery is strictly forbidden.** Ātáröš may not be depicted; only the Flame itself may stand for him, and even the Flame may not be reduced to a mere drawing. The Ātárzád temples are aniconic. The Tellumi temples were full of deity-statues; this is one of the central charges the Ātárzád lay against Tellumi religion.
- **The Tōqēl**, the Keeper of the Unconsuming Flame, is the senior religious authority of the Ātárzád. By tradition always a Khorshad, always elderly, always elevated only on the death of the previous holder. The current Tōqēl is **Aspánsár son of Yáhōshí**, a man of perhaps eighty winters who is widely held to have spoken with Ātáröš in dreams.
- **The Festival of the Promise** is celebrated three times yearly—at the spring equinox, at midsummer, and at the autumn equinox—by pilgrimage to Mt. Shōfar. Every adult Ātárzád is expected to make the pilgrimage at least once before death; many make it annually. The summer festival draws upward of fifty thousand pilgrims, who camp in the valleys around the mountain and gather at the Unconsuming Flame for the recitation of the Covenant.
- **Dietary law** distinguishes the Ātárzád sharply from the Tellumi. Meat from any animal that did not die with its blood properly drained may not be eaten. Certain animals—most notably the desert hare, the wild boar, and several species of reptile—are wholly proscribed. The first fruits of every harvest and the first-born of every herd must be offered at a fire-temple before the remainder may be consumed.

## Economy

The Ātárzád live off what they took: the irrigated gardens and palm groves of the four cities, the tolls of the caravan road where it comes into the belt, and the herds they graze on the uplands round Mount Shōfar. They did not initially understand oasis-agriculture; the Tellumi water-engineers and palm-grove cultivators were essential to keeping the canals running after the Conquest, and a substantial Tellumi technical class persists in the cities today as a result. Over three generations the Ātárzád have absorbed enough of the technique to manage the systems themselves, but the most senior water-masters in every Ātárzád city are still, by overwhelming majority, Tellumi by descent.

Caravans coming down from the steppe pay the Ātárzád at the gates of the belt: a toll on every loaded camel, a fee for a guide through the canal country, and a levy for lodging in the caravanserais that the fire-temples own. The Ardashír hold the gates of Ashkarad and take the largest share. Dates, grain, wool and the cattle and horses of the uplands go out in return, and Ātárzád merchants carry belt goods to Amradad and to Haradian ports.

The cities are taxed; the herding tribes pay a tithe of cattle and horses; the Council of the Twelve maintains a modest standing treasury at Beit-Shōfár. There is no central tax-collecting bureaucracy; each tribe is responsible for raising its own contribution and presenting it at the annual Council session.

## Defense

Each tribe maintains its own warrior society—the **Sárdá** of the Bāhrām, the **Ārmán** of the Khorshad, the **Zhárván** of the Mihrān, and so on. The warrior societies are age-graded; young men enter them at sixteen and serve through their forties. There is no professional standing army; mobilization is by tribal call, with the twelve societies coordinating through the Council in time of inter-tribal action.

The Ātárzád fighting style is built around mounted archery, fast-moving cavalry detachments, the use of the great horses of the uplands to outpace and outmaneuver heavier forces, and a distinctive willingness to take heavy losses for theologically-significant objectives. The Tellumi were defeated in the Conquest in part because they faced an enemy whose acceptable casualty rate exceeded any rate a normal professional force would have countenanced. The Ātárzád have retained that institutional willingness.

## Relations with Other Peoples

To the **southwest**, the [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Sultanate of Amradad]] is the dominant Āsháian-pantheon power and is, technically, the senior religious authority for all Āsháian-faith peoples—the Mōbad of Āhúrdáén's Great Fire-Temple in Amradad is the formal head of the Āsháian priesthood. Relations are complicated by the fact that the Sultanate's official religion is the full pantheon (with Āhúrdáén supreme) and the Ātárzád consider this idolatry. Diplomatic intercourse is maintained—the Ardashír merchants would not tolerate its breakdown—but it is cool. The Sultanate considers the Ātárzád to be schismatics whose elevation of Ātáröš to exclusive worship is a covenant violation against Āhúrdáén; the Ātárzád consider the Sultanate to be polytheistic apostates from the original Mūshárán revelation. The Sultanate hosts the largest Tellumi exile community in the world, which it has consistently refused to extradite.

To the **northwest**, the [[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Confederation of Haradian States]] is a major trading partner. The Ardashír merchant-houses maintain offices in Sanctara and Alyssar, and Haradian bankers handle Ātárzád commerce in the Vylarian Sea. Religious differences do not impede trade.

To the **east**, beyond the waste, lie [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]] and (much further) [[place-tanvuregin|Tānvür]]. Contact is thin and through merchants; the Ātárzád know of these distant civilizations but have little practical engagement with them.

To the **far west**, [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Aû'Khelâthu]] is a special case. By Ātárzád doctrine, no Flame-born may set foot in Aû'Khelâthu willingly—to do so is to return to the place of bondage. The Ardashír merchants who must reach the Northern Fertile Region for trade do so by sea via Haradian shipping rather than overland through Khelâthi territory; the marginal cost of the longer route is considered a religious obligation. The current Gar-Aû of Aû'Khelâthu has, in recent years, made overtures suggesting that any returning Flame-born would be welcomed and that the old bondage is many generations gone; the Council of the Twelve has not deigned to reply.

To the **north**, the open waste belongs to the steppe tribes, who are independent of everyone. The Ātárzád pay them for passage and guest-right as any caravan does, and trouble on the uplands round Mount Shōfar is as likely to come from a tribe's raiders as from a Tellumi rising.

Within Tellumel, the [[affiliation-khzrncnfdrtn|Tellumi Confederation]] is the eternal adversary. The two peoples share the belt, share a handful of gods (whom the Tellumi honor and the Ātárzád merely acknowledge), and are locked in a blood-feud that no living person remembers the beginning of. The Ātárzád hold four cities by the Promise; the Tellumi claim them by descent from the high springs and centuries of continuous habitation. Neither will yield.

## The Blood-Feud {#the-blood-feud}

The Ātárzád-Tellumi blood-feud is not a single ongoing war; it is a continuous low-level conflict punctuated by larger crises. Ordinary Ātárzád and Tellumi live within the same cities, trade with each other, and (rarely but not never) intermarry, and most years pass without any large action. But:

- Tellumi rebellions periodically erupt in the conquered cities, usually centered on a particular grievance (a confiscated property restored to a Tellumi heir, a denied water-right, a Tellumi shrine secretly maintained that the Ātárzád discover and demolish). The rebellions are suppressed, often brutally.
- Ātárzád settler families occasionally extend cultivation into peripheral oases that the Tellumi consider ancestrally theirs; the Tellumi respond with raids; the Ātárzád respond to the raids; the cycle escalates.
- Religious provocations—a Tellumi who openly worships Āhúrdáén in an Ātárzád city, an Ātárzád who desecrates a peripheral Tellumi shrine—produce flashpoints that may take a generation to subside.
- The Tellumi exiles in Amradad maintain a continuous diplomatic and intelligence campaign aimed at recovery of the lost oases; the Ātárzád maintain a continuous counter-campaign.

The current generation is in a relatively quiet phase. The last full-scale rebellion was suppressed fourteen years ago; the next is overdue.

## Famous Figures

**Aspánsár son of Yáhōshí**, current Tōqēl, Keeper of the Unconsuming Flame. Eighty years old, holds the office for life, by tradition the spiritual head of the Ātárzád people. Said to dream of Mūshárā.

**Yázdarmīd of Bāhrām**, current Rēzhul of the Bāhrām tribe, the senior chief of the largest tribe and the man most likely to be acclaimed Dūkhen should the next crisis come. A career warrior, in his late fifties, considered politically moderate but militarily uncompromising.

**Hānā the Prophet**, a young woman (by some accounts only twenty-three) who has begun preaching that Ātáröš has shown her the imminent return of Mūshárā in flesh and that the Flame-born must prepare themselves through stricter observance, the demolition of the remaining Tellumi communities within the Ātárzád cities, and the formal expulsion of the Tellumi population. The Council of the Twelve has not yet determined whether she is to be honored or repudiated. Her following grows.

**Pārvīz the Renegade**, a former Ardashír merchant-prince who was excommunicated thirty years ago for marrying a Tellumi woman of high birth and refusing to repudiate her. He lives in Amradad with his wife and their children, who are formally Tellumi by his renouncement. He is wealthy, well-connected, and considered by some Tellumi exiles to be a useful ally and by others to be a useful idiot.

## Commerce and Currency

The Ātárzád live on the tolls of the caravan road where it enters Tellumel and on the produce of the four cities' gardens and the uplands' herds. Inside the cities, trade runs through [[lore-bartercnmy|barter]] and [[lore-kinhalcrdt|tribal credit]] customs, with the Ardashír counting-houses at Ashkarad settling what tribes owe one another; the Tellumi craftsmen and merchants of the cities have begun to adopt modest moneylender practices along Vedyaran lines (see [[lore-vdyrnbnkng|Vedyaran Banking]]). Tolls are paid in kind or in coin as the caravan master prefers. Foreign coin (Vylarian Argo from western trade, Khelâthi gezan pieces from southern trade) circulates by intrinsic-metal value among traders.
