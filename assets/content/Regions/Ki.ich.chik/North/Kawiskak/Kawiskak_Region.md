## Overview

Kawiskak Region is the land of the [[lore-kawiskakclt|Kawiskak]], the Spruce People, and lies within [[place-northkiichchik|Northern Ki'ich'chik]] on the [[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik Continent]]. It is the great northern forest: spruce and birch on bare rock, more lakes than anyone has counted, and a winter that lasts seven months. The only roads are the rivers, open water in summer and ice in winter. About 65,000 people live in it. Some 60,000 are Spruce People, who hunt it in small family bands and meet at the fishing lakes in summer, and a few thousand more are the [Barren-Ground People]{.name data-type="lore/culture"} beyond the treeline. Nobody farms here and nobody lives in one place all year.

## Geography

The region begins where the country of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Grass People]] ends. North of the plains the grass breaks into groves of aspen, the groves close into woods, and within a few days' walk the aspen gives way to spruce. From there the forest runs north for weeks of travel to the treeline, and east past the [Bitter Water]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, the great salt bay that cuts into the north of the continent, toward the eastern ocean.

Under the forest is old rock. Across most of the region the ground is bare pink and grey stone scraped smooth, with soil only in its hollows, and every hollow holds a lake, a bog or a river looking for a way out. Black spruce stands in the bogs, white spruce and birch on the higher ground, jack pine on the sand ridges, and tamarack turns yellow in the muskeg each autumn. Muskeg is bog of moss and water, knee-deep and slow. In summer nobody crosses it who can go around. In winter it freezes hard and becomes the easiest ground in the forest.

The rivers of the northern grass run north and east out of the parkland and gather in [Okiskosipa]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, the Fish Lake, a lake so long that a canoe needs ten days to travel it. One great river leaves it and runs north-east through rapids and falls to the Bitter Water. Most travel in the region follows that river or the lakes and portages beside it.

## Seasons

Winter is the longest season and the one the people plan their lives around. The lakes freeze at the first snow and stay frozen until late spring, and in the hardest cold the trees crack like thrown stones. A band spends the winter on its own ground, hunting and trapping, and moves camp on snowshoes when the game moves.

Summer is short and bright, with days that barely end. The blackflies and mosquitoes rise from the muskeg in clouds thick enough to drive moose into the lakes and caribou onto the windy ridges, and people go the same way, to camps on open shores where the wind keeps the flies down. Fires burn through the forest most summers, and the jack pine and the berries come back first after them.

Two seasons are worse than winter: break-up in spring and freeze-up in autumn, when the ice is too thin to walk on and too thick for a canoe. For a few weeks each time nobody travels, and a band caught between waters waits where it is.

## Game and Fish

Caribou are the forest's wealth. Small herds of forest caribou live in the bogs all year, and in winter the great herds of the barren grounds come south into the trees in tens of thousands, then go north again in spring to calve beyond the treeline. A band that meets the herds at a narrows or a river crossing kills enough in a week to eat through the cold months. Moose are taken in the river valleys, beaver in every stream, and bear in their winter dens.

Hare is the food of a lean winter. In most years the hares are everywhere and any child can snare them. Every ten years or so they die off, and in the winter after that the bands that depend on them starve. Every band knows the bad winters by the hare years.

Fish are the summer and the safeguard. Whitefish, pike and lake trout fill the nets in every lake, and in spring the sturgeon run up the rivers to spawn below the falls, where people spear them by the hundred. A band that cannot find game in winter cuts holes in the ice and sets its nets beneath it. Geese and ducks come north in spring and south in autumn by the thousand, and furs come from the marten, otter, mink, lynx and fox.

## Travel

Nobody in the region rides, and nobody on the continent does. In summer the people travel by canoe, made of birch bark sewn over a frame of cedar and sealed with spruce gum. A canoe is light enough for one person to carry over a portage, the trail around a rapid or between two lakes. In winter they walk on snowshoes and pull their goods on toboggans of split birch, and dogs pull with them. A family can move its whole camp in a day.

A traveler from the south needs a guide in either season. Water that looks open runs into a falls, a portage trail disappears into muskeg, and ice that holds a person at noon gives way in the afternoon.

## The Treeline and Beyond

North, the forest thins. The spruce grow shorter and further apart and stand in islands on the sheltered slopes, until they stop. Beyond them lie the barren grounds: rolling tundra of moss, lichen and rock, long gravel ridges that snake across the land, and lakes without a tree around them. The great caribou herds calve there in early summer. In winter the barrens are nothing but wind.

The Barren-Ground People live out there. They are an older people of the open land: small bands in hide tents and half-sunken houses ringed with stone, who spear caribou at river crossings with lances and take seal on the ice of the Bitter Water's shore. They also dig copper. Lumps of pure copper lie in the gravels of the [Copper River]{.name data-type="place/feature"} in the far north-west, and every late summer the Barren-Ground bands bring them to the treeline and trade them to the forest bands for spruce gum, birch bark and wood for spear shafts. That copper goes south from hand to hand.

Now the [Ice-Edge People]{.name data-type="lore/culture"}' kin are moving east along the arctic coasts, with dog teams, big skin boats and harpoons whose heads turn in the wound. They take the coasts, the sealing grounds and the best caribou crossings, and the Barren-Ground People fall back south toward the trees. Each winter more of their bands come into the forest, onto ground the Spruce People's bands have hunted for generations. Some forest bands take them in and some drive them off. A few of the northernmost Spruce bands have given up their ground and moved south-east into the Pine People's country.

## Borders

South, the forest opens into parkland and then the grass of the [[place-tenlayargn|Tenlaya Region]], held by the Grass People, whose northern bands come to the Fish Lake each summer to trade. South-east, at the edge of the old rock, the forest meets the lake country of the [[place-osetakonargn|Osetakona Region]], and the Pine People, the northernmost nation of the [[affiliation-osetakona|Maple People]]. The Ice-Edge People' kin hold the far north, and their boats come further east along the coast each summer. Far to the east, beyond the Maple People's shore, are the eastern ocean and the strange boats of the [[lore-nordheimnclt|Nordmen]] ([[lore-westrnvoyages|The Western Voyages]]), which the Spruce People know only from stories carried up the rivers.

None of the Spruce People's bands belongs to the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]], and no Spruce band has sent anyone to its council.

## Trade

Furs and copper go south. Beaver, marten, otter and lynx pelts, the Barren-Ground copper and birch-bark canoes go to the Grass People at the Fish Lake and to the Pine People at their northern villages. From the Grass People come bison fat, dried meat pounded with berries, robes and tobacco, and from the Pine People come maple sugar, corn, flint and the Maple People's own lake copper. The forest bands trade at the edges and nowhere else. Few traders of the compact nations come further in than the Fish Lake, and those who do come with a Spruce guide.
