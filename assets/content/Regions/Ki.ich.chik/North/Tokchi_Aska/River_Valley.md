# terran_analog: the central Mississippi valley from the mouth of the Ohio to the Missouri, the American Bottom around Cahokia above all, with its bluffs, oxbow lakes and levee ridges

## Overview

The River Valley (_hokpa sompiliha_) is the heartland of [[affiliation-tokchiaska|Tokchi Aska]], the One Fire: the floodplain of the great river and the bluffs that wall it, within the [[place-tokchiaskargn|Tokchi Aska Region]]. About 900,000 people live in it, more than in the rest of the nation together, in mound towns of one to fifteen thousand and in the hamlets that carry earth to them. Every Great Sun has ruled from it, and every Scattering has begun where its towns stopped sending maize.

A canoe on the river in summer is never out of sight of smoke. The towns stand on the old levee ridges a little above the floodplain, each round a plaza and its mounds, and between them the bottomland is a single field of maize, beans and squash broken by oxbow lakes, cane brakes and stands of cottonwood. A traveler coming downriver counts the towns by their great mounds, which rise above the tree line before the palisades come into view.

## Geography

The great river, the _hokpa_, runs south through a floodplain six to ten miles wide between bluffs of yellow loess. It wanders across that floor over the generations, cutting off its old loops as oxbow lakes and leaving long ridges of sandy soil where its banks used to be. Those ridges are the valley's dry ground, and every town in it sits on one. The low ground between them floods most springs and grows the best maize in the north once the water goes down: a household on the bottomland harvests twice what it eats.

The oxbow lakes and backwaters feed the towns as well as the fields. They hold catfish, gar and drum, and in autumn and spring the ducks and geese come through in flocks that take an hour to pass. The cane brakes on the low ground supply arrow shafts, mats and the walls of houses, and the bluffs supply chert for blades and hoes.

Where the eastern river comes in from [[place-hanalxilanrgn|Ha'nal Shi'lan]], the valley is at its widest, and [[place-hokpahosa|Hokpa Hosa]] stands at the meeting of the waters. A day's paddle below it lies [[place-hayopokta|Hayo Pokta]], the Sun Mound, and upriver of the Sun Mound stands [Pokta Hoshalo]{.name data-type="place/site"}, the Old Mound, where the Keepers say the first fire was set above the flood. A day's paddle below Hayo Pokta the mounds of the Second Gathering's city stand empty in the cottonwoods, and the valley's towns take no earth from them.

## The Western Bank

Across the river the bluffs give way to rolling prairie and open oak woods, and beyond them the grass of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Grass People]] runs west out of sight. The western towns are the valley's frontier. They build double palisades, keep their granaries inside them, and send their young men to the muster at [[place-kiyopokta|Kiyo Pokta]], the Falcon Mound, where the Falcon Chief gathers warriors against the plains bands. The same towns hold an autumn trade with the bands, maize and pots for robes and dried meat, and a raid in spring does not stop the trade in autumn.

## What Moves on the River

The river is the valley's road, and almost nothing moves overland that can go by water. In summer the dugouts carry maize up and down it to Hayo Pokta as tribute and back again as gifts, and the Great Sun's granary on the plaza takes in more than any town could eat. Copper comes down from the north through Hokpa Hosa, shell comes up from the [[place-koyalorgn|Shell Coast]], and robes and hides cross the river from the west. At the New Fire the traffic reverses for four days: runners carry coals from the great mound to every town, and canoes wait at the landings to take them on.

## Floods

The spring flood is the valley's gift and its fear. A town is sited where the old people say the water has never reached, and in a high year the mounds are the last dry ground, with families camped on their flanks among the graves. The year of a great flood is the year a town learns whether its Mound Chief kept the granary full. The Keepers read a high flood as a sign that the fire was badly kept, and the commoners notice that a flood fills the Keepers' storehouses with offerings.
