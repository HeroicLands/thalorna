## Overview

The Kelwanuk are the Cedar People, the nation of the western coast, about 200,000 people in villages of cedar-plank houses along the fjords, inlets and outer beaches of the [[place-kelwanukrgn|Kelwanuk Region]]. They take their name from the red cedar (_kelwa_), the tree that gives them their houses, canoes, boxes and clothes. The south calls them _Ish'bal Ki'ahk_, the Eagle People, after the eagles carved on the crest poles of [[place-tsilkasis|Tsilkasis]], where its traders first met them. Their ways are set out in the [[lore-kelwanukclt|Kelwanuk]] culture note, and their tongue is the [[skill-kelwanuklng|Kelwanuk Language]].

The Cedar People are rich, and they are rich because of the sea. Every year the salmon come up their rivers in runs so great that a village dries and smokes a year's food in a few weeks, and the coast adds seal, whale and shellfish, and the grease of the eulachon, a small oil fish that every feast must give. With food so plentiful, the people live in large permanent villages of cedar-plank houses facing the beach, with carved house posts inside and crest poles outside that show the histories of the lineages who live there.

## Who Rules

The house rules, and nothing rules the houses. A house (_temla_) is a great plank building and the lineage of thirty to eighty people who live in it, with its own names, crests, fishing places and dances. Its chief (_hemkis_) holds its highest name for life, orders when and where its people fish, takes a share of every catch, and gives it back at his feasts. A chief does not raise his voice: his Speaker (_talwamis_) speaks for him, recites the house's names and seats, and settles quarrels with the Speakers of other houses.

Every house is sharply ranked. Its nobles (_yekwamis_) hold its hereditary names, each with a seat at the feasts of the coast and the crests and rights that come with it. Its commoners (_temlamis_) are of the house but hold none of its names. Its slaves (_helayekwa_), taken in raids or born to slaves, belong to it outright and have no name at all.

Above the houses there is only the order of seats. Every name on the coast has its place in one order, from the first seat of the first house to the last seat of the least, and every feast seats and serves its guests in that order. The chief of the house that sits first holds the first seat (_kenwatukwa_). He speaks first at any council of chiefs, is served first at any feast, and leads when the houses go to war together. That is the whole of his authority. His house keeps the first seat while its gifts are the greatest on the coast, and another house takes it when they are not. The [Eagle House]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} of Tsilkasis has held it for four generations of chiefs.

## The Giving Feast

Rank on the coast is claimed and confirmed at the giving feast (_kitala_). A chief who takes a new name, raises a pole, marries his daughter or lifts the name of a dead predecessor invites the other houses, feeds them for days, and gives away or destroys a fortune in blankets, canoes, grease and copper before them. His Keeper of the Count (_kalumis_) says the count of every gift aloud, and the guests paid as witnesses (_nilkwamis_) remember it. The guests' acceptance of the gifts is their acknowledgment of his claim. Every gift is a debt (_humkwa_) that the receiver must answer with a greater one at a feast of his own, and a house that cannot answer falls down the order of seats. Rival chiefs ruin their houses trying.

The greatest gift is a copper (_lukwa_), a beaten sheet shaped like a shield, with its own name and history. A copper's worth rises every time it changes hands. A chief who breaks one, cutting off a piece and giving it to a rival, destroys a fortune before witnesses, and the rival must break one of equal worth or lose face.

In winter the houses put away their summer names and hold the winter dances (_hilamala_), in which masked dancers act out the meetings of the first ancestors with the powers of the sea and the forest. The masks are among the finest carving in the north.

## War and the Compact

The Cedar People's war canoes (_yakwis_) are great seagoing dugouts, each cut from a single cedar, that carry thirty or forty warriors along the coast. A war canoe captain (_yakwismis_) commands each. The houses raid each other in feuds, and they raid distant coasts for slaves, most of all to the south.

The Cedar People are the compact's sea power on the western coast, a month's journey from the quarry and the other four nations. They keep the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] for the prestige of a seat at its council, and the house that holds the first seat sends one of its nobles with a Speaker to talk for him. A seat is a thing the Cedar People understand.

## Trade

The coast trades with itself first: grease, dried salmon, cedar boxes, blankets and canoes move between the houses, and most of it changes hands at feasts. To the south the Cedar People trade along the coast with the [[affiliation-shuqash|Shell-Bead People]], whom they court for their shell money and raid for slaves, and neither side forgets either. In summer their traders cross the mountain passes to the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Grass People]] and the [[affiliation-teyumi|Turquoise People]], and through the Turquoise People a few southern goods reach the coast: a macaw feather on a dancer's mask is something a chief shows his guests. The passes close in winter. To the north, beyond the last villages, the [[affiliation-tikupilarit|Ice-Edge People]] trade ivory and the furs of animals unknown farther south for cedar boxes and grease.
