## Overview

The Teyumi are the Turquoise People, the nation of the desert's northern rim, about 150,000 people in towns of stone great houses across the mesas and canyons of the [[place-teyumirgn|Teyumi Region]]. They take their name from turquoise (_teyu_), which they hold to be rain made stone. The south calls them _Ishtem Tsul_, the Bear People, after the bears painted on the walls of their chambers, where the bear (_matsau_) is a healer and a guardian. Their ways are set out in the [[lore-teyumiclt|Teyumi]] culture note, and their tongue is the [[skill-teyumilng|Teyumi Language]].

A great house (_hosa_) is a terraced block of stone and adobe, four or five stories high at the back and stepping down to a walled plaza, with hundreds of rooms. Some are laid out so that the midwinter sunrise or the moon's farthest rising strikes a marked wall. Straight roads run between them over the tableland, and climb the cliffs by cut stairways. On the uplands the towns dry-farm corn, beans and squash; in the lowlands toward the desert they lead river water over their fields through canals (_kenuva_).

## Who Rules

The great house rules its town, and the rain rites rule the great house. Each town's great house is kept by one lineage, the great-house kin (_hosayo_), and that lineage gives the town both its rulers. The rain chief (_lumamota_) keeps the town's chief rain rites and orders its year: when it plants, when it dances, whom it expels. His sister or his mother is the house head (_hosamota_), who keeps the storerooms and decides who is fed. The two argue their disagreements inside the lineage, and the town hears the result.

Below them, every adult is an initiate (_peluyo_) of at least one chamber (_pelu_), the round rooms sunk into the plaza where the rites are held. The chamber elders (_pelumota_) teach the songs, lead the rites, and settle most quarrels inside their own chambers. A sun watcher (_waimayo_) stands at a fixed place each morning, reads where the sunrise falls along the rim, and names the days on which everything else happens. The clowns (_pukiya_) mock wrongdoers in the plaza at the summer dances, and their mockery is the first punishment a town gives.

The authority is real because the storerooms are. A great house that feeds its town in a dry year keeps its people and draws families from the canyons around it. One whose storerooms run empty loses them, and its rain chief's rites are doubted with it. Every house head swears, on taking her office, that her storerooms open before her chambers close. The oath comes from the [Year of Shut Doors]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, when the great houses of the canyon kept their corn for the rites and the hungry starved below their walls.

## The Towns Together

No town commands another. The towns of different canyons are bound by their roads, by marriages between their clans, and by the canyon feast each autumn at [Hosa Oyala]{.name data-type="place/region"}, the Canyon of Houses, where the first great houses of the north stand and where every town sends pilgrims. Quarrels between towns are settled at the feast, when they are settled at all. When a southern war party comes out of the desert, the towns act together because they must: signal fires on the mesa tops carry the warning north in an hour, and every great house draws up its ladders.

In the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]], the Turquoise People are represented by great-house kinsmen and a chamber elder. They are the compact's early warning, and they ask the other nations for warriors whenever the south comes. The other nations suspect them of being too close to the south, because they hold the desert trade and know the southern traders by name.

## The Northern Gate

Every trader and pilgrim who survives the crossing of [[place-chakultzklrgn|Ch'akul Tsa'kul]] comes first to [[place-kwatuhosa|Kwatuhosa]], the South House. Its Keeper of the South Road (_kwatuyo_) receives them, decides what goes north and at what price, and hears what they say of the south. The southern goods are few and precious: cacao, drunk by elders at the great feasts; scarlet macaws, kept alive in the great house plazas for the feathers the cloud dancers wear; and cast copper bells, worn at the dancers' knees. Turquoise goes south in return. The Turquoise People pass the southern goods on to the other nations at their own price.

To the west, over the mountains and a trail of desert springs, the towns' traders go to the [[affiliation-shuqash|Shell-Bead People]] of the western coast for shell beads and abalone shell, which the cloud dancers wear beside the macaw feathers. They pay in turquoise and cotton cloth, and they press the coast's chiefs to join the compact. The chiefs have not joined.

The rain dances cannot be danced without the macaw feathers, and some elders say the people are sending their rain away in turquoise to buy the masks they call it with. The word for the Keeper of the South Road means, in plain speech, the southerner.

## Drought

Drought is the Turquoise People's great fear. A town's fields, its canals and the corn in its great house all depend on the rain, and the rites of the chambers exist to call it. The towns hold that rain comes to hearts at peace, and a dry year ends with a search for the dry hearts (_pesatasi_) that are keeping it away. Those found are put out as the Dry (_pesayo_). The last three summers have been short of rain.
