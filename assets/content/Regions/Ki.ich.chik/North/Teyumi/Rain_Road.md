## Overview

The Turquoise People have no word for religion. What they have instead, the chamber elders call Luma Ponu, the Rain Road: the whole of what a town must do and be for the rain to find it. It is kept in the chambers (_pelu_), sunk into the plaza of every great house, and in the fields, the kitchens and the hearts of the people. It has no temples apart from the chambers and no priests apart from the elders, the sun watchers, the rain chiefs and the bear doctors, all of whom are townspeople who farm. Nothing in it is done alone. A prayer is said by a household, a rite is danced by a chamber, and a dry year is answered by a whole town.

A newcomer is taught the first rule before anything else: the rain comes to calm hearts. Everything else the faith asks follows from it.

## The Powers

The Turquoise People name few powers, and they ask little of most of them.

The Sun (_waima_)
: comes up over the eastern rim each morning and walks the sky to the western rim. Over the year his rising walks north along the rim to his summer house and back south to his winter house. At midwinter he must be turned back toward summer, and the sun-turning is held to help him. Every morning a woman throws a pinch of cornmeal toward him from her door.

The Grandmother Below
: keeps the world under this one, from which the people came up through the navel in the oldest songs and to which the dead go down. She takes in everyone who dies and sends them back up as clouds. She sends nothing up for the dry-hearted dead except dust. The Turquoise People do not dance for her and do not ask her for anything. They bury their dead well.

The cloud grandparents (_omalo_)
: are the dead come back, the rain clouds over the fields of their kin. Every field gets cornmeal for them at its edge, every child is told that the cloud dancers are its grandparents, and every summer rite is danced to bring them home.

The Bear (_matsau_)
: is painted on the chamber walls with a line from his mouth to his heart. He is a healer and a guardian, the strongest thing in the mountains, and the one power who fights for the people against sickness. He has his own chamber in most great houses, and his initiates are the bear doctors.

The Water Serpent (Muva Pohiwa)
: lives under the springs and the rivers and lets the water out of the ground. He is horned, and long enough to hold a river in his coils. The canyon towns honor him at their springs with a bead and a word. The lowland towns of the [[place-kenuvasokorgn|Canal Plain]] feed him turquoise at their head gates every spring and fear him more than any other power, because a river he holds back empties every canal on the plain at once.

The people also honor their first ancestors by name. [Teyuwesa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Turquoise Walking, who led the walk out of the [Old Houses]{.name data-type="place/site"} to the [[place-hosaoyalargn|Canyon of Houses]], is sung in every chamber. [Hepuluma]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Rain at the Door, who opened her storerooms in the [Year of Shut Doors]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, is sung in every women's chamber and some men's. Neither is prayed to. They are the grandparents everyone shares.

## The Chambers

A chamber is a round room sunk into the plaza and roofed with timber and earth, entered by a ladder through a hole in its roof. Its floor has a fire pit, a stone that turns the draft from the vent, a bench around the wall, and the navel: a small hole in the floor, plugged with a stone, through which the people came up and the dead go down. A rite opens when an elder lifts the stone.

Every adult is an initiate (_peluyo_) of at least one chamber. A child is taken into one at nine or ten, in the winter, and is shown the cloud dancers unmasked. Each chamber keeps its own songs, its own rites and its own share of the year, and an initiate owes it work, food and a turn in its dances. The men's chambers keep the dances and the masks. The women's chambers keep the rites of the corn, the grinding and childbirth, and a man does not go down their ladders.

The ordinary tools of the faith are small. A prayer feather is a turkey or macaw feather tied to a stick with cotton string and planted where the prayer is meant to go: in a field, at a spring, on a grave or at the edge of a road. A road of cornmeal is a line of meal sprinkled on the ground to show a power where to walk, and one is laid before the cloud dancers when they come up the ladder and before the dead when they are carried to the back rooms. Turquoise is given to the dead and to the springs, because it is rain made stone and carries the seed of rain wherever it goes.

## The Bear Doctors

The bear doctors (_matsauyo_) are the Turquoise People's healers, and the nearest thing they have to a person who deals with powers on another's behalf. A bear doctor is an initiate of the Bear chamber, and most come to it by surviving a sickness the chamber cured. A person who is cured owes the Bear his life, and pays it by learning the Bear's work.

They hold that sickness is a thing put into a body, a splinter of bone, a thorn, a pebble or a knot of hair, by a dry-hearted person or a dry-hearted dead one. A bear doctor called to a sickbed sings the Bear's songs, looks into the sick person through a lump of clear quartz held to the eye, finds where the sickness lies, and sucks it out through the skin. He spits it into his palm and shows it to the family. Then he asks the question the family dreads: who put it there?

The answer is usually that nobody living did, that the sickness came on a dusty wind from an angry dead person and is now gone. Sometimes the bear doctor names a living person. A person named once is watched. A person named twice is sent to a chamber elder to settle whatever grudge his heart is carrying. In a dry year the naming becomes the Dry Search.

## The Round of the Rites

The sun watcher (_waimayo_) names the day of every rite from the sunrise along the rim. The great rites of the year are set out in the [[lore-teyumiclt|Teyumi]] culture note: the sun-turning at midwinter, the planting, the rain dances of high summer, and the canyon feast after the harvest. Three others belong here.

Feeding the Serpent
: is held in the lowland towns at dawn on the day the head gates are opened in spring. The canal chief (_kenuvamota_) and the chamber elders wade into the river where the main canal leaves it, and each throws in a turquoise bead and asks the Water Serpent to let the water go. Nobody eats until the water has reached the last field on the canal, which on a long canal is after dark.

The Far Rising
: comes once in a generation, when the moon rises at the far north of its wandering. Its watchers keep the count at [[place-yaluhepu|Yaluhepu]], the Moon Door, where the moon rises that year between two pillars of rock. The towns build in the year it comes, a story or a wing on a great house, because what is built for the moon is held to last.

The Dry Search
: is held after a run of dry summers, when a rain chief asks the bear doctors to find the hearts keeping the rain away. The bear doctors of a town go through it house by house over four days, looking at each person through their crystals and listening to what the neighbors say. On the fifth morning they name the dry hearts in the plaza, and the rain chief sends each one to a chamber elder or puts each one out as one of the Dry (_pesayo_). No Dry Search has been held at the canyon feast, where it would bind every town at once, since the Year of Shut Doors. The rain chief of [[place-yaahosa|Yaahosa]] has asked for one there this autumn.

## The Argument

The Dry Search is the part of the faith the Turquoise People argue about, and the argument is the one the [[lore-teyumiclt|Teyumi]] culture note sets out. The search always lands on the hungry, the angry, the odd and the old, and it has never once named a great-house kinsman. The elders who defend it say that the doctrine of the calm heart is sound and that the great houses have only ever failed it. The elders who oppose it ask what the rain is answering, if it answers the search.

The bear doctors are on both sides. [Huvowesa]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Bear clan, the oldest bear doctor in the canyon, would lead the search at the feast if it is held. The last Dry Search he took part in, as a boy carrying the meal, named his mother, who walked out of her town with her sister and was never heard of again. He has not said whether he will lead this one.

# The Roads {#truth .secret}

The chamber elders teach their initiates what the straight roads are for, and no initiate tells an outsider. The roads are for the dead. A cloud that comes back to its kin needs a way home from wherever the wind has taken it, and the roads run straight because the dead walk straight, as a runner (_ponuyo_) does when he carries a message without adding to it. Every road leads back to the Canyon of Houses, the first navel and the first rain.

The oldest road in the north leaves the canyon going south, toward [[place-kwatuhosa|Kwatuhosa]] and the desert. The elders of the Grandmother House teach that it runs on under the sand all the way to the Old Houses, and that it is the road the people walked north on. They also teach that the dead of the Old Houses still walk north along it, as dust storms, because they died when the rain left their country and their hearts were dry. A rain chief who wants to know whether the south is coming watches the desert for dust as closely as the Keeper of the South Road watches for traders.

Whether any of this is true is a matter of belief. The roads themselves are real, older than any great house now standing, and wider than their traffic needs. The road south does run on into the desert past Kwatuhosa, and travelers who cross Ch'akul Tsa'kul have followed its curbs in the sand for days at a time.
