A Ki'ich'chik day is a meeting of counts. The [[affiliation-itzanpnthn|Itsani]] priesthood keeps four of them at once: the sun's year, the 260 days, the moon and the stars. A day takes its character from where they stand together. A farmer needs the first two, and from the temple he needs one answer: whether the day he means to sow on is open. The priests need all four. The astronomers of [[affiliation-ixilanchalix|Ish'ilan Ch'alish]] keep every one of them, because in their teaching the calendar is the goddess's own language, and every count kept on earth is an imperfect copy of the one she wrote at the creation.

The Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an of each city fixes its calendar. The solar priests of [[affiliation-tzenultqan|Ts'en'ul Tq'an]] keep the count of days and sell the auspicious ones. The observatories supply the reckoning that both depend on.

## The Ts'en Cycle {#tsen}

The _ts'en_ count is the sun's year of 365 days, and it governs the fields, the seasonal festivals and the ordinary round of work. It runs eighteen months of twenty days each, and then five days that belong to no month. The months are named for what the year is doing in them. The day of a month is counted from one to twenty, so the eighth of the war month is _Q'ishe Shok'alal_.

The year opens at the winter solstice, when the sun stands lowest. On that day the Ts'enmah climbs the pyramid and draws his own blood into the eternal flames, and every household rekindles its hearth from the temple fire. Each month closes with the Warden of the Debt announcing whether the city's blood-ledger stands in surplus or in deficit, so a Ki'ich'chik reckons the month by twenty days of work and one public verdict.

1. **Ch'ulikal**, the new. The year opens on its first day, the winter solstice.
2. **Ts'umaqal**, the cool nights.
3. **Ne'shukal**, the dry. The ground is cleared for sowing.
4. **K'ayikal**, the flames. The cleared fields are burned, and the smoke hangs over the valleys.
5. **Shikal**, the seed. The vernal equinox falls on its twelfth day, and the maize goes in on the days the temple has opened.
6. **Ne'al**, the water. The rains come.
7. **Ha'nakal**, the green.
8. **P'uqilal**, the thunderclouds.
9. **Ch'akunal**, the serpent, for the storm-serpent whose storms are hardest in it.
10. **Pakumal**, the sun blossom.
11. **K'inishal**, the sunlight. The summer solstice falls on its fourteenth day, and captives die on the pyramid's summit.
12. **K'ushal**, the maize ear.
13. **K'anishal**, the golden flower, for [[affiliation-kanixhakan|K'anish Ha'kan]]. The harvest festivals are held in it, when the temples feed everyone, slaves included.
14. **Ts'opilal**, the baskets. The harvest goes into the temple stores.
15. **Shok'alal**, the war. With the harvest in, the campaigning season opens.
16. **Ish'balal**, the jaguar. The autumnal equinox falls on its fifth day.
17. **Ts'uqilal**, the dead.
18. **Ishanal**, the sleep. The sun sinks toward the solstice.

## The Five Doors {#doors}

The five days at the year's end are _Shots'emal_, the doors. They belong to no month and to no god's festival, and the faith holds them days of terrible power, when the boundary between the worlds grows thin. Each has its own name:

- **Ts'amul**, the empty: the count of months has run out.
- **Ha'kik**, the cold.
- **Sh'an**, the hush.
- **Tq'al**, the edge.
- **Shots'ik**, the door, the last and the thinnest.

Living through them is a matter of keeping a fire. A household hearth must not go out during the five days. A family whose fire dies has opened a door, and a fire-priest of [[affiliation-kayikchalix|K'ayik Ch'alish]] must be fetched at once to close it. In practice the household sits up in turns, feeding the hearth through five nights, and a family short of firewood borrows it. The temple opens none of the five for planting, marrying, building or war, so the work of the year stops, and the city waits for the solstice with its doors shut and its fires high.

## The Tq'an Cycle {#tqan}

The _tq'an_ count runs 260 days. Twenty day-signs turn against the numbers one to thirteen, so that the days go _Pe' Ne'ik_, _Q'o Ch'umik_, _Lishe Umaq_, and onward. The thirteenth number falls on the thirteenth sign, and the fourteenth sign takes the number one again. No pairing of number and sign comes round a second time until all 260 have passed. The signs are short words, because they are said every day:

| Sign    | Meaning   | Sign    | Meaning |
| ------- | --------- | ------- | ------- |
| Ne'ik   | water     | Shak'il | eagle   |
| Ch'umik | wind      | Pik'il  | owl     |
| Umaq    | night     | Kulim   | stone   |
| Shikel  | seed      | Qatik   | blade   |
| Akun    | serpent   | P'uq    | storm   |
| Ts'uqik | death     | K'otsal | cord    |
| Amuk    | crocodile | Lanik   | road    |
| K'ush   | maize     | Ish'il  | star    |
| Tsulik  | reed      | Mel     | moon    |
| Balik   | jaguar    | K'ayil  | flame   |

The count is the frame against which the planets are read. Each of the visible planets belongs to a god, and the astronomers hold that they keep time with the 260 days. The Reckoner of Conjunctions computes where in the count each will meet another, and from the meetings he issues the auspicious and forbidden days to every other branch of the priesthood. No seed is planted, no war declared, no marriage consummated and no building begun on a day he has not opened. He publishes his conclusions and never his workings.

That rule reaches into every household. A family planning a wedding, a house or a journey pays the solar priests to find it an open day, and those fees are one of the solar branch's incomes. A Ts'enmah who means to make war waits on the same reckoning, and a war declared on a forbidden day is held lost before it is fought. Birth cannot wait on the count. The water-marking is given the hour a child is born, whatever the day, and the solar priesthood has never won the argument that it should be delayed. The day still marks the child, because a Ki'ich'chik name records the circumstances of a birth, and a child born on Balik often carries _Ish'bal_, the jaguar, among its names.

## The Round of Fifty-Two Years {#round}

The two counts meet again every fifty-two years, after 18,980 days. Each Ts'en year opens five signs further along the Tq'an count than the last, so only four signs ever open a year: Ne'ik, Ts'uqik, Shak'il and K'otsal. The number of the opening day climbs by one each year. A year is called by the day that opens it, and the present year, which opened at the winter solstice of 719, is _Olek' Pe' Ts'uqik_, Eleven Death.

When a year is due to open on _Pe' Ne'ik_ again, the round is complete and the [K'ayik Kul'al]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the Living Flame, is held. On Shots'ik, the last of the doors, the priests themselves put out every fire in the city. This is the one darkness the faith allows. The temples go dark, and the city keeps silence for a day and a night, believing that if the fire cannot be drawn again, Ts'en'ul Tq'an has withdrawn his sanction and the age is over. At sunrise on the solstice the Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an climbs the great pyramid with the highest fire-priests beside him, and the Master of the Living Flame works the obsidian mirrors and polished jade against the rising sun until the tinder catches. The Kindlers run the new fire to every quarter, and every hearth in the city is lit from that one spark.

The last Living Flame was drawn at the winter solstice of 670. The next falls at the winter solstice of 722, 1 [Kalmethandi]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} in [[lore-commoncal|the Vylarian Calendar]], and it closes the eightieth round of the Fifth Age.

## The Ts'uqal Cycle {#tsuqal}

The _ts'uqal_ count is the moon's month, the thirty days in which [[place-vaelith|Vaelith]] goes from dark to dark. It governs ritual timing and divination, and the worship of the dream and fertility gods. The Warden of the Moon-Houses keeps its record in the moon-houses of the observatory. Twelve moons fill 360 days, five short of the year, so each year the turnings fall five days earlier in the Ts'en months.

The count turns at the dark of the moon. At each turning the death-walkers of [[affiliation-tzuqilixbal|Ts'uqil Ish'bal]] hold the Calling of the Dead at the sacred pools, where the living speak the names of everyone lost since the last turning. The dream-temples of [[affiliation-naliktzuqal|Nal'ik Ts'uqal]] fill on the same dark nights, and the sealed codices of the serpent-keepers are inspected before witnesses from two other branches. The moon's months never line up with the Ts'en months for long, and the priesthood keeps the two apart.

## The Ish'ilan Cycle {#ishilan}

The _ish'ilan_ count is the year of the fixed stars. The stars rise on the same night in every year that has been counted, so the stellar year and the sun's year run the same 365 days. The astronomers keep them as separate counts all the same, one read from the sun's horizon and the other from the stars. The Master of the Monoliths checks the solstice stones against the record each year, and the stellar count carries the great festivals that mark the ages of the world.

It is also the count of the age itself. The astronomers number the years of the [Fifth Age]{.name data-type="lore/cosmology"} from the first winter solstice of its sun, and year 1 of the Fifth Age (FA) opened at the winter solstice of 3439 BF. The year that opened at the winter solstice of 719 AF is 4158 FA. The years before the Fifth Age (BFA) are counted backwards from it, though the priesthood holds that nothing of the four drowned, burned, shaken and scattered ages survived to be dated. The goddess is held to know where in this count the age will end, which is why Presiding Ch'alish Tq'ans have more than once ordered a prediction from the observatories suppressed.

## Reading a Date

A full date gives the Tq'an day, then the day and month of the Ts'en year, then the year of the Fifth Age: _Olek' Q'o Kulim, Q'ishe Shok'alal, 4158 FA_ is Twelve Stone, the eighth of the war month, in the year 4158, which is 14 [Elthezandi]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} 720 AF. The Tq'an day and the Ts'en day together name one day in fifty-two years, and the year of the age fixes which round.
