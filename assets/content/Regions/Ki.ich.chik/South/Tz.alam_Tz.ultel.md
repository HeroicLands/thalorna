## Overview

A llama-train coming up to the pass halts at a stone gate in the mist, and nothing goes through it until every bale has been opened, named and knotted on a cord. Ts'alam Ts'ultel, Spring Mountain, is the fortress of the highest pass through the mountain range that runs the length of [[affiliation-ixbaltzaku|Ish'bal Tsa'ku]]: about 15,000 people in a city cut into the living rock, commanding the road between the western valleys and the eastern ones. Every load that crosses the mountains passes it, and every load pays.

## The Fortress

The upper city sits on the saddle of the pass and is seen for a day's march in clear weather, which is rare: the pass is cool and wet, and cloud stands in it most mornings. The upper city is the smaller part. Behind it the fortress runs back into the mountain in galleries and chambers cut from the rock, level below level, where the storehouses, the armories and the great cisterns lie. The spring that names the place rises inside the mountain and fills the cisterns, and the fortress can hold out for years on what it keeps underground.

Two walls close the pass, one facing each way, with the city between them. A train enters by one gate, is counted in the court, and leaves by the other.

## Who Governs

The fortress has the realm's two halves, each with its own lord. The lord of the upper half, the Ts'ikmah, is always one of the Sun's kin, named by the Ts'enmah, and he commands the garrison and the pass. The present Ts'ikmah is [P'enqal Q'aptel]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a gray, exacting man who walks both walls twice a day. The lord of the lower half leads the kin groups who live in the fortress and serve the storehouses and the cisterns.

The garrison is mostly the labor turn. Each valley of the realm owes the fortress a number of men each year, counted by the heads of twenty households and sent up the road under their own Ts'obmah, and they serve their turn on the walls in steel breastplates from [[place-nekiahkul|Ne' Ki'ahkul]] before going home. A smaller standing garrison of professional soldiers, trained in mountain fighting and the defense of the gates, stays year-round and teaches each new levy its post. The two do not mix much.

## The Count of the Pass

A merchant declares his cargo at the gate. The cord-keepers knot each load, its owner and where it is bound, and the keepers of the storehouses take the toll in kind: a share of cacao, cloth, metal or feathers from each bale, which goes into the galleries for the Ts'enmah and the temples. The cords of the pass are read by the inspectors against the cords of the towns the trains came from, and a merchant whose load is lighter at the pass than it was at the market is asked where the rest went.

The toll makes the fortress rich, and almost none of the wealth stays in the upper city. Life there is plain and martial. The people are soldiers, their families, the storehouse keepers and a few merchants who sell to the garrison, and rank and standing in the garrison decide almost everything.
