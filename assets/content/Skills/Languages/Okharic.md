Okháric is a tongue of the Okháric (isolate) family. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Okháric, from the halting phrases of a traveler to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

## Overview

Okháric is the principal tongue of the [[affiliation-okharis|Kingdom of Okháris]] and surrounding communities in the northern fertile regions of [[place-xerathia|Xerathia]]. Spoken by approximately 5-7 million people, Okháric occupies a unique position in the continent's linguistic landscape as the bridge language between the Kheperian Empire to the south and the distant civilizations of the east.

Unlike its neighbors, Okháric has developed a robust oral tradition alongside written literature. Religious texts in a sacred archaic form are preserved and recited by trained priests, while vernacular speech continues to evolve with marked vitality. The language reflects centuries of trade, cultural contact, and adaptation, making it a living repository of regional history.

## Phonology

### Consonant System

Okháric employs a moderately-complex consonantal inventory influenced by areal contact with neighboring linguistic zones:

**Stops and Affricates:**

- Unvoiced: p, t, k
- Voiced: b, d, g
- Affricates: ch (TS), j (DZH)

**Fricatives:**

- Unvoiced: f, s, sh (as in "ship"), kh (as in Scottish "loch")
- Voiced: z, zh (as the "s" in "measure")

**Nasals:** m, n, ng (ŋ, particularly in final position)

**Liquids & Approximants:** l, r, w, y

**Characteristic Features:**

- A robust system of final nasals, particularly -ng and -m, marking grammatical classes
- The velar fricative /kh/ is distinctive and frequently occurs in both initial and medial positions
- Geminate consonants appear in certain morphological contexts, particularly plurals and intensives
- The affricate /ch/ occupies an intermediate position between stops and fricatives

### Vowel System

Okháric distinguishes five cardinal vowels, each occurring in short and long variants:

**Vowels:** a (short/long ā), e (short/long ē), i (short/long ī), o (short/long ō), u (short/long ū)

**Diphthongs:** Common sequences include ai, au, ei, oi, which tend to be produced as true diphthongs rather than vowel clusters

**Vowel Harmony:** Weak evidence of historical harmony; modern speech shows only vestigial traces, mostly in plural formation and diminutive morphology

### Phonotactic Patterns

Analysis of attested names reveals:

- **Initial Clusters:** Less common than in Vedyari; typically restricted to obstruent + liquid or fricative + stop combinations
- **Syllable Structure:** Predominantly CV; CVC structures appear frequently in medial positions
- **Final Consonants:** Commonly -n, -ng, -l, -r, -t, -s; final vowels are common, particularly in nominal classes marked for definiteness
- **Word Length:** Moderate; names are typically two to three morphemes, though some compounds extend to four or more syllables
- **Stress Patterns:** Generally penultimate; in longer compounds, secondary stress appears on the first syllable

## Grammar Notes

### Word Order

Okháric predominantly follows a **Subject-Verb-Object (SVO)** order in declarative main clauses, though postposed complements and topicalization create variation.

### Nominal System

- **Nouns** inflect for number (singular/plural), with some evidence of a historical dual
- **Gender:** A three-way system (masculine, feminine, neuter) is preserved in adjectives and some pronominal forms, though nominal gender marking has largely eroded
- **Definiteness:** Prefix and suffix markers indicate definite vs. indefinite status; demonstratives are frequently used to intensify definiteness
- **Case:** Archaic case endings are preserved in the sacred liturgical register; vernacular speech relies primarily on word order and prepositions

### Verbal System

- **Tenses:** Past, present, future; aspectual distinctions (perfective, imperfective, habitual) are productively marked
- **Mood:** Indicative, subjunctive, conditional, and imperative are all distinct
- **Voice:** Active voice is primary; passive constructions are less frequent but well-formed; reflexive and middle voice formations are marginal
- **Person/Number Agreement:** Verbs inflect for person (first, second, third) and number (singular, plural)

### Participles and Infinitives

- Participles are used attributively and predicatively; they readily nominalize into agent and patient nouns
- Infinitival forms exist but are less productive than in Vedyari; more commonly expressed through gerundial constructions

## Script & Literacy

**Traditional Script:** Okháric employs an abjadic consonantal script, [[skill-okhsshscrpt|Okh-sesh]], consisting of 23 basic characters, with diacritical marks indicating vowels. This system is ancient and is preserved in sacred texts and formal inscriptions. The consonantal base renders the script somewhat ambiguous, requiring context for correct interpretation.

**Hybrid Script ([[skill-okhsshscrpt|Okh-sesh-vowel]]):** A modified version adds explicit vowel markers, making it less ambiguous and more suitable for trade and administration. This system is now standard for mercantile use.

**Literacy:** True literacy is confined to the priesthood, professional scribes, and perhaps 20-35% of the nobility. Guildsmen in major urban centers may possess functional literacy – recognizing trade marks, numerals, and common abbreviations – but rely on scribes for contracts and record-keeping. Rural populations are almost entirely non-literate. The priesthood maintains exclusive knowledge of the oldest liturgical forms, which are recited rather than read, reducing the practical necessity for mass literacy of the sacred texts.

## Historical Development

### Proto-Okháric

Okháric is an isolate language with uncertain external relationships. Some scholars propose ancient connections to languages of the far south (possibly related to Nyáluban or other Central Xerathian languages), but evidence is sparse and speculative. Its isolate status has made it a focus of intense linguistic study, as it may represent a linguistic refuge population.

### Classical Period (Age of Kingdoms)

The standardization of Classical Okháric occurred approximately 1,500 years before present, during the period when the Kingdom of Okháris emerged as a major regional power. Sacred liturgical texts were fixed at this time and have been preserved with remarkable fidelity through oral tradition and written inscription. This classical register remains the prestige form and is taught to priests and scribes.

### Contact-Induced Change

Unlike Vedyari and Kheperi, which have maintained relatively conservative classical standards, Okháric has been subject to more rapid vernacular change due to its role as a trade language. Extensive contact with Kheperi, Vedyari, and maritime pidgins has introduced loanwords and structural innovations.

### Modern Dialects

Regional speech shows considerable variation:

- **Urban Okháric:** More conservative; maintains gender and some archaic case forms; influenced by liturgical registers
- **Rural Dialects:** More divergent; simplification of verbal morphology; loss of gender distinctions; greater borrowing from neighboring languages
- **Trading Post Dialects:** Extreme simplification; approaching creolized structures; significant code-switching

## Regional Dialects

### Northern Okháric (Heartland)

- Most conservative; closest to Classical Okháric
- All verbal tenses and moods are morphologically realized
- Gender distinctions are maintained
- Pronunciation emphasizes the distinctive /kh/ and final nasals

### Western Okháric (Borderlands & Trade Routes)

- Moderate innovation; some verbal simplification via auxiliaries
- Gender distinction weakening, particularly in adjectives
- More loanwords from neighboring languages
- Stress patterns increasingly variable

### Southern Okháric (Riverine & Agricultural Communities)

- More rapid innovation
- Verbal morphology increasingly simplified
- Gender largely lost except in pronouns
- Final nasals may be denasalized or simplified
- Considerable mutual influence with Kheperi

## Sample Phrases

1. **Okháré, né thalé** – _Greeting; literally "Okháric one, greetings"_
2. **Khátu en ónda** – _Blessing formula; "May the kingdom endure"_
3. **Sómba ren, lékha órung** – _Affirmation; "In the name of the ancestors, I stand firm"_
4. **Mákoro en zarûn** – _Trade expression; "May prosperity flow"_
5. **Tébu khuma shára en óta** – _Sacred oath; "Let the earth witness this truth"_

## Related Languages

Okháric maintains linguistic contacts with several neighboring languages:

- **Kheperi:** Ancient contact has left traces in sacred vocabulary and some archaic grammatical forms; modern contact is primarily through trade language
- **Vedyari:** More distant contact; some mutual intelligibility in trade pidgins, but limited direct borrowing
- **Maritime Pidgins:** Okháric has been both a substrate and source in the development of trade linguas

## Naming Traditions

### Cultural Significance

In Okháric tradition, names are deeply connected to ancestral identity, spiritual lineage, and clan affiliation. The bestowal of a name is often a ceremonial event overseen by elders or priests, linking the child to the community and to spiritual forces. Names are believed to carry the virtues and achievements of honored ancestors, creating a spiritual continuity between generations.

### Naming Patterns

**Given Names (Male):**

- Frequently incorporate geographical or clan references, particularly place names or territorial markers
- Many reference qualities valued in the culture: strength, courage, wisdom, endurance
- Often compound structures joining a descriptor with a suffix marking masculine gender or status
- Characteristic phonology includes prominent nasals and the velar fricative /kh/
- Examples: _Kwâmi_ (strength-bearer), _Ngoro_ (one of the river), _Táfari_ (traveler)

**Given Names (Female):**

- Similarly structured but often ending in open vowels or the feminine suffix -a or -i
- Many reference beauty, grace, fertility, or connection to nature
- Frequently incorporate diminutive suffixes, creating terms of endearment
- Examples: _Ádira_ (noble woman), _Kíma_ (beloved), _Nyóra_ (the river's gift)

**Clan or Tribe Names:**

- Typically reference the ancestral founder, a geographical feature, or a significant historical event
- Often elaborate compounds marking the relationship to a sacred site or spiritual principle
- May include honorific prefixes or suffixes marking the tribe's status or military prowess
- Examples: _Mbárazi_ (the gatherers), _Ngóma_ (drum-people, keepers of tradition), _Dómana_ (river-lords)

### Titles and Honorifics

Among the nobility and priesthood, extensive titulature is employed. These titles may reflect military rank, religious office, or exceptional accomplishment and are often prefixed to or substituted for personal names in formal contexts.
