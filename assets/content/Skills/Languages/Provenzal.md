Provenzal is a tongue of the [Pelwar Family]{.name data-type="lore"}. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Provenzal, from the halting phrases of a traveler to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

Provenzal is the melodic tongue of [[place-provenzrgn|Provenzia Region]], a realm where commerce meets artistry and the traditions of courtly culture flourish. Spoken in the prosperous merchant cities and cultivated courts of the southern coast, Provenzal is the language of síladors, merchants, and nobles who trade in beauty as readily as silk and spices.

The roman repertoire retains `q` and `Q` for the formal greeting's fixed que. This formula does not add a new sound to the ordinary palette.

## Overview

Provenzal speakers pride themselves on the liquid grace of their language. Where [[skill-vylarilng|Vylari]] is formal and structured, Provenzal is flowing and emotional. The language carries the influence of both its Vylari administrative heritage and the fey-touched [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] to the north, having absorbed melodic patterns and poetic vocabulary from both neighbors while maintaining its own distinctive character.

The language is inseparable from [[lore-provenzianclt|Provenzal]] culture: love songs and courtly poetry are composed in Provenzal, sung by traveling bards, and memorized by citizens of all classes. To speak Provenzal without grace is considered a personal failing.

The [[skill-provnzlng#lexicon|Provenzal Lexicon]] gives the words with their sources, the inherited and the learned side by side.

## How Provenzal sounds

Provenzal is soft and sung, the coast speech of courtly verse. Its consonants weaken between vowels, and its words end mostly on _-a_ or _-e_.

**The vowels.** _a_, _e_ and _o_ are open and full. A final _e_ is sounded, as the guide reads every vowel.

**The consonants.** _l_, _r_, _n_, _m_, _s_, _v_, _ch_ and _j_ carry the language. _ny_ stands where a softened _n_ falls before a vowel.

**Clusters.** Only gentle pairs meet: _lv_, _rv_, _lz_, _ndr_ and _str_. A word opens on one consonant or on _tr_, _pr_ or _br_.

**Endings.** Provenzal words end in _-a_, _-e_, _-el_, _-or_, _-ena_ or _-is_. Lyric and courtly words take _-eza_.

**What Provenzal leaves out.** It has no _th_ or _dh_, which soften to _t_ and _d_. It has no _kh_ or _gh_, no _zd_, and no _-os_ from [[skill-helonclng|Helonic Language]].

## Phonology

Provenzal is marked by a high proportion of sonorant consonants and open vowel patterns, giving it a naturally melodic quality:

**Consonants:** The language favors liquid consonants (l, r), nasals (m, n), and the voiced fricatives (v, z, j). Hard stops are softened in connected speech—intervocalic stops often become fricatives or nasals (Vylari _Arka_ becomes Provenzal _Arva_). The language shows no clear uvular fricatives or emphatic consonants. Word-final consonants are rare, often elided in casual speech.

**Vowels:** Provenzal employs five primary vowels with systematic distinction between open and close variants. Nasal vowels are phonemic, particularly in words borrowed from or influenced by Élavendri speech. Diphthongs are common (ai, ei, oi, au, eu, ou) and serve both phonetic and expressive purposes.

**Prosody:** The language favors a rising intonation at phrase boundaries, creating a naturally questioning, inviting quality. Stress is generally initial (FA-ris, AR-vel) but varies for poetic effect. Long vowels are expressed through vowel doubling or diphthongization, making the language highly flexible for verse composition.

**Distinctive Features:** Provenzal employs consonant lenition and assimilation freely—a trademark of the language's fluid character. The palatal fricative /j/ marks the boundary between formal and familiar speech (similar to Vylari's formal registers but less rigid). Regional variation is accepted and even celebrated; each city is known for its distinctive pronunciation patterns.

## Grammar Notes

**Word Order:** Provenzal employs a flexible Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order, allowing poetic inversion for effect. Object pronouns frequently precede verbs, creating a flowing, musical clause structure. This flexibility makes Provenzal ideal for poetry and song.

**Gender:** Provenzal maintains two primary genders (masculine and feminine) marked through article and adjective agreement. Neuter nouns have been largely absorbed into one of the primary genders over centuries of change. The gender system is somewhat irregular, reflecting the language's mixed heritage.

**Articles and Definiteness:** Provenzal employs a complex system of articles reflecting not just definiteness but also closeness to speaker (near/far/neutral distinctions). Some dialects employ four-way distinctions; others reduce this to two.

## Everyday expressions

_Jo okete korvatis_ means “I see the sun”; _jo tavete dokela_, “I hear a voice”; and _jo taurate ulmera_, “I keep water”. These concrete spoken utterances put the speaker first, then the action and its object. The copula in ordinary speech is _ez_: _jo ez revote_ means “I am free”. The poetic _en_ keeps its place in the songs below.

_Jo neltate neltis_ means “I smell a scent”; _jo tirmate tirmis_, “I taste a flavour”; _jo tolvate dechaze_, “I touch a hand”; and _jo zarvete zarvandon_, “I feel a bodily sensation”. _Jo silvate korvatis_ directs my gaze toward the sun; _jo silvarete korvatis_ watches it attentively. _De lizela ez talv daje neltis_ describes this flower as fragrant, with a pleasant scent. With _korete_ in place of _daje_, the scent is unpleasant. _De lizela demate daje okandon_ means “This flower seems good”, giving a good appearance rather than guaranteeing its quality.

In _jo taurate kremila_, “I feel anger”, the speaker keeps anger within; _jo taurate jaleza_ similarly expresses joy. These are particular ordinary feeling expressions. _Zarvete_ gives bodily sensation, while _taurate_ in these utterances names the feeling one harbors. _Vedamolis_ names felt guilt over one's own wrongdoing; the old _damolis_ keeps its place in the language of judgment.

_Netoron_ is romantic love, _ansevela_ affectionate friendship, _soreton_ the love of family, and _ultenora_ selfless love offered to all without seeking a return. Sexual lust is _bruzeza_. The song word _ansevirsa_ retains its lyric use. _Jervela_ is loving devotion; _jerval_ remains the duty owed to house, patron, city and clients.

_Avreton_ is contentment, peaceful ease with what one has. _Avandon tamber_ names the felt satisfaction of a desire fulfilled; _avandon ar tamber_ the frustration of a desire blocked or unfulfilled. _Dorandar avandon_ is ordinary longing for someone or something absent; _solmere_ retains the songs' ache for a person, place or time gone. _Jo taurate avandon tamber_ expresses the feeling of satisfaction, rather than merely reporting that a task has been completed.

_Varelon ol dunandon_ is confidence in one's ability. _Zedime varelon_ is certainty that a claim is true; the conviction may be mistaken. Truth itself is _varelza_. _Varelon_ also names faith or trust, the holding of a thing true. _Memrande ol orfenon_ is gratitude, grateful remembrance of a kindness received. _Ternandon_ is fear; _melvar ternandon_ overwhelming terror. _Velira_ is appreciative admiration and _melvar velira_ profound reverence. _Ternandon ul velira_ joins fearful wonder and admiration as awe.

In the feeling expressions _zelmira fa cho_ “pity for you”, _zelmira talv cho_ “sympathy with you”, and _zirnon talv cho_ “enmity with you”, the person concerned follows _fa_ or _talv_. A name can stand in place of _cho_. _Talv_ is the word for “with” preserved in the traditional formal greeting; _ol_ links a feeling to its object or source in the expressions given here.

_Jo pazate paza_ means “I eat food”; _jo vimate ulmera_, “I drink water”; _jo lotate_, “I sleep”; and _jo ez anlotandar_, “I am awake”. _Lorve_ is sleep itself, while _lotokandon_ is a dream experienced in sleep. _Jo ez talv pazavandon_ reports bodily hunger, and _jo ez talv vimavandon_ thirst. After enough food or drink, _jo ez pazele_ means “I am sated”. _Jo ez talv avandon ol tumera_ says “I am tired”, in need of bodily rest. With _melvar avandon_ in that expression, the need is overwhelming: the speaker is exhausted. The courteous ruin-word _umeltandar_ keeps its building sense.

_Jo zirvate ir_ means “I breathe in”; _jo zirvate fos_, “I breathe out”. In these breathing expressions _ir_ directs the breath inward and _fos_ outward. _Jo ar dunate zirvate_ reports suffocation when breath is cut off or the air is unbreathable: the speaker is physically unable to breathe. Here _ar_ negates the ability _dunate_, and _zirvate_ names the action that cannot be performed. It does not merely mean holding one's breath by choice. _Jo zarvete ritena_ means “I ache”, with the sufferer as subject; _jo ritenate cho_ means “I hurt you”, with the person hurt as object. _Jo taurate ritena_ expresses suffering bodily pain. _Jo linkate brudamis_ means “I bleed”, losing blood from the body. _Jo ez talv kelmeza ol karveza_ reports a fever, sickness accompanied by bodily heat.

_Jo olimate nurizon ir nurkovra_ means “I conceive a child in the womb”; _jo lozete nurizon ir nurkovra_, “I carry a child in the womb”, reports pregnancy. _Lozandon ol nurizon ir nurkovra_ names that condition. The womb phrase distinguishes pregnancy from carrying a born child in the arms. _Jo nurezate_ means “I give birth”; _jo sulmenate nurizon_, “I nurse a child at the breast”. These are distinct actions.

_Ankelmele_ is healthy, _kelmele_ sick, and _ritele_ wounded. _Okarun_, _tavarun_ and _valmarun_ name physical impairments of seeing, hearing and walking; a dark room, an inaudible sound or a temporary barrier does not establish those conditions. The fixed expression _nelve nizele_ describes mild cold, “cool”. _Ulmerele_ and _anulmerele_ distinguish wet and dry.

_Nurete ol semrote_ is a brother's son, a nephew; _nureta ol semrote_ is a brother's daughter, a niece. Substituting _semrota_ gives the sister's child. _Kambete ves kambeta_ includes either husband or wife as a spouse. _Soretis ol dumva_ names a household's kin as a family, rather than including unrelated servants. _Konare_ is an ordinary companion; _olvete_ retains the role of riding beside a patron. _Trumare_ can lead a common activity in peace as well as war, while _trumar_ remains a hired captain general. _Galete_ is a student without requiring the academy named in _galedo_.

_Sedon ol karalze_ is the physical hearth, the place of the house's kept fire. _Ombela tereme_ names a long seat for several people, a seating bench; _gardema_ retains the master's workshop place. _Tolbon_ is ordinary cloth and _lorrame_ the finest woven silk. A storage jar is _tolkemon ol kerandon_, a pot for holding things. _Kovra ol dumela_ names the room's overhead surface, its ceiling. _Funela melvar_ is a thick rope; _funela nizele_ a fine sewing thread.

_Sedon ol joreza_ names physical land or territory, whether owned or not, as distinct from water. Bare _jormalis_ keeps its owned-land meaning. The whole expression _jormalis levre_ conventionally names a flat open plain, whether owned or not; that geographical use belongs to the phrase. _Joreza ol salkon_ names sand, including sand away from a shore; _joreza ul ulmera_ is the conventional name for mud, earth mixed with water. _Sirete_ is an ordinary river and _sirete nizele_ a small stream; _vilare_ remains the great river. _Lamote ol kaldere_ calls a tree's limb a branch. As weather reports, _ar uradon_ means “no cloud”, clear weather, and _ez talv uradon_ means “it is cloudy”, with clouds in the sky.

_Urkis_, _tavemer_ and _karzis_ count one, two and three; _arzun_ counts zero. _Olime_, _etore_ and _karzore_ name the first three positions. For later positions, _de ombela ez ir sedon ol raetur_ means “This chair is in position seven”, seventh in the sequence being arranged. _De ombela ez ir sedon ol zorimas_ puts it in position eight. The number after _ol_ gives the numbered position; these expressions do not add an ordinal ending. _Solte ol solte_ is a quarter, half of a half. _Tavemer zedime_ means both, all two; _urkis ol tavemer_ either of the two, and _ar urkis ol tavemer_ neither.

In comparing the two speakers' shares, _jo kerate tolkemon mazeron; cho kerate tolkemon nizeron_ means “I have more pots; you have fewer pots”. With _ulmera_ in place of _tolkemon_, the comparison concerns more or less water, an amount rather than a count. _Zolme tolkemon_ is many pots and _itre tolkemon_ few; _zolme ulmera_ is much water and _itre ulmera_ little. In that same comparison, _jo kerate zolmimme tolkemon_ assigns me the greatest count of pots, while _cho kerate itrimme tolkemon_ assigns you the smallest. _Zolmimme ulmera_ and _itrimme ulmera_ compare the greatest and smallest amounts of water. These quantities differ from _tereme_, long in extent. _Rozeti tavemer_ is about two and _zetai tavemer_ exactly two.

_Jo kerate ombela_ means “I have a chair”; _jo kurete ombela_, “I take a chair”; and _jo lendate ombela_, “I put a chair in place”. _Jo tambate ombela_ makes or creates a chair. _Jo tavate_ means “I act; I do something”, without making a particular object. _Jo demate ulmera_ gives water in an ordinary exchange; _daimate_ retains the patron's giving. _Dumva ez_ means “A house exists; there is a house”: the named thing comes before _ez_, with no identity or quality complement in this particular existential utterance. _Ombela ez_ similarly states that a chair exists. _De ombela ez ol jo_ means “This chair belongs to me”; _cho_ can replace _jo_ for “belongs to you”.

_Jo lokate fir dumva_ means “I arrive at the house”; the destination follows _fir_. _Jo tigate ir_ means “I go in”, entering; _jo tigate fos_, “I go out”, exiting. _Jo valmate zel_ means “I crawl”, moving low along the ground. _Jo lozete us_ lifts something upward. _Jo nembelate irkon_ means “I drop a cup”, releasing the cup so that it falls. _Jo kermate zetimai_ destroys something by breaking it completely. _Jo ralvate fos sedon ol ritavon_ means “I flee from the place of danger”. _Jo tigate fos sedon ol dumva_ means “I leave the house”, departing its place; _linkate_ retains letting go or releasing. These are the particular movement and action expressions given here.

_Jo dokate_ means “I speak”; _jo klamete_, “I call out; I shout”; and _jo anklamete_, “I whisper”, using quiet breathy speech without voicing. _Narthen_ is “tell”, the verb named in the merchant's invitation below; that invitation keeps its attested imperative. _Jo demate arlen_ gives a name, naming someone or something. _Jo memrate orfenon_ expresses thanks, openly acknowledging a kindness rather than merely remembering it privately. _Jo varelate talv cho_ means “I agree with you”, sharing your judgment. _Jo ar avete_ refuses an offered proposal, withholding consent. _Jo mazeron avete ulmera ves jentize_ means “I prefer water to wine”, wanting the first option more than the second.

_De ulmera ez silkre_ says this water is transparent; _de tumisa ez silkre_ says this mark is visually distinct and easy to discern. These are two uses of _silkre_: seeing through a material, and seeing a clear outline or mark. _Jo vakate ritavon_ is a threat when the speaker signals their own intention to cause harm to another. An innocent warning of an external danger does not use this conventional threatening sense.

_Jo ez ir de sedon_ means “I am here”, in this place; _cho ez ir at sedon_ means “You are there”, in that place. _De ombela ez lair morvalis_ puts this chair beside the table; _de ombela ez pral morvalis_ puts it in front of the table. _Jo tigate fir sedon telvare_ goes up toward a higher place, and _jo tigate fir sedon zele_ down toward a lower place. _Jo tigate fos sedon ol dumva_ leaves from the house; _jo tigate fir veramis_ goes toward the city. _Fir_ names a destination and _fos sedon ol_ a source in these examples. _Teredon tavir dumva ul veramis_ is the distance between house and city, with the two measured places on either side of _ul_.

_Jo marendate ir kanel ol cholsama_ means “I learn during the harvest”: the event follows _ol_, after “in the time of”. _Pral kanel ol cholsama, jo marendate_ puts that learning before the harvest. _Ker cho lotate, jo marendate_ means “While you sleep, I learn”, placing the two events at the same time. _Jo sistete fir kanel ol cholsama_ waits until the harvest as endpoint. _Jo ternate ir dumva fos kanel ol cholsama_ means “I live in the house since the harvest”, naming the starting event after _ol_. These concrete temporal utterances use the listed action forms.

In counting occasions, _kanel_ is a time when an action occurs. _Jo vimate ulmera urkis kanel_ counts one occasion of drinking water, once; _jo vimate ulmera tavemer kanel_ counts two, twice. _Zolme kanel_ repeats it on many occasions; _antigai_ merely says “again”, without an exact count. _Ir zolme kanel_ is often, _ir itre kanel_ rarely, _ir etris kanel_ sometimes, _ir zedime kanel_ always, and _ir arurkis kanel_ never. _De jhorne_ is today; _pral de jhorne_ yesterday; _etor jhorne_ tomorrow. _Olimai jo tigate; etorai jo lokate; ortemai jo sistate_ means “First I go; next I come; finally I stop”. _Pral denkanai_ reports “already”, accomplished before now; _fir denkanai_ is “yet”, up to now, and _ar ... fir denkanai_ “not yet”.

_Jo okete vetis_ means “I see myself”; _cho okete vetis_, “You see yourself”. In these reflexive examples _vetis_ refers back to the subject of that same utterance. _Dene lokate_ means “He comes”; _dena lokate_, “She comes”; _denoi lokate_, “They come”; _enoi lokate_, “We come”. The reference words here are used in their listed forms. _Ombela ol jo_ is my chair and _ombela ol cho_ your chair. _De ombela ez ol enoi_ means “This chair is ours”. The near article _de_ and distant article _at_ point out their following nouns: _de ombela_, this chair, and _at ombela_, that chair.

_Eza cho vimate ulmera?_ asks “Do you drink water?” _Eza kedis lokate?_ asks “Who comes?” _Eza ketis ez de tamba?_ asks “What is this work?” _Eza kete ombela ez ol cho?_ asks “Which chair is yours?” _Eza de dumva ez ir kete sedon?_ asks “Where is this house?”, in which place. _Eza cho lokate ker?_ asks “When do you come?” _Eza cho tigate oleti ketis?_ asks “Why do you go?”, because of what. _Eza cho tavate sa ketis?_ asks “How do you act?”, in what manner.

_Eza cho kerate kete rozon ol tolkemon?_ asks “How many pots do you have?”, what number of pots; _eza cho kerate kete zolmon ol ulmera?_ asks “How much water do you have?”, what amount of water. _Eza de ombela ez ol kedis?_ asks “Whose chair is this?” _Eza cho felzate lambize fir kedis?_ asks “To whom do you send a word?” _Eza cho lokate fos kete sedon?_ asks “From where do you come?” _Eza cho marendate fa ketis?_ asks “For what purpose do you learn?” _Eza cho dokate ketis?_ asks the other speaker to clarify: “What do you mean; what are you saying?” The questions retain ordinary subject, action and object placement.

_Ez varelin_ answers yes, “It is true”; _ar ez_ answers no, “It is not so”. _Varlai ez_ emphatically confirms “Indeed”, while _eza varlai?_ asks “Really?” _Sa cho dokate, de dumva ez soltere_ means “According to what you say, this house is large”: it reports your claim without verifying it. _Sa okandon_ qualifies an apparent truth, as things look. _Jo dokate talv ar chesdande_ offers a guess, a tentative answer without knowing. _Jo lendate varelon: de dumva ez soltere_ sets that claim down as an assumption or premise. _Setdokandon_ is an intentional lie; _setdochemon_ is an untrue assertion that may instead be a mistake. Neither is mere lack of confidence.

_Jo tigate ul cho lokate_ means “I go and you come”. _Jo ar tigate ul cho ar lokate_ negates both events: “I do not go, nor do you come”. _Jo sistete oleti cho lokate_ means “I wait because you come”. _Zai cho lokate, ta jo sistete_ means “If you come, then I wait”. In _zai cho ar lokate, ta jo tigate_, “Unless you come, I go”, the condition is your not coming and the consequence is my going. _Zai denon ar ez ta jo tigate_ gives the alternative consequence: “If this is not so, then I go; otherwise I go”.

_Narti cho lokate, jo tigate_ means “Although you come, I go”; _nartir ritavon, jo tigate_, “Despite danger, I go”. _Cho lokate; nartai jo tigate_ says “You come; however, I go”. _Jo avete tigate; aletai jo sistete_ says “I want to go; instead, I wait”. _Jo marendate ta jo dunate tavate_ gives a purpose: “I learn so that I can act”. In these examples _ta_ introduces either a condition's consequence or a purpose, with each complete clause giving its own subject and action.

_Ves jo tigate ves jo sistete_ means “Either I go or I wait”; _jo ar tigate ul jo ar sistete_, “Neither do I go nor do I wait”; _ul jo tigate ul cho lokate_, “Both I go and you come”. _Jo ar chesdate zai dene lokate ves dene ar lokate_ means “I do not know whether he comes or does not come”, leaving both alternatives open. The subjects and predicates in these complete examples can be replaced by the actual alternatives under discussion. _Denon ez_ introduces a restatement, “That is”; _dokate arlen_ an exact identification, “Namely”; and _sa denon_ an example.

_Jo dunate tigate_ means “I can go”, while _jo ar dunate tigate_ denies ability. _Jo jestate tigate_ means “I have permission to go”. _Jo jestate tigate naren jo ar dunate tigate_ states permission alongside inability: “I may go, but I cannot go”. With a proposed departure, _cho tigate_, already under discussion, _jo jesterate cho_ grants you permission for it; _jo anjesterate cho_ forbids you to make it. The person receiving the permission or prohibition is the object of those two particular verbs. _Denon zikai dunate tambirete_ says “That might happen”, an uncertain possibility rather than permission.

_Jo jervete tigate_ means “I must go”, an obligation. _Jo jervete ar tigate_ means “I must not go”: the obligation is to refrain from going. _Jo ar jervete tigate_ instead denies an obligation to go. The old _orfeate_ retains owing a favor. _Cho dajvete tigate_ advises “You ought to go”, without compulsion. _Denon ez jestare ul ar jerpele_ describes an option that is permitted and not required. _Ar jestare_ is used for an actual prohibition, not simply for the absence of an express permission. _Talv ar verdavis_ is the conventional stronger no-worth description, worthless; a thing of modest value still has worth.

The traditional formal greeting _Brave jhorne genthe korthe. que la preja soie talv vei_ is used as a whole: “Good day, noble companion; may peace be with you”. _Preja talv cho_ wishes peace as an ordinary goodbye. At the door, a host's _cho ez dumvar_ welcomes the other person as a guest under the roof. _Jo avete orfenon_ asks a kindness, “Please”, before a particular request. _Jo memrate orfenon ol cho_ thanks you for your kindness; _cho ar orfeate_ answers that no favor is owed in return. _Cho linkate jo_ politely asks to be left room or allowed to pass, “Excuse me”. _Jo taurate antambeza_ says “I am sorry”, holding regret for what happened. _Cho anorfenate jo_ asks “Forgive me”, with the speaker as the person to be forgiven.

The fixed attention calls _cho tavrelate_, _cho silvate_, _cho sistete_ and _cho sistate_ ask “Listen!”, “Look!”, “Wait!” and “Stop!” _Cho lokate fir de sedon_ asks “Come here!”; _cho tigate fos de sedon_, “Go away!”; _cho zumbrate jo_, “Help me!” _Cho dokate antigai_ asks for the words again, “Pardon?” These are the listed spoken requests; the merchant invitation below retains its own _narthe_. _Jo lambirete_ says “I understand”, and _jo ar lambirete_ “I do not understand”. _Eza cho dokate denon sa ketis?_ asks how to say the expression being referred to; _eza arlen ol denon ez ketis?_ asks what the referred-to thing is called.

_Ziromon_ is a general arrangement; _zirotal_ keeps its precedence and room-order sense. _Konalon ol zolme sortalon_ names a tribe as an association of clans. _Orfenon_ includes the social debt of a kindness, while _nezora_ is a debt in money. _Zolme frenche_ names wealth, much property or means; _itre frenche_ conventionally names poverty, lacking enough to live on. _Laton kelterete_ is a secret matter in general. _Pelzandon ol jentamis_ conventionally names a change of form, a transformation; _tamba ol oleton_ the effect brought about by a cause, including a resulting state as well as a physical product. Felt satisfaction remains _avandon tamber_, distinct from _zedimandon_, completion of an act.

## Script & Literacy

Provenzal is written in the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Semaron]], the Vylarian alphabet, which is the common script of [[place-aureldirgn|Aureldia]] and is used across the region by tongues that share no descent with one another. Local scribal traditions have developed distinctive letterforms, and a flowing, decorative hand is conventional for poetry and formal documents.

The borrowing shows where the letters run out. The Semaron sets one sign to one sound and has no sign for a nasal vowel, so Provenzal's phonemic nasal vowels arrived with nothing of their own to be written in. Provenzal scribes set the imperial curl above a vowel to show a nasal; the curl belongs to the Semaron spelling, and the transliteration on these pages writes no nasal vowels. A Vylarian reading a Provenzal page therefore reads every nasal as a long vowel, which is the most frequent misreading in cross-border correspondence and the reason a careful Provenzal clerk spells a nasal out in full whenever the letter is bound for an imperial reader.

True literacy is confined to the clergy, professional scribes, and perhaps a third of the nobility (courtly culture values refinement, and literate lords are admired, but many still rely on personal secretaries). Provenzia's commercial orientation means that merchant guildsmen often possess functional literacy—enough to recognize trade marks, prices, and simple labels—but even wealthy traders rely on scribes and solicitors for contracts, correspondence, and formal documents. The vast majority of the population cannot read or write. The language's close association with poetry and song means that many non-literate speakers can recite and compose in Provenzal through oral tradition alone.

## Élavendri Influence

Provenzal's distinctive character owes much to centuries of contact with [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] speakers along the northern border. This influence is visible in three domains:

**Borrowed Vocabulary:** Provenzal has absorbed Élavendri words wholesale in the domains of art, emotion, magic, and nature—areas where the Pelwar southern tongues had limited vocabulary. Key borrowings include:

_tel_ (light, beauty—from Élavendri _thel_)
: used in poetry and courtly speech where the native Pelwar _lucherne_ sounds too prosaic

_sil_ (song, enchantment—from Élavendri _-síl_)
: the sílador tradition uses this word rather than the Pelwar _chanvor_

_daren_ (memory, deep-knowing—from Élavendri _daren_)
: no Pelwar equivalent exists for this concept of embodied memory

_ael_ (light, radiance—from Élavendri _-ael_)
: appears in personal names and poetic compounds: _aelcore_ "radiant heart," _aelvois_ "bright voice"

_thal_ (shadow, mystery—from Élavendri _-thal_)
: used in literary and magical contexts: _thalnuit_ "shadow-night," _thalrev_ "mystery-dream"

_ve_ (truly, in truth—from Élavendri particle _ve_)
: an emphatic particle used in oaths and poetry

**Phonological Traces:** The Élavendri nasal vowels entered Provenzal through these borrowings and spread to native words by analogy—this is the origin of Provenzal's distinctive nasalization, which sets it apart from Vylari and [[skill-tarvenlng|Tarveni]].

**Poetic Grammar:** Provenzal poetry freely employs Élavendri word order (VSO) and the Élavendri copula _en_ alongside native Provenzal _ez_. The use of _en_ in verse is considered elevated and archaic—a sílador who can weave Élavendri phrasing into Provenzal song demonstrates mastery of both traditions.

## Historical Development

Provenzal developed from Proto-Pelwar stock through a different path than Vylari, maintaining more direct contact with the fey-speaking populations to the north. The language absorbed significant Élavendri vocabulary, particularly in domains of art, emotion, and magic. The resulting blend created a tongue that is simultaneously familiar to Vylari speakers (allowing trade and diplomacy) and distinctly its own.

Unlike Vylari's imperial standardization, Provenzal evolved through artistic and commercial competition among city-states. This led to a language more resistant to formal regulation—there is no official "High Provenzal" equivalent to Vylari's imperial register.

## Regional Dialects

**Cotiere (Urban Coast):** The prestige dialect of major trading cities like [[place-jalnazdal|Jalnasdal]] and [[place-kalveza|Kalveza]]. The most standardized form, used in literature and formal commerce. Shows greatest Vylari influence.

**Montanhe (Mountain Interior):** Spoken in inland regions such as [[place-palvothe|Palvote]] and smaller highland towns. Preserves archaic vowel patterns and shows greater Élavendri influence. More nasal vowels and different stress patterns than coastal speech.

**Parlage dou Port (Port Dialect):** A pidgin-like simplification used between multilingual merchants, showing greatest mixture of Vylari and Élavendri elements. Technically a register rather than a dialect.

## Sample Phrases

"L'athor en le tel de l'anvre."
: "Love is the light of the soul." (From a famous sílador poem; note the Élavendri borrowing _tel_ "light/beauty" preferred over native _luerne_, and the poetic copula _en_ from Élavendri rather than native _ez_.)

"Brave jhorne genthe korthe. que la preja soie talv vei."
: "Good day, noble companion; may peace be with you." (Formal greeting; _jhorne_ "day," _preja_ "peace," _soie_ "be" (from _evren_ "to be"), _talv_ "with." Pure Provenzal—no Élavendri borrowings in everyday speech.)

"Me vei athore d'un aelcore verdhen."
: "I love you with a sincere radiant heart." (Courtly declaration; _aelcore_ is a hybrid compound—Élavendri _ael-_ "radiance" + Provenzal _core_ "heart." This kind of compound is characteristic of the sílador register.)

"Narthe-me de vei thraves!"
: "Tell me of your travels!" (Invitation to conversation, common in merchant settings; _narthe_ imperative of _narthen_ "to tell," _thraves_ "travels." Plain commercial Provenzal, no Élavendri influence.)

"Le sil en daren, le thalrev en tel."
: "Song is memory, shadow-dream is light." (High poetic register, heavily Élavendri-influenced; _sil_ "song/enchantment," _daren_ "deep-knowing," _thalrev_ "shadow-dream," _tel_ "light"—all borrowed from Élavendri. The copula _en_ and balanced structure echo Élavendri poetic forms.)

## Related Languages

Provenzal occupies a unique position among Pelwar languages as a bridge between the imperial Vylari tradition and the fey-touched Élavendri north. It shares fundamental Pelwar grammar with Vylari and [[skill-tarvenlng|Tarveni]], but has absorbed so much Élavendri vocabulary and phonetic influence that mutual intelligibility is limited. Speakers of Provenzal and Élavendri often find common ground in poetic expression, though everyday conversation remains challenging.

The relationship to [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal Language]] and [[skill-varokhlng|Varokhi Language]] is more distant; northern speakers find Provenzal almost incomprehensibly soft, while Provenzal speakers consider northern tongues harsh and inexpressive.

## Naming Traditions

Provenzal names reflect the culture's emphasis on beauty and emotional resonance. Given names are often chosen for their melodic quality as much as their meaning. Double names (Pál-ara, Bór-vel) are common among nobility, each part carrying meaning or honoring ancestors.

Provenzal naming practices show greater acceptance of variation and personal choice than other Pelwar-speaking regions. A person might be known by multiple names in different contexts—a formal name, a merchant's name, and a sílador's name are all acceptable. Married women commonly retain their birth names rather than adopting their husband's, a practice unique among the [[place-midhalnrgn|Mídhalión cultures]].

The tradition of "love names" is widespread: a person might grant an intimate friend or lover a special private name not used in public. Using a love name in public is a serious breach of intimacy.

Élavendri influence is visible in Provenzal naming—the morphemes _-ael_ (light/radiance), _th-_ (shadow/mystery), and _-aen_ (flowing/becoming) appear frequently in given names, particularly in the northern Nordavene tradition where Élavendri contact is strongest. A name like _Lánael_ or _Brénael_ marks a family that values the fey-touched poetic tradition; a name like _Thalyra_ or _Tharyn_ carries connotations of mystery and depth.

## Provenzal Lexicon {#lexicon}

In a merchant's house in [[place-jalnazdal|Jalnasdal]], a boy of twelve is learning to say the same thing twice. His tutor gives him a word, _nasdal_, the court where his father's suits are heard, and then its learned twin, _nasdalo_, the court of record the notaries write in; then _docheme_, a speech, and _dochemo_, an oration of the kind the academies teach. By the end of the lesson he can hear which of the two a man has chosen, and why, and what it says about him. That is most of what a Provenzian education is for.

This is the word-hoard of Provenzal, the tongue of the Provenzians. It is the daughter of ancient Vylari that changed most in its sounds. It made articles of all three of the old pointing words, softened its consonants between vowels and let the `n` between two vowels fall, leaving the vowel before it nasal. It has taken words twice from outside. The empire's law books gave it a learned layer, so that many words stand in two forms, the inherited one and the one the notaries took back. The fey-touched north gave it the words for light, dream, longing and song, which is why its lyric sounds like no other tongue's.

## Sounds and spelling {#sounds-and-spelling}

Its words are written in the setting's one guide; see [[doc-prnncthlrn|Pronouncing Thalorna]].

The nasal vowels are the tongue's own. Wherever the ancient tongue set an `n` between two vowels, the `n` fell and left the vowel before it nasal: the ancient _yunakos_, a recruit, is _joache_, said in three syllables. The words borrowed from Élavendri, whose own nasals are written with an `n`, reinforced the sound.

## The learned words {#the-learned-words}

The empire's law books, its coins and its University gave Provenzal a second layer of words, taken from imperial Vylari as it is written and not as it was inherited. A learned word keeps its consonants hard between vowels and keeps a final _-o_ where the inherited word lost its ending, so the two forms of one root stand side by side: _nasdal_ and _nasdalo_, _marendal_ and _marendalo_. The inherited word belongs to the street and the household, the learned one to the notary and the academy.

## The words from the north {#the-words-from-the-north}

Provenzal had little to say about light, dream, longing or the uncanny, and it borrowed what it lacked from [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] across the northern border. The loans came with the songs, and the lyric is still where they live.

## Words {#words}

### The city and its standing

A Provenzian names his city before his family, and the city words are the oldest civic vocabulary in the tongue, inherited from the speech of [[lore-pelwarmigr|the Migration]] and worn smooth by a thousand years of council meetings. _Veramis_ is a city, a walled place of citizens, and it is the word a Provenzian means when he says where he is from. The council that governs it, whether of nobles, of rich citizens or of a guild, is _kurtema_, and the square where it does its business in sight of everyone is _rembalis_.

Every office in this field ends in _-e_, the old agent ending, and the same offices exist in imperial Vylari with the learned ending _-o_. The native word names the city's officer, and the learned one the empire's.

### Patron and client

The relation that organizes a Provenzian life is patron and client, and its words are inherited whole. A patron is _daimela_, a word that ends like a feminine one but names a man, as it did in the ancient tongue; his patronage is _daimelal_, and to give as a patron gives is _daimate_. The client's attendance at the patron's door at first light is _pramolza_, the morning call, and a city's streets are full of men making it.

A man with no patron is _anzirote_, unplaced, which in Provenzia is not a description of freedom but of exposure. The debts a client runs up are _orfeate_, to owe, and its learned cousin _nezora_, a debt in money, is a different thing entirely: a Provenzian can repay the second.

### Love and its conduct

Courtly love is a code of conduct, and its vocabulary is borrowed from the north and from the law in roughly equal parts. A lover's vow is _tolmesa_, an Élavendri word; the native _tolmeza_ is the same word as it came down in the south, and it means a vow made before a notary. A _tolmesa_ cannot be sued on, and that is its point.

The ache of absence, the longing for a person or a place or a time that is not coming back, is _solmere_, which in Élavendri means the smell of rain on its way. Provenzal took the word for its weather and made it a feeling, and it is the word every lyric in the tongue is built around.

### Song and the síladors

The great Provenzal lyric is sung by a sílador, and most of the words for what he does came from the north. The song itself, _olnare_, the turn of a long song, _aurente_, and the tune he hums before he finds the words are Élavendri, and so is the singer: _sourime_, the Élavendri word, stands beside the native _sorime_, a poet who writes for the page. The difference is the one the tongue cares about. A poet makes a thing a patron can own; a singer makes a thing that exists only while he is singing it.

Rhetoric is the skill the culture rewards most, and its words are inherited and learned at once. An oration is _docheme_, an orator _doche_, and the learned forms _dochemo_ and _dochando_ belong to the academies, where a merchant's son learns to tell the two apart.

### The house and the street

Provenzia's cities are marble and aqueduct and amphitheater, half of them inherited from the empire and half built since out of trade money, and the words for what stands in them are inherited too. A family of standing lives in a _tevaime_, a townhouse on a good street, and summers at its _komrela_ in the hills; the house is the public record of the family, and its upkeep is not a private matter. A building nobody has repaired is _umeltandar_, resting, the courteous word for a ruin, and the one the Provenzians use of the empire's buildings.

### The gods and the hearth

Provenzians keep the gods' festivals and endow their temples, and a wealthy man funds a shrine as readily as a school, for the same reason. The priest is _sarcheande_, the keeper of a god's house, and the household's own gods, _karalzis_, the ones of the kept fire, have a niche by the door of every house in the tongue's country.

### Learning and the academies

Education is valued in Provenzia and the valuing is sincere, and the academy words came in twice: once inherited, once borrowed back from the empire's University. _Marendis_ is wisdom and _marendal_ a school, the native forms. The academies at the great cities use the learned _marendalo_ and _zetmarendalo_, the University, and a family that can afford it puts a son through one whatever he means to do afterward.

Magic is taught there too. The word is _tordima_, and it names the art as the fey-touched north practices it as well as the art taught in the academies.

### The house, kin and the name

A Provenzian's house is the reputation of his name, not the building, and it comes first of everything he owes. The founder of a house is _derte_, a word that was once hallowed. A married woman keeps her birth name, which is why the tongue has a word, _arleis_, for a house name carried into another house, and a man's earned name, _kiramarle_, is given to him by his city after a deed and not by his family.

### Law and the written act

Every city has its own courts and none of them agree, so the law words are many and paired. The native words are what a Provenzian says in the street: _nasdis_ a judgment, _krezul_ a suit, _semnalis_ a contract. The learned words are what the notary writes, taken from the empire's law books: _nasdalo_, a court of record, _vornalo_, the written law. A merchant who cannot use both is at the mercy of his notary.

### The market and the counting-house

Provenzia is rich and clever and convinced that being both is a moral achievement, and the money words are the busiest in the tongue. The moneylender, _fezume_, is a respected man with a seat on most councils, and interest, _fezumal_, is discussed at dinner as openly as the weather. The coins are imperial and so are their learned names. The scale is _tradina_, a learned word, because the honest scale is something a city guarantees and not something a man keeps at home.

### Craft and the workshop

Provenzia's craftsmen are better paid and better regarded than any west of them, and their words are inherited and exact. A craft is _palmeza_, learned at a master's bench, _gardema_, and the master is _garde_, the one who holds a bench and may take an apprentice, _kolmete_. To keep a craft's secret is _keltate_, the same verb a lover uses for discretion.

### Vine, field and river

The country around each city is vineyard and orchard and the river that carries the wine down to the harbor. The vineyard is _gurimal_; the harvest _cholsama_. The great river is _vilare_, which in the ancient tongue was hallowed, and still takes the god's ending here although no Provenzian thinks of the river as one.

### The harbor and the sea

Half the cities of Provenzia stand on river mouths and harbors, and the sea words came down from the empire's fleet. The harbor is _volchema_ and a sail _lentize_; the steady northerly wind of late summer that closes the sailing year is _aistroi_.

### Arms and the field

The cities fight one another often and briefly, with hired troops and short memories, and the war words are the empire's legion words grown small. _Trameze_, a legion in the ancient tongue, is a city's sworn company; _trumar_, a general, is whoever the council hires to lead it this season.

### The body, life and death

Appearance is the public record of a family's standing, and the body words that matter are the ones that show: _jentamis_, a face, and _tolvema_, a cloak, the old citizen's gown that a Provenzian of standing still wears to council. Death is _cholvazis_, said as seldom as it can be; the Élavendri _selvore_ is the word for it in a song.

### Light, dream and the fey north

Where the southern tongue had little to say, it borrowed from the north, and nearly every word for light, dream and the uncanny is Élavendri. The lyric is full of them. _Áulira_, light, became _aulira_, and _sírnaste_, a thin place, became _sirnaste_.

### Time and the year

The calendar is the empire's, counted from its founding, and the festivals are every city's own. _Lordeme_ is a festival, and every city keeps its own.

### Qualities

The qualities a Provenzian of standing claims are _solvime_, weighty, and _zeote_, wise.

### Doing, going and being

A verb is cited by its infinitive, commonly ending in _-ate_ or _-ete_ in native words. _Zeotadora_ and _solchadora_ keep their listed forms. The motion and state auxiliaries, the copula and the rest stand with the grammar words.

### Numbers and grammar words

The three pointing words of the ancient tongue all became articles, and a Provenzian chooses among them as a speaker of another tongue chooses a tone of voice: _de_ for what is near and his own, _te_ for what is the listener's, and _at_ for what is far, famous or past. The copula is _ez_. These small words wore down, and stand here with the ancient forms they came from.

## Attested names {#attested-names}

Every name in the scope of the Provenzal tongue—the Provenzians, their cities, valleys and offices, the tongue itself—with every note where it is met. A name in the reader's tongue is listed so that the register is complete, and it is not built. A Provenzal name whose **Built from** cell shows `—` is one the lexicon does not yet make.

### Tongues

```sql
SELECT '`' || tag || '`' AS "Tongue", meaning AS "Meaning"
FROM tongues WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-provnzlng' ORDER BY position
```

### Names

```sql
SELECT '**' || name || '**' AS "Name",
       coalesce(array_to_string(list_transform(notes, n -> '[[' || n || '|' || name || ']]'), ', '), '—') AS "Notes",
       '`' || tongue || '`' AS "Tongue",
       coalesce(array_to_string(list_transform(built, x -> '`' || x || '`'), ' + '), '—') AS "Built from"
FROM register WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-provnzlng' ORDER BY position
```
