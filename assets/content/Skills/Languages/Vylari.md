Vylari is a tongue of the Pelwar family. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Vylari, from the halting phrases of a traveler to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

Vylari is the imperial tongue of the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian Empire]] and the most widely spoken of the [[lore-pelwarpepl|Pelwar People]] languages. It serves as the common tongue of trade, law and administration across much of Mídhalión, spoken in the great cities of [[place-alyssa|Alyssa]], [[place-hylen|Hylen]], [[place-moktur|Moktur]] and [[place-salkhalon|Sâlkhâlon]], and its oldest form is still the tongue of every rite in the [[affiliation-hlykngdmnrtm|Holy Kingdom of Nartûm]]. It is also the language of law, scholarship and treaty across western Ankaris: the educated of every western realm learn it, and a scholar of Dúnavarre writes to a magistrate of Provenzia in Vylari, whatever each speaks at home.

## Overview

Vylari is a tongue of endings. A noun carries its role in the sentence on its last syllable, an adjective repeats its noun's gender, number and case, and a verb carries its subject, so that a Vylarian can set his words in almost any order and still be understood. Orators and poets use that freedom the way a builder uses an arch: a sentence can hold its verb to the end, set an adjective at one end of a line and its noun at the other, or mirror one clause in the next. The schools teach those figures as carefully as they teach the endings.

The language also carries the empire's order in its grammar. Ancient Vylari had four genders, and the fourth, the **hallowed**, belonged to the gods, the sovereign and the founders of the great houses. Imperial speech lost the gender and kept its endings on noble and divine names, which is why a Vylarian hears rank in a name before he hears anything else. The [[doc-vylarilexcn|Vylari Lexicon]] gives the words, the endings and the rules for names; this page gives the grammar.

## Two Stages

**Ancient Vylari** is the scholars' name for the speech the Pelwar carried west in the [[lore-pelwarmigr|Migration]]: strict in its rules, rich in forms, with seven cases and a vocative, three numbers, four genders, a wishing mood and a full middle voice. It survives in the oldest [[skill-thrvrknscrpt|Thâravárkon]] inscriptions and in the tongues descended from it, and the ritual speech of Nartûm is the nearest thing to it still spoken.

**Imperial Vylari** is the empire's speech today. In the street it has loosened: the dual and the locative are gone except in fixed phrases and place names, the wishing mood survives only in oaths and blessings, unstressed long vowels are said short, and prepositions creep in where the endings used to serve. **High Vylari**, the register of the Senate, the law courts, the temples, the arcane charters and formal oratory, keeps the ancient endings in use. A magistrate pronouncing sentence, a priest consecrating an altar and a senator moving a vote all speak it, and an educated Vylarian can move between the two registers inside one sentence for effect.

## Phonology

**Consonants.** Imperial Vylari has the stops _p t k b d g_, the same stops with a breath after them (_ph th kh_, never said as _f_ or as in _thin_), the hissed _s_ and buzzed _z_, _v_, _h_, _m n l r_, _x_ for _k_ and _s_ together, and _y_ before a vowel. The contrast between plain and breathed stops tells words apart, and a foreigner who says every _th_ as a plain _t_ is understood with an effort. Ancient Vylari had a third series of stops closed hard in the throat and released with a click (_t' k' d' g'_), voiced stops with a breath after them (_dh gh_), and _w_; four sound changes turned it into the imperial system, and the [[doc-vylarilexcn|Vylari Lexicon]] sets them out.

**Vowels.** Five vowels, _a e i o u_, each short or long; the romanization writes a long vowel with a circumflex. The [[skill-semrnscrpt|Sêmarion]] does not mark length in ordinary writing, and the grammar depends on it: a class I noun's nominative and ablative differ only in the length of the last vowel, so a poet can leave a word hovering between two readings on the page. Four diphthongs: _ai au ei oi_.

**Words.** A word opens on a vowel, one consonant, or a stop followed by _l_ or _r_; it closes on a vowel or on _s n r l x_. No more than three consonants stand together.

**Stress.** The stress falls on the next-to-last syllable when it is long (a long vowel, a diphthong, or a closing consonant) and otherwise on the syllable before it. It follows from the lengths, so nothing marks it. The result is the measured, falling rhythm foreigners hear in a Vylari speech: _zarândôs_ is stressed on _rân_, _sôrimâ_ on _sô_, _ulthâlon_ on _thâ_.

## Grammar

### Nouns and the four genders

A noun belongs to one of five classes, or to the hallowed declension, and has a gender. Gender is mostly formal: class I nouns are mostly feminine and class II mostly masculine, but _daimêlâ_ (a patron) is a class I noun that takes masculine agreement because of what a patron is, and the names of trees are feminine though they decline in class II. The neuter takes things, beasts of burden, children not yet of age and slaves. The hallowed takes the gods, the sovereign, the founders of houses, the great river and the gold coin, and it has no sex and no dual. The [[doc-vylarilexcn|Vylari Lexicon]] explains what moving a word between the genders means at law.

The cases are the nominative (the subject), the vocative (the person addressed), the accusative (the object, and the goal of motion), the genitive (of), the dative (to, for), the ablative (by, with, from) and the locative (at, in). The endings are deliberately economical. In class I one ending, _-ê_, serves the genitive singular, the dative singular and the nominative plural, and the nominative _sôrimâ_ and the ablative _sôrima_ differ only in a vowel's length, which the script does not write. In every neuter the nominative and accusative are alike. A poet works those overlaps the way a musician works a held note.

### Adjectives and adverbs

An adjective agrees with its noun in gender, number and case, which is what lets a poet separate the two by a whole line. Most adjectives decline like _thêlvarôs_ (noble): class II for the masculine and neuter, class I for the feminine, and the hallowed endings for a hallowed noun.

### Pronouns and pointing words

A verb's ending carries its subject, so a personal pronoun is emphatic. _Ên_ (I) said aloud is a raised voice, and a client who uses it to his patron has forgotten himself. The second person plural, _khôroi_, is also the formal "you" to one person, and the choice between it and the singular _khô_ is never neutral: the plural is owed to a superior and to any stranger of standing, and the singular used to an equal who has not offered it is an insult.

|               | Singular | Dual (ancient) | Plural   |
| ------------- | -------- | -------------- | -------- |
| first person  | _ên_     | _ênthôr_       | _ênoi_   |
| second person | _khô_    | _khôthôr_      | _khôroi_ |

The third person is a pointing word. There are three, and each carries an attitude as well as a place:

| Pointing word | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter    | Sense                                                                               |
| ------------- | --------- | -------- | --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| near me       | _dênôs_   | _dênâ_   | _dênon_   | "this, here by me"                                                                  |
| near you      | _thêsôs_  | _thêsâ_  | _thêson_  | "that of yours"; of a person, contemptuous                                          |
| yonder        | _alâthôs_ | _alâthâ_ | _alâthon_ | "that, over there"; of a person, grand: _alâthôs Thêlvarâs_, "the famous Thêlvarâs" |

The reflexive _vêth-_ (accusative _vêthen_, genitive _vêthis_, dative _vêthei_) points back to the subject of the main verb, so in reported speech it always means the speaker being reported and never the reporter. A Vylari historian keeps three people straight in one sentence that way.

### Verbs

A verb has four conjugations, named for the vowel its stem takes before the endings: _â_ (_vâkâthen_, to show), _ê_ (_ôkhêthen_, to see), _i_ (_thâvîthen_, to hear) and a consonant stem with _e_ (_yârethen_, to be). The personal endings carry the subject:

|                                     | Active                  | Middle and passive            |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| I                                   | _-mên_                  | _-mênar_                      |
| you, one                            | _-thâ_                  | _-thâr_                       |
| he, she, it                         | _-tôr_                  | _-tôra_                       |
| we two, you two, they two (ancient) | _-vên_, _-thôn_, _-tôn_ | _-vênar_, _-thônar_, _-tônar_ |
| we                                  | _-mêni_                 | _-mênir_                      |
| you, many                           | _-thêr_                 | _-thêra_                      |
| they                                | _-ntôr_                 | _-ntôra_                      |

**Two stems.** Every verb has an imperfective stem for action in progress and a perfective stem for action done. The perfective takes the prefix _ê-_ and changes its vowel: _vâk-_ (show) has the imperfective _vâkâ-_ and the perfective _êvêk-_.

| Form             | How it is made                             | Example                               |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| present          | imperfective stem + ending                 | _vâkâtôr_, he shows                   |
| past in progress | imperfective stem + _-s-_                  | _vâkâstôr_, he was showing            |
| perfect          | perfective stem + ending                   | _êvêktôr_, he showed                  |
| past perfect     | perfective stem + _-is-_                   | _êvêkistôr_, he had shown             |
| future           | imperfective stem + _-l-_                  | _vâkâltôr_, he will show              |
| subjunctive      | the stem vowel _â_ becomes _ê_             | _vâkêtôr_, that he show               |
| wishing mood     | stem + _-oi-_                              | _vâkoitôr_, may he show               |
| infinitive       | imperfective _-âthen_, perfective _-ethen_ | _vâkâthen_, _êvêkethen_               |
| participles      | present _-ândar_, past _-thôs_             | _vâkândar_, showing; _vâkthôs_, shown |

The two pasts divide the work of a story. The past in progress paints the scene—the rain was falling, the Senate was sitting—and the perfect moves the events along. A historian who wants a crisis to land breaks into the present for it, and a run of bare infinitives with their subjects in the nominative gives a breathless sequence of deeds.

**The middle.** Ancient Vylari had a full middle voice, for actions done to or for oneself: _vâkâtôra_, he shows himself, he is shown. Imperial Vylari reads it as a passive, and the verbs that only ever had middle forms survive as deponents, middle in form and active in sense: _zênôthâtôra_, he ponders; _sôlkhâtôra_, he follows, as a client follows. The impersonal passive stages an action with nobody doing it: _kôrdâtôra_, "there is fighting".

**The subjunctive as a point of view.** In a clause giving a reason, the indicative is the narrator's word and the subjunctive is someone else's. _Hôlêthi phâlvâstôr_ says "because he was away", and the writer vouches for it; _hôlêthi phâlvêstôr_ says "because, as they claimed, he was away", and the writer does not. One vowel moves the point of view, and the courts and the historians use it constantly.

**Reported speech.** A statement at one remove puts its subject in the accusative and its verb in the infinitive: _thelmon vâkâthen_, "(he says) that the client shows". The construction keeps the reporter at arm's length from what he reports, which makes it the natural voice of irony.

### Word order

Because the endings settle who did what, the order of words is free for emphasis and music. Legal prose keeps the subject first and the verb last by long convention, and ordinary speech mostly does the same; High Vylari in verse and oratory does as it likes. Three figures are taught in every school:

- **The golden line**: two adjectives, the verb, then the two nouns they belong to, in the same order. _Thêlvarân mêlvar vâkâtôr sôrimân kalmêthar_: "the great ancestor shows the noble song", where _thêlvarân_ (feminine accusative) waits four words for _sôrimân_ and _mêlvar_ (nominative) five for _kalmêthar_.
- **The mirror**: one clause and its reverse, the endings keeping the roles clear. _Sôrimân vâkâtôr kalmêthar, kalmêtharen vâkâtôr sôrimâ_: "the ancestor shows the song; the song shows the ancestor".
- **The suspended sentence**: clause after clause stacked before a main verb held to the last word, so that an audience hears the whole of a case before it learns what is being asked of it. Senate speeches are built this way, and a skilled orator can keep a chamber silent for a long minute waiting for his verb.

## Script & Literacy

Vylari employs the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Sêmarion]], an alphabetic script derived from the ancient imperial runes and refined over centuries into a flowing, efficient writing system. The script reads left-to-right and distinguishes capital and lowercase forms, and it marks vowel length only in schoolbooks and lawyers' copies, with a small bar over a long vowel where a misreading is possible. True literacy—the ability to read and compose continuous text—is confined to the clergy, professional scribes, and perhaps two in five of the nobility (imperial administration values educated lords, though many provincial nobles still leave reading to their clerks). Guildsmen in major cities may possess basic functional literacy (recognizing trade marks, numerals, and simple words), but even prosperous merchants rely on scribes and solicitors for contracts, correspondence, and record-keeping. In rural provinces, literacy is virtually nonexistent outside the local temple.

An older runic form, [[skill-thrvrknscrpt|Thâravárkon]], persists in ceremonial and architectural contexts. The ancient imperial inscriptions use a highly formalized variant with ligatures and special marks for religious names, and they write every sound of ancient Vylari, the clicked stops among them.

## Historical Development

Vylari descends from ancient Vylari, the tongue of the western Pelwar who came down to the coast in the Migration; the eastern Pelwar, conquered in the [[lore-varkhadcnq|Varkhad Conquest]], gave rise to [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal]] and [[skill-varokhlng|Varokhi]] instead. On the coast the tongue settled, built an empire and lightened its sounds, while the high register kept its grammar almost whole. Vylari is the most conservative of its family in grammar; [[skill-aelendlng|Áelendi]] is the most conservative in sound.

The empire took words from every people it ruled, chiefly terms of craft, trade and the sea, and the oldest religious words show the touch of the Áelendan highlanders, with whom the coastal Vylari shared a speech long before either had a name for it.

## Regional Dialects

**High Vylari:** The formal register of law, liturgy, the Senate and literature, taught in the schools. It keeps every ancient ending except the dual and holds every long vowel at its full length.

**Alyssa Dialect:** The quick, clipped speech of the greatest port. It shortens unstressed vowels, prefers prepositions to the rarer cases, puts the verb wherever it likes, and wears the "yonder" word down to a bare _a'_ before a noun, so that _a' thelmôs_ means simply "the client". High Vylari has no article, and a schoolmaster who hears _a'_ in his classroom corrects it.

**Hylen Lowland Dialect:** Says _th_ as _s_ and shortens long vowels everywhere, not only where they are unstressed. It keeps old verb forms the capital has lost, the past in progress among them, and its farmers still use the dual of oxen and of hands.

**Nartûm Ritual Dialect:** The speech of every rite in the Holy Kingdom of Nartûm, deliberately kept by its priesthoods. It still declines nouns in four genders, the hallowed among them, still uses the dual and the wishing mood, and still says the breathed voiced stops that imperial Vylari lost. It is the nearest living thing to ancient Vylari.

## Sample Phrases

- **"Ulthândoi ôkhointôr dênon zaron, ul kalmêtharên."**—"May the gods see this oath, and the ancestors." (High Vylari: the opening of a sworn oath, in the wishing mood.)
- **"Daimêla, ôrphênâmên."**—"Patron, I am in your debt." (The client's greeting at the morning call; no pronoun, since saying "I" aloud would be forward.)
- **"A' thelmôs lôkhâtôr; a' daimêlâ phâlvâtôr."**—"The client's come; the patron's out." (Alyssa street speech, with the worn-down article.)
- **"Hûmelthândar yâretôr."**—"It is resting." (Said of a ruin, with a straight face.)
- **"Thêson!"**—"That thing of yours!" (A free person named in the neuter: the gravest insult the grammar allows.)

## Related Languages

Ancient Vylari is the mother of a family, and the scholars of [[place-orzara|Ôrzâra]] call the family by the empire's name. [[skill-aelendlng|Áelendi]] split from it first, in the highlands, and keeps its old sounds, its dual and its wishing mood in the oaths of the Pact; the Áelendan reject the name of the family outright. [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] is the lowland daughter of Áelendi and keeps the dual. [[skill-provnzlng|Provenzal]] lost its cases and made articles of all three pointing words. [[skill-tarvenlng|Tarvéni]] kept three cases, three genders and the dual, held up by an older mountain speech beneath it. [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal]] and [[skill-varokhlng|Varokhi]] are cousins from the eastern Pelwar, outside the family. The [[doc-vylarilexcn|Vylari Lexicon]] sets roots from all five daughters side by side.

## Naming Traditions

A Vylari name is a given name and a house name, the given name first, and a man of note adds an earned name after both, given by a city or an army after a deed or by a family after a death. A given name is a word with a gender ending; noble children take the hallowed _-âs_ whether they are sons or daughters. A house name says whose descendants the bearer belongs to: the old houses use the genitive plural _-ândis_ of their founder's name, houses the sovereign has raised take _-âtês_, and provincial families of standing often take their estate's name with _-ênôs_.

A calling name is a child's name, the given name cut short with the neuter ending _-izon_, and it is used only by family and the closest friends of equal rank. The [[doc-vylarilexcn|Vylari Lexicon]] gives the endings and the rules in full.
