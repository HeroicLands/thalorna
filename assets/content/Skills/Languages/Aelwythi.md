Aelwythi is the speech of the island of [[place-aelwyth|Aelwyth]]. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Aelwythi, from the halting phrases of a visitor to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

The [[doc-aelwthlexcn|Aelwythi Lexicon]] gives the elements the island's names are built from, the register of the names in use, and the names that stand outside the pattern.

## Overview

Aelwythi is the tongue of the three Aelwythan realms, [[place-aldorathrgn|Aldorath]], [[affiliation-kingdmdnvr|Dúnavarre]] and [[place-ondareth|Ondareth]], which share essentially one culture and one speech though they share no political order ([[lore-aelwythanclt|Aelwythan]]). It is spoken in the manor and the market town, at the Synod's harbor and in the Senate's portico, and an Aelwythan carries it wherever the island's people go.

Nearly every human on the island descends from the [[lore-pelwarpepl|Pelwar]] refugees who reached it about 1200 BF, and Aelwythi is the speech of those descendants. The notes name no mainland tongue as its parent and give no account of how it parted from the speech the refugees brought, and this page says no more than they do.

Other speech on the island stands apart from it. The Nordmen of [[place-stormveld|Stormveld]] speak [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal]], and the [[place-pshtrwldrns|Peshtar]] clans keep a language family of their own with no written form. Aelwythi is not the speech of either.

## Beside Élavendri and Vylari

Three tongues meet on Aelwyth, each with its own work. Aelwythi is the island's own speech: plain, direct and quick to take in foreign words. Mainlanders call it coarse; Aelwythans hold that its plainness is honesty.

[[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] is the language of courts and verse. Its grammar draws fine distinctions of rank, courtesy and feeling that Aelwythi leaves unsaid, and it carries prestige at court, above all at [[affiliation-kingdmdnvr|Dúnavarre]], whose Synod keeps close ties with the Élavendren royal house. [[skill-vylarilng|Vylari]] is the language of law, scholarship and treaty, and the learned, clergy, lawyers and diplomats of the island use it as their counterparts across the west do.

Ordinary folk who stay at home speak Aelwythi alone. The travelled and educated commonly have more: nobles and courtiers, the learned, merchants and traders, sailors, and anyone who goes abroad. Such a person speaks Aelwythi, the neighbour's Élavendri, and Vylari for anything written to last. The learned and well-travelled of Élavendre likewise speak Aelwythi. Aelwythi and Élavendri have long borrowed words from each other.

## How Aelwythi is written down

Aelwythi is given in these pages in the reader's own language, in a register that stays older than the ordinary prose of the notes. Anything said, sung or recorded in Aelwythi is set in this register throughout: quoted speech, songs, proverbs, oaths, charms and folklore. The notes that describe the language, the people and the land stay in the setting's ordinary present-tense prose; only the text in Aelwythi follows the rules below.

**Rules for writers.**

- **Grammar.** One person addressed familiarly is _thou_ (subject), _thee_ (object), _thy_ or _thine_ (possessive, _thine_ before a vowel). _Ye_ and _you_ address several people, or one with respect. A verb after _thou_ closes in _-est_ (_thou goest_, _thou hast_, _thou art_), and a verb after _he_, _she_ or _it_ in _-eth_ (_he goeth_, _she hath_). Questions and denials are formed as the older speech formed them: _dost thou know_, _I know not_, _fear not_, _wilt thou_, _shalt thou_. Word order may invert for emphasis, verb before subject.
- **Vocabulary.** Words in use around the year 1600, and no others, each in the sense it then had. A word coined, or a sense acquired, in a later age is not used. Where a word is in doubt, a plainer word that was certainly current takes its place.
- **Style.** The cadence and idiom of the older stage, both prose and verse. Antique words scattered through modern sentences are wrong; the sentence itself is built the older way. Verse keeps its metre and rhyme.

**The same rule governs the lexicon.** Any gloss, saying or example written in Aelwythi on the [[doc-aelwthlexcn|Aelwythi Lexicon]] page follows these rules; the lexicon's own commentary stays in ordinary prose.

### Sample passages

A harbor-gate greeting:

> "Whither goest thou, stranger, and what dost thou carry in thy pack? The fog lieth heavy on the water, and the gate is shut ere dark. Tarry with us this night, and we will ask thee nothing more."

A saying of the manors:

> "A word once given is a stone laid in the wall: pluck it out, and the whole house falleth."

A harvest song:

> The wool is shorn, the barley bound,
> The last of summer's gold is ground;
> Fill high the cup, and drink it deep,
> And name the dead that lie asleep.

A charm said at the crossroads:

> Go softly, thou that walkest late,
> And leave thine offering at the gate;
> Who watcheth where the roads divide
> Will let the courteous pass beside.

## Naming Traditions

Names on Aelwyth are in the reader's own language, and they are kept in the forms already in use. The island's people name by compounding, and the same small stock of elements serves places, people and families.

**A place name is a compound of quality and ground.** The first part says what the place is like or whose it is; the last says what kind of ground it is. _Ashford_ is a crossing by the ash trees, _Brynwold_ the upland of the hills, _Wyndmarch_ the border country of the narrow ways, _Stonyhaugh_ the stony river meadow, _Coldbeck_ the cold stream, _Threndale_ the valley of lament, and _Greywater_ the grey river. A joining _s_ marks ownership, as in _Kingshollow_.

**A given name is two elements set together,** and a feminine name often closes on _-a_, _-ith_ or _-rith_: _Leofric_, _Edmund_, _Aldwin_, _Hilda_, _Edith_.

**A byname is usually a place.** The family of a farm, a spring or a clearing takes its name: _Chilton_, _Barnwell_, _Helmsley_, _Ashby_, _Winwick_. A byname closing on _-ing_ marks kin, as _Harding_ and _Browning_ do, and one closing on _-ling_ marks the small or the young, as _Grayling_ does.

**Some names stand outside the pattern.** The realm names in _-ath_ and _-eth_ (_Aldorath_, _Ondareth_, _Veldareth_), _Aelwyth_ itself, a few Vardain and Khazári place names, the Élavendri-form names of the Dúnavarre embassy, and the Nordmal names of Aelwythans of Nordmen descent are all attested and are kept as written. The lexicon lists each with its reason. A new name for the island is built from the listed elements, or it is entered there as an exception.
