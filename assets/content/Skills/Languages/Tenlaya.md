The Tenlaya Language is the tongue of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Tenlaya]], the Grass People of the northern plains. Fluency measures the range of what a speaker can say in it, from a trader's few dozen words of meat, corn and price to the speech of someone raised in a band and taught the songs of a society. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

## Overview

The hunting bands and the earth-lodge villages speak one tongue, and each hears the other in it. Band speech is quick and clipped, and drops the last vowel of a word whenever the next word opens on one. Village speech is slower and keeps every vowel, and its old women use words for corn, for the lodge and for the fields that a band child has never needed. A band hunter and a village farmer understand each other at once. Both can tell, by the third word, which kind of person is talking, and the autumn trading is full of jokes that turn on it.

West of the mountains the bands that fish the salmon falls and dig roots on the plateaus speak it with a drawl of their own, and use a few words for fish and roots that the plains do not know. Nobody on the plains mistakes them for another people.

The tongue is related to nothing in the south. A southern trader who has learned [[skill-kiichek|Ki'i'chek]] from childhood hears nothing he knows in it. The Grass People have no use for southern speech either, and those who trade with the [[affiliation-teyumi|Turquoise People]] for southern goods do it in the Teyumi tongue or by signs.

Nobody writes it. The Grass People keep their law in songs, their treaties in pipes and their years on painted robes, and the pictures on a robe name a winter, never a word.

## How the Tenlaya Language sounds

The Tenlaya Language is open and quick, and many of its words close on a soft _n_. Its syllables are mostly a consonant and a vowel, and its long words are strings of them: _tanshe_ is a bison, _yuchan_ a drive, _keshunwinte_ the name Gives Robes.

**The vowels.** _a_ leads by far, with _i_ and _e_ after it and _o_ and _u_ behind. Two vowels never stand side by side; a consonant always comes between them.

**The consonants.** _k_ and _t_ are the commonest, then _ch_, _sh_ and _h_, then _w_, _p_, _m_, _n_, _l_, _s_ and _y_. Three pairs open a word: _sk_, _sp_ and _st_. Inside a word _sh_, _s_ and _h_ may close a syllable before _p_, _t_ or _k_, _k_ before _t_, and _n_ before most consonants: _taspe_, red, _wahki_, dust, _kehta_, ten.

**Shapes and endings.** Most words have two syllables, many three, a few four; the words a speaker uses most are the shortest. A word ends on a vowel, most often _a_, or on _n_, and the _n_ is frequent enough to set the tune of the tongue: about one word in three closes on it.

**What the Tenlaya Language leaves out.** It has no _b_, _d_, _g_, _f_, _r_, _v_, _z_ or _j_, no _th_, none of the rasping _kh_ or _gh_ of the mountain tongues, and none of the catches and ejective pops of Ki'i'chek. No word opens on two consonants except _sk_, _sp_ and _st_, and three never stand together.

## Phonology

### Consonant System

**Stops:** p, t, k

**Affricate:** ch

**Fricatives:** s, sh, h

**Nasals:** m, n

**Liquid and glides:** l, w, y

### Vowel System

**Vowels:** a, e, i, o, u, each said as the spelling guide gives it, and never run together

### Stress

A word of two syllables is stressed on the first, and a longer word on the second-to-last, by the rule of [[doc-prnncthlrn|Pronouncing Thalorna]]: _tanshe_ is TAN-she, _yuchanka_ is yu-CHAN-ka. No word of the dictionary needs an accent.

## Grammar Notes

### Sentence Structure

The verb comes last. The one who acts comes first, then whatever is acted on, then the verb: _mo sho chopiki_, I see you. A word of time or place comes before the verb, and _nushe_, not, stands immediately after the word it denies: _skuma stuhan nushe_, the rain does not come.

### Verbs

A verb does not change. _Chopiki_ is to see, and _mo chopiki_, _sho chopiki_ and _tuye chopiki_ are I see, you see and they see. Whether a thing happened, happens or will happen is said with a word of time when it matters, and left to the listener when it does not.

### Nouns and Possession

A noun does not change for more than one. A speaker who needs to say how many says it with a number or with _chashowa_, many: _keyan chashowa_, many lodges. The owner stands before what is owned, with nothing between them: _mo oshu_ is my lodge, and _hesun chekan_ the bundle of the village.

### Pronouns

_Mo_ is I and _mon_ we; _sho_ is you, whether one or many; _tu_ is he, she or it, and _tuye_ they. There is no separate word for a man or a woman spoken of.

### Questions

A question opens with _chun_ or with one of the question words built like it: _chuwa_ who, _chuta_ what, _chuno_ where, _chuhan_ when, _chuye_ why, _chuke_ how. _Chun sho hiye sapi_ is "do you drink water?" The roman spelling marks the question with `?`, and the speaker marks it with the opening word.

### Building Words

Four endings make most of the built words. _-ka_ makes the one who keeps or does a thing: _yuchan_, a drive, gives _yuchanka_, a hunt chief. _-ya_ makes the people of a thing: _tenla_, grass, gives _tenlaya_, the Grass People, and a band is named the same way. _-hin_ makes a noun of an act or a quality: _skisa_, to live, gives _skisahin_, life. _-tan_ gives a number its place: _shapotan_, third. Two words set side by side make a compound, the first saying which: _skewa_, a winter, and _keshun_, a robe, give _skewa keshun_, a year-robe.

## Everyday speech

_Nawe_ is the greeting at a lodge door, and _mikin_ the word at parting. _Lekiye_ thanks. _Ehon_ is yes and _kunye_ no, and a refusal is softened by offering something else in the same breath.

_Mo sho chopiki_ means "I see you", and it is the answer to _nawe_. _Chun sho hitin_, "do you eat?", is the first thing said to anyone who comes into a lodge, and the answer is always yes, because a guest who refuses food has refused the lodge. _Tanshe stuhan_, "the bison come", is shouted down a camp when the scouts come in, and nobody speaks loudly after it until the drive is over.

_Mo oshu_ is my lodge, and in a village a man who says it means his wife's, because the earth lodge belongs to the women who built it. _Kuhashi_ is mother and _cheyowa_ father, _chasha_ a child, _sheki_ a grandmother and _ahalache_ a grandfather. _Shuwa_ is kin, and a speaker uses it for anyone who has a claim on the meat.

Numbers count by tens: _ishe_, _henu_, _shapo_, _nesho_, _kotan_, one to five; _kehta_ is ten, _henu kehta_ twenty, _opahin_ a hundred. A number takes _-tan_ to give its place: _ishetan_, first, _neshotan_, fourth.

## Script and Literacy

The Tenlaya Language has no script. The Grass People keep the count of their years on a robe: each band's year-robe keeper (_keshunka_) paints one small picture for each winter, in a spiral out from the center, and names the winter by it. A picture of a lodge with its door painted black is the winter a great man died; a ditch with flames above it is the Winter of the Ditch. The keeper can recite the whole spiral, winter by winter, and that recitation is the band's history. The pictures name a winter. None of them writes a word.

Warriors paint their coups on their robes and lodge covers in the same way, and the drive lanes on the plains are marked with cairns (_wehi_) older than any band. Neither is writing. The Grass People have seen southern writing on goods that came north through the Turquoise People, and see no use in it.

## Naming Traditions

A child is named on the fourth day of its life by a grandparent or a respected elder, and the name is two words of the dictionary set together, most often a thing of the land or the sky and a thing it does: _Kuntonwiwan_, Stands in Thunder, _Shiwetuta_, Bright Moon. A first name is a hope. A person who does something worth telling takes a new name for it, given at a giveaway or a society dance, and a man who has counted many coups may have had four or five names by the time he is old. _Keshunwinte_, Gives Robes, is a name earned that way.

A place is named the same way, with two words set side by side, the first saying which: a thing and what is known of it, or what stands there and where. _Tanshe Yawa_ is Bison Ford, _Henu Shewan_, two rivers, the Forks, _Kunton Toshe_ Thunder Cliff and _Kopen Mokin_ Flint Hill. A country is named for what it gives: _Wehi Oshte_, the Cairn Plain, is the country of the drive lanes, and _Potonta Yunto_, the Root Fields, the country where the western bands dig roots.

A band is named with _-ya_ on something it is known for: the _Kanweya_ are the Coyote band and the _Wehiya_ the Cairn band. A person is said to be "of" the band they hunt with, and can change bands by moving their lodge.

### Clusters

Most syllables are a consonant and a vowel. Two consonants meet inside a word where a syllable closes on _n_, _sh_, _s_, _h_ or _k_ before the next; _ch_ and _sh_ are each one sound, so _yashki_ holds two.

```sql
SELECT 'Most consonant letters together inside a word' AS "Rule", max AS "Count"
FROM clusters WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-tenlayalng'
```

## Making names {#making-names}

**A person** carries two words set together: _keshunwinte_ is _keshun_, a robe, and _winte_, to give. **A band** is a word with _-ya_: _kanweya_, the Coyote band. **A place** names what it is or what happens there: Tanshe Yawa is _tanshe_, the bison, and _yawa_, a ford, the village where the herds cross the river, and _chiti hesun_, the fire village, is the ruin the Grass People call the Burned Village.

**The people** are _tenlaya_, the people of the grass, and the name these pages give them is the Grass People. The south calls them _P'enqal Ch'akal_, after their grassland, and its traders the Coyote People, after the coyotes painted on the shields of one of their societies.
