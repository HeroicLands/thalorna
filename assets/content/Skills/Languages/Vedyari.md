Vedyari is a tongue of the Vedyari (isolate) family. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Vedyari, from the halting phrases of a traveler to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

## Overview

Vedyari is the primary tongue of the city-states of [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara Region]]. It is a language of considerable antiquity, and it has parted across the centuries into a classical and a vernacular form. Classical Vedyari serves the sacred texts, the legal documents and the formal ceremonies, and differs markedly from the spoken dialects of common discourse. It is a weighty and complex tongue, and the civilization that speaks it prizes precision, ritual and philosophical inquiry.

Vedyari speakers are found throughout the city-states and in trading communities across the eastern world. The language has proven remarkably durable, maintaining grammatical coherence even as phonological shifts have created regional variation.

## How Vedyari sounds

Vedyari has two registers, and they sound alike but end differently. The classical tongue of the temple, the court and the law ends its words on a vowel. The tongue of the road, the market and the house closes many words on a consonant. Most words have two or three syllables, a name is usually two words joined, and a word of four syllables is long.

**The vowels.** _a_ carries most words, with _i_ and _u_ after it. _e_ and _o_ are rarer, and few words open on them. Two vowels stand together only as _ai_ and _au_.

**The consonants.** _r_ and _n_ lead. The plain stops _t_, _k_, _d_ and _p_ come next, then _s_, _m_ and _v_, with _sh_ and _j_ for colour. _bh_ is common, as in _bharava_ and _bhurka_: a _b_ said with a breath after it, the two letters as the guide reads them, said together. Vedyari writes _bh_, as the guide allows a tongue whose note says so. _kh_ and _gh_ keep the guide's throat sounds and belong to a smaller, older set of words, such as _ghaul_ "night" and _okhra_ "a loom"; most of them stand at the start of a syllable. A new word reaches for a stop before a _v_ or an _r_.

**Clusters.** Consonants meet in a few favoured pairs and triples inside a word: _ndr_, _shr_, _kr_, _dr_, _vr_ and _pr_. A word opens on at most two consonants together. _ksh_ stands inside a word, as in _akshora_, never at its start.

**Endings.** A classical word ends on a vowel, most often _-a_: the gods _Rasikara_ and _Kalavrata_, the titles _maharava_ and _senapavi_, the words _dorma_ "duty" and _janpoda_. A person or a thing done ends in _-ona_, _-ika_, _-in_, _-ini_ or _-ija_, and a lord or a god in _-endra_, _-esha_ or _-deva_. The spoken word may close on one consonant, _n_, _r_, _t_, _k_, _s_, _l_, _m_ or _d_, or on _nd_: _lam_ "a child", _ghal_ "a sword", _daum_ "eight". A place people travel to closes the same way: a town in _-pur_, a village in _-gram_, a landing in _-ghat_, a fort in _-kot_, a pass in _-dvar_, a peak in _-shikhar_, a grove in _-ban_, a ground in _-stal_, a seat in _-stan_, a bank in _-tir_, and a keeper's land in _-pal_; a hero's name ends in _-bir_ or _-bira_; a weekday ends in _-var_. A given name ends on _-a_, _-i_ or a consonant (_Chandreli_, _Shilabir_, _Jayanit_); a clan name more often on a vowel.

**Built names.** A name is one word or two joined, seldom three: _Chandreli_ is _chendra_ and _sundeli_, "moon, the beautiful", and _Danurkirti_ is "bow-fame". A name of five syllables or more is an old name or a title.

**What Vedyari leaves out.** It has no _th_ and no _dh_, since the guide reads both as the sounds of _thin_ and _this_, and no _ph_, which readers say as _f_. It avoids _lv_ and the endings of [[skill-helonclng|Helonic Language]], _-os_ and _-is_. No word closes on two consonants but _nd_, and none closes on _sh_, _kh_, _bh_, _v_ or _h_.

## Phonology

### Consonant System

Vedyari employs a rich consonantal inventory marked by articulatory precision:

**Stops and Affricates:**

Unvoiced
: p, t, retroflex t, k

Voiced
: b, d, retroflex d, g

Affricates
: ch (TS), j (DZH)

Aspirated
: kh, gh, bh—each stop with a breath after it

**Fricatives:**

Unvoiced
: s, palatal sh, retroflex sh, h

Voiced
: z (marginal)

**Nasals:** m, n, retroflex n, palatal ny

**Liquids & Approximants:** l, r, retroflex r; y, v

**Distinctive Features:**

- The retroflexes (t, d, n and r) are phonemically significant and appear frequently in speech, marking a clear articulatory tendency
- Palatal sh and ny maintain distinctness from their dental/velar counterparts
- Geminate consonants are common and grammatically meaningful, marking compound formation

### Vowel System

Vedyari distinguishes five cardinal vowels, each occurring in short and long variants:

**Vowels:** a, i and u (each short or long), e (mid-front), o (mid-back)

**Diphthongs:** Common sequences include ai, au, which may function as monophthongs in rapid speech

**Vocalic r:** The r-colored nucleus of a syllable is a vowel, and an _r_ standing between two consonants is that vowel: _Prtimaja_ is four syllables, _Pr-ti-ma-ja_.

**Vowel Harmony Patterns:** Historically significant; elements that select for front vowels (i, e) or back vowels (a, o, u) tend to cluster within morphemes.

### Phonotactic Patterns

Analysis of attested names reveals:

Consonant Clusters
: A word opens on a single consonant or on one of a short list of pairs: a consonant before _r_ (_pr_, _tr_, _dr_, _gr_, _vr_, _shr_), a consonant before _y_ or _v_ (_vy_, _shy_, _sv_, _jv_), and the old pair _jn_. Inside a word no more than three consonants stand together, as in _Chendra_ and _Sandva_. The aspirates _kh_, _gh_ and _bh_ are single consonants, so _Bharava_ opens on one

Syllable Structure
: Predominantly CV or CVC; onset-heavy structure favors initial consonants

Final Consonants
: A word closes on a vowel or on _n_, _r_, _t_, _k_, _s_, _l_, _m_ or _d_, or on _nd_; the retroflex and palatal nasals close a word in the scholars' texts

Word Length
: Most words and names have two or three syllables. A name is one word or two joined, and a name of five syllables or more is an old name or a title.

## Grammar Notes

### Word Order

Classical Vedyari follows a **Subject-Object-Verb (SOV)** order in main clauses, though marked inversions occur in poetry and philosophical verse.

### Nominal System

- **Nouns** have number (singular/plural) and, arguably, honorific status
- **Agreement:** Adjectives and determiners agree with nouns in gender (masculine, feminine, neuter)
- **Compounds:** Noun-noun and adjective-noun compounds are extremely productive; the rightmost element bears primary stress

### Participles

- Participial forms are common as attributive modifiers and in periphrastic constructions

## Script & Literacy

**Classical Script:** Vedyari employs the [[skill-vdykshrscrpt|Vedyakshara]], a syllabic script of approximately 48 distinct characters, each representing a CV syllable. Consonant clusters and final consonants are marked through diacritical modification of the base glyph. The script evolved from an older abjadic writing system (now preserved only in sacred contexts) and shows clear influence from neighboring systems.

**Common Cursive:** A more flowing, connected variant has developed for administrative and mercantile use, reducing the syllabic inventory to roughly 36 core forms with additional ligatures.

**Literacy:** Formal education in Classical Vedyari is the province of the priesthood, professional scribes, and perhaps one noble in six. Even in the wealthiest city-states, the general population is overwhelmingly non-literate; merchants and guildsmen possess functional literacy at best—enough to recognize trade marks and numerals—and rely on scribes for contracts and record-keeping. In hinterland regions, literacy is virtually nonexistent outside the local temple. The mercantile caste has driven adoption of simplified script variants, but these are used by hired scribes rather than merchants themselves.

## Historical Development

### Proto-Vedyari

Vedyari's roots lie in an isolate family with no clear external relations, on the evidence of the archaic ritual texts and the etymologies. Some scholars propose remote connections to the tongues of the far east (_[[skill-tantsoulng|Tantsou Language]]_) and the southwest (_[[skill-khelathlng|Khelathi Language]]_). Nobody has shown one.

### Classical Period (Age of Kingdoms)

The standardization of Classical Vedyari occurred roughly 1,200 years before the present, during the reign of the philosopher-kings of [[place-madustan|Madustan]]. This period saw the composition of the great philosophical commentaries, legal codes, and epic narratives that remain the prestige texts of the civilization.

### Modern Era

Since [[lore-stndrdmdstn|the Standardization at Madustan]], gradual sound changes have created divergence:

Lenition of intervocalic stops
: p, t and k soften to f, "th" and a throaty kh between vowels in some dialects (more pronounced in southern city-states)

Nasalization of vowels before nasals
: Incipient; most evident in rapid speech

Merger of dental and retroflex sibilants
: Common in younger speakers

Classical Vedyari remains largely frozen in literature and formal address, but vernacular speech has simplified many inflectional endings and leveled irregular paradigms.

## Regional Dialects

### Northern Vedyarian (Varcine Region)

- Retains more conservative phonology; retroflex consonants pronounced with greater distinction
- Preserves older endings
- Stress patterns follow classical norms (penultimate mora receives primary stress)

### Southern Vedyarian (Coastlands & Trading Cities)

- More rapid speech; intervocalic lenition more pronounced
- Inflectional endings frequently reduced to single vowels
- Stress shift: increasing tendency toward word-initial stress in compounds
- Greater borrowing from neighboring linguistic systems

### Eastern Outlier Dialect (Remote Merchant Colonies)

- Extreme simplification of agreement
- Loss of gender distinctions in some contexts
- Extensive code-switching with neighboring trade languages
- Represents early stages of creolization

## Naming Traditions

### Cultural Significance

Vedyari names are cosmologically and philosophically charged. A personal name states the family lineage, the spiritual aspiration and the social position of the person who carries it. Names commonly take elements from mythology, from virtue terms or from the celestial bodies, and bind the individual to the metaphysical order.

### Naming Patterns

**Given Names (Male):**

- Often compounds of two or three words, joining a virtue, a god's favor or a quality of the sun or the moon to a word for what the bearer is to be
- Frequent elements: _palda_ (keeper), _pavi_ (lord), _rajva_ (king), _-deva_ (of _devra_, a god), _birda_ (hero), _vikroma_ (valor), _anonda_ (joy), _kirta_ (fame)
- Preferred vowel harmony creates euphonious, rhythmic quality
- Examples: _Darmapalda_ (keeper of the law), _Senapavi_ (lord of the host), _Shilabir_ (hero of the stone), _Madurava_ (sweet voice)

**Given Names (Female):**

- Similarly compound, often incorporating elements denoting grace, devotion, or cosmic principle
- Common endings: _-i_, _-ika_ and _-a_, which close the names of both women and men
- Deity names and sacred geographical features appear frequently
- Examples: _Padmavali_ (a row of lotuses), _Chandreli_ (moon, the beautiful), _Vanika_ (of the grove)

**Clan Names:**

- A compound of two words, sometimes three
- Often feature ancient founder names or mythological ancestral figures
- May include place or occupational elements
- Examples: _Danurkirti_ (bow-fame), _Vidsamondal_ (the circle of learning), _Tejanand_ (brilliance and joy)

### Honorifics and Titles

Formal address often includes patronymic constructions (_-suta_, _-vaya_: "offspring of") and epithetive titles denoting virtue, rank, or accomplishment. These may be appended or prefixed, creating elaborate formal names used in ceremonial and official contexts.

### Calling Names

Beside the formal name, every Vedyari has a _calling name_, a short form of the given name used by kin and friends and offered rather than taken: _Padmavali_ is _Padmi_ at home. It keeps the first one or two syllables of the given name and closes on _-u_, _-i_ or _-a_, or doubles the first syllable. The lexicon named below gives the rule in full, with the calling names the setting records.

## Vedyari Lexicon {#lexicon}

A temple scribe in [[place-rajepur|Rajepur]] who teaches a newcomer to read the names on a map begins with the doors. _Dvarna_ is a door, and the passes through [[place-sthrnwall|the Southern Wall]] are doors: [[place-suryadvar|Suryadvar]] the sun's door, [[place-meghadvar|Meghadvar]] the storm's, [[place-tamradvar|Tamradvar]] the copper road's. Learn twenty such words and the map of [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]] reads itself, because almost every Vedyari name is two or three ordinary words set side by side.

This is the word-hoard of the Vedyari tongue: the words a player or a GM reaches for when a Vedyari names a child, a village, an office or a god, the rules those words obey, and the way two of them join into a third. A word made from these by those rules is a Vedyari word. The last section is a register of every Vedyari name the setting uses, with the note where each is met and the words it is made from.

Every word here is written as names are written across the setting.

## How to read the dictionary {#how-to-read-the-dictionary}

An entry gives the word as it is cited: a noun in its plain form, a verb as its bare stem with a hyphen after it, since a stem never stands alone. A word made of smaller parts names the words and suffixes the form is made of, joined by `+`, and the form is exactly what the rules below make of them.

### Fields

```sql
SELECT name AS "Field", holds AS "What it holds"
FROM fields
WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng'
ORDER BY position
```

## Sounds and spelling {#sounds-and-spelling}

### Word edges

A word opens on a vowel, on a single consonant or on one of the pairs below, and closes on a vowel or on one of the consonants below. A verb stem is bound and never closes a word, so it may end on any consonant.

```sql
SELECT CASE edge WHEN 'openCluster' THEN 'Pairs that may open a word' WHEN 'closeConsonant' THEN 'Consonants that close a word' END AS "Edge", coalesce(nullif(array_to_string(list_transform(sounds, x -> '`' || x || '`'), ', '), ''), '—') AS "Sounds"
FROM edges WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' ORDER BY position
```

### Clusters

```sql
SELECT 'Most consonants together inside a word' AS "Rule", max AS "Count"
FROM clusters WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng'
```

Two vowels never stand side by side except as `ai` and `au`; where two words would bring them together, the joining below decides what is written.

## Building words {#building-words}

Two moves make every built word in this lexicon, and a new word made the same way is as good a Vedyari word as any here.

**A compound.** Two words set side by side make one, and the first describes the second: _surta_ "the sun" and _dvarna_ "a door" give _suryadvar_, the sun's door. Most names of places and offices are compounds, and many have three or four members: _meghra_, _mukhva_ and _kalda_ give _meghamukhakala_, the month of the mouth of the clouds. A hyphen may stand at a seam, and where it does the two words keep their own shapes: _Surta-samedra_.

**A suffix.** A word or a verb stem takes one of the suffixes below: _gan-_ "count" takes _-oka_ to give _ganoka_, a reckoner, and _vreta_ "a vow" takes _-in_ to give _vretin_, one under a vow.

### Joining

Where the first word of a compound closes on a vowel and the second opens on one, the two vowels meet as the table says. Every other seam writes both words whole.

```sql
SELECT array_to_string(list_transform(first, x -> '`' || x || '`'), ', ') AS "First closes on", array_to_string(list_transform(second, x -> '`' || x || '`'), ', ') AS "Second opens on",
       '`' || written || '`' AS "Written", coalesce(example, '—') AS "Example"
FROM joining WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' ORDER BY position
```

### Suffixes

```sql
SELECT '`' || form || '`' AS "Suffix", attaches AS "Stem", gloss AS "Makes", coalesce(nullif(array_to_string(list_transform(examples, e -> regexp_replace(e, '^(\S+) > ([^\s,;]+)', '_\1_ > _\2_')), ', '), ''), '—') AS "Example"
FROM affixes
WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng'
ORDER BY position
```

## Making names {#making-names}

**A person** carries a given name and the name of a clan, the given name first: [[being-nrvtjnnd|Narava Tejanand]] is Narava of the clan of brilliance and joy. A given name is one word or a compound of two or three, wishing something on the child: a virtue, a god's favor, a quality of the sun or the moon. _Chandreli_ is "moon, the beautiful."

### Given names

```sql
SELECT string_agg('`' || form || '`', ', ' ORDER BY position) AS "Ending", gloss AS "Given to"
FROM affixes WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' AND kind = 'name'
GROUP BY gloss ORDER BY min(position)
```

A woman's name is often a man's word with _-i_ in place of its closing _a_: _sundora_ "beautiful" gives _sundeli_.

**A clan** is named by a compound of two words, sometimes three, which keeps the founder's name or the virtue the lineage claims: _Danurkirti_ is bow-fame, and _Vidsamondal_ is the circle of learning. A clan name does not change with the gender of the person who carries it.

**A place** is a compound whose last member says what kind of place it is, and whose first member says which:

```sql
WITH named AS (
  SELECT alias AS label, min(slug) AS slug FROM (SELECT unnest(name.aliases) AS alias, address.slug AS slug FROM notes WHERE type = 'place') GROUP BY alias
  UNION ALL
  SELECT name.full, address.slug FROM notes WHERE type = 'place' AND name.full NOT IN (SELECT unnest(name.aliases) FROM notes WHERE type = 'place')
)
SELECT '`' || e.form || '`' AS "Last member", e.sense AS "A place that is",
       '[[' || n.slug || '|' || split_part(e.examples[1], ', ', 1) || ']]' || substring(e.examples[1], length(split_part(e.examples[1], ', ', 1)) + 1) AS "Example"
FROM elements e JOIN named n ON n.label = split_part(e.examples[1], ', ', 1)
WHERE e.language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' AND e.kind = 'place' ORDER BY e.position
```

A person from a place takes _-i_: a [[place-rajepur|Rajepur]] man is a Rajepuri, a woman of [[affiliation-vyalendra2|Vyalendra]] a Vyalendri.

**An office** is a compound whose last member says what the holder does: _palda_ keeps (_shetrapalda_, a keeper of fields), _pavi_ presides (_sabhapavi_, the presiding officer of a sabhra), _nayeka_ leads, _adyoksha_ oversees, _achorya_ teaches. A title from a verb takes _-oka_, as _dorshaka_, a seer, or _-in_, as _vretin_, one under a vow. The great titles are compounds with _mahva_: _maharava_, _mahaprasoda_.

**A god** of the [[affiliation-vrnkpnthn|Varnuka]] is named by a compound that says what the god keeps or does: [[lore-kalavratadty|Kalavrata]] is the vow of death, [[lore-meghonatadty|Meghonata]] the lord of the clouds, [[lore-vyalendradty|Vyalendra]] the chief of beasts. A god's adjective takes _-an_, and a god's day takes _vura_.

**A work** of literature or law is named in the reader's tongue by what it does, as The [[lore-rcitkngsmhnd|Recitation of the Kings of Mahanodi]] is.

### Calling names

A woman entered on the temple roll as _Padmavali_ answers to _Padmi_ in her mother's kitchen. The long name is the formal one: it stands in records, in the temple and before a court, and it is the name a stranger uses. The short one is the _calling name_, used by kin and friends, and it is offered, never taken; the [[lore-vedyariclt|Vedyari]] culture note says what follows from that. Every calling name is cut from the given name by one rule, and it is never made from the clan name.

**Counting syllables.** A syllable is counted by its vowel. Each short vowel, long vowel and diphthong is one syllable, and so is an `r` standing between two consonants, so _Prtimaja_ has four: _pr·ti·ma·ja_. A single consonant between two vowels opens the second syllable; where several stand together, the first closes the syllable before and the rest open the next, as in _chan·dre·li_. A seam adds nothing of its own: where the joining has merged two vowels into one, as _podma_ and _avoli_ merge in _padmavali_, the merged vowel is a single syllable, _pad·ma·va·li_.

**Cutting the name.** Keep the first one or two syllables of the given name whole, carry over the consonants that open the next syllable, and close on `-u`, `-i` or `-a` where that syllable's vowel stood. Put another way, the given name is cut just before its second or third vowel and a closing is added: _Darmapalda_ is cut to _Darm-_ and gives _Darmu_, and _Padmavali_ is cut to _Padm-_ and gives _Padmi_. The letters before the cut are the given name's own, long vowels long; where a name is written with a stress mark the pages do not use, the vowel beneath it may be read short or long. The other way to make a calling name is to double the first syllable, writing its opening consonants and its vowel twice: _Suvarti_ gives _Susu_. A name that opens on a vowel has no doubled form, since two vowels never stand together.

A calling name is said aloud far more often than it is written, and one sound changes on the way: a vowel `r` is spoken and written `ri`. _Prtimaja_ is cut to _Prit-_ and gives _Pritu_, and _Drksharana_ is cut to _Drksh-_ and gives _Driksha_. Apart from that, everything before the closing comes from the given name, and a calling name always closes on a vowel, so it keeps the word rules without effort.

```sql
SELECT coalesce('`' || form || '`', 'the first syllable doubled') AS "Closing",
       CASE usual WHEN 'man' THEN 'a man' WHEN 'woman' THEN 'a woman' ELSE usual END AS "Usual for"
FROM calling_names WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' ORDER BY position
```

A calling name in _-u_ is a man's and one in _-i_ a woman's, so _Darmu_ names a man and _Padmi_ a woman. The given names themselves are not divided this way: a short _-i_ closes a given name of either.

Every Vedyari has a calling name, and the pages record it wherever the given name is long. A person whose given name runs to the number of syllables below or more carries the calling name as an alias, and the register lists it in the tongue `calling`, with the given name it is cut from in its **Built from** cell.

```sql
SELECT 'Fewest syllables in a given name the pages give a calling name' AS "Rule", min_syllables AS "Count"
FROM calling_names WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' LIMIT 1
```

### Retired names

Some names are kept out of the setting: the gods, persons, places and terms of Earth, and forms the rules refuse. Each stands below with the name the setting writes for the same thing, or a dash where it writes none, and no page writes them, however accented. A literature note quotes its work in the work's own words and a poem keeps its own, so a retired name may stand in either, and a `terran_analog` comment names the Earth source on purpose.

```sql
SELECT '`' || form || '`' AS "Retired",
       coalesce(array_to_string(list_transform(instead, x -> '`' || x || '`'), ', '), '—') AS "Written instead",
       why AS "Why"
FROM retired WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-vedyarlng' ORDER BY position
```
