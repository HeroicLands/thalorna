Jürthāti is a tongue of the Tāntsou family. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Jürthāti, from the halting phrases of a traveler to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

Jürthāti is the speech of the [[affiliation-jenchaiempr|Jénchài Empire]]—the great island and the lesser island east of the mainland, which their inhabitants call **Tēyan Jenjai**, the Nine Mountains Above the Clouds of Heaven: the peaks stand above the clouds, and the people who live on them stand above every other people, the western mainland first among them. In speech the name shortens to **Jenjai**, the Nine Mountains, the native reading of the name the court writes _Jénchài_. It descends from the Tāntsou brought across the strait a thousand years and more ago, and a thousand years of separation, mutual non-recognition and open war have made it a distinct language rather than a dialect. [[affiliation-tantsouempr|Tāntsou]] does not concede this, and Tāntsou grammarians still describe Jürthāti as a provincial corruption; Jürthāti scholars return the compliment by describing mainland speech as what happens to a language administered by clerks.

The [[doc-jurthatilexcn|Jürthāti Lexicon]] gives the words in their native reading with the changes that make them from Tāntsou, the endings of the honorific grid, and the rules a house or a granted name is built by.

## Overview

Where [[skill-tantsoulng|Tāntsou]] is a language of ministries—precise, compounding, built to specify—Jürthāti is a language of obligation. Its distinctive machinery is social: an elaborate apparatus of honorific, humble and neutral verb forms that encodes the relative standing of speaker, listener and subject in nearly every sentence uttered. There is no neutral way to say that a man arrived. The verb must place him, and place the speaker relative to him, and a wrong choice is not a grammatical error but an insult with consequences in a society where a retainer's honor is a live instrument.

The language is correspondingly indirect. A refusal is very rarely a negation; it is an expression of the difficulty of the thing, or of the speaker's own inadequacy to it, and a listener who takes it literally has misunderstood. This has ruined more than one mainland embassy.

## Phonology

**Vowels:** Five, **a e i o u**, each short or held long as **ā ē ī ō ū**. The mainland's gliding pairs have mostly settled into long vowels—_ao_ and _ou_ are long _ō_ and _ū_ on the islands—and only _ai_, _ei_ and _ui_ survive as pairs.

**Pitch:** The mainland's four tones have collapsed into a **pitch-accent** that the islanders do not write. A word the mainland sings in the high level or the rising tone is said high on the islands; one in the falling or the low tone is said low. The acute and the grave simply drop: _léi_, spirit, is _rei_; _shòu_, water, is _shū_; _yàn_, cloud, is _yan_; _wéi_, jade, is _wei_. The high level tone left length behind, so the mainland's _tān_, celestial, is still _tān_. The pitch distinguishes word pairs rather than carrying grammatical weight—_tsū_ said high is earth, said low a tribe—and many mainland words have fallen together outright, which the shared script keeps apart in writing. The tone-poor result is the plainest difference between island and mainland speech.

**The lost _-ng_:** A closing _-ng_ has gone into the vowel before it, which is held long: _téng_, heaven, is _tē_; _jing_, metal, is _jī_; _wōng_, the sea, is _wō_. A closing _-n_ stays, held for a beat of its own.

**Consonants:** The islands have no _zh_ and no _l_: _zh_ became _z_ and _l_ a tapped _r_, so the mainland's _zhò_, dragon, is _zo_. An _h_ before _u_ became _f_, and a _t_ before _u_ became _ts_, so _hou_, harmony, is _fū_ and _tōu_, earth, is _tsū_. Inside a compound the second part's voiceless opening is voiced: _chai_, mountain, stands alone with its _ch_ and closes a compound as _-jai_, so the Nine Mountains are _Jenjai_. Two consonants meet only where a syllable's _-n_ stands before the next. The overall effect is a language of open, evenly weighted syllables—flatter than Tāntsou, and markedly easier for a foreigner to pronounce badly enough to be understood.

**Prosody:** Even mora-timing, a long vowel and a closing _-n_ each counting as a beat. Jürthāti verse is counted rather than stressed or rhymed, which is why the short forms—the nine-mora and eighteen-mora shapes composed at every level of society—travel so poorly into western tongues.

## Grammar Notes

**Word order:** Subject-Object-Verb, rigidly. The verb is final and carries the honorific load, so the socially decisive information in a Jürthāti sentence arrives last, and interrupting a speaker before the verb is both rude and uninformative.

**Particles, not cases:** Grammatical relations are marked by postposed particles rather than by suffixed case endings—_ku_ after the doer of the act, _ri_ binding a word to the one after it, _bo_ closing a question. Tāntsou grammarians treat this divergence from mainland practice as the clearest evidence of decay.

**Honorific register:** Three tiers of verb (humble, neutral, honorific) interact with a further distinction between plain and polite forms, and with a witnessed form for speech before a third person of standing, giving a practical grid of twelve ways to say the same act. The verb _wa_, to answer, is _wanū_ when the speaker humbly answers, _wadān_ when a lord answers, and _wadānrēwū_ when a retainer reports his lord's answer to a guest in the lord's hall. Retainer speech to a lord, lord to retainer, lord to lord of equal rank, and any of these in the presence of a third party are all formally distinct.

**No grammatical gender**, and no number marking on the noun; both are supplied by context or by counters, of which there are many, sorted by the shape and nature of the thing counted.

## Script & Literacy

Jürthāti is written in [[skill-tengchouscript|Téng Chou]], the same logographic Celestial Record the mainland uses—the script is not tied to either language, and a Jürthāti retainer and a Tāntsou minister who cannot exchange three spoken sentences can negotiate in writing without difficulty. Neither court will concede a separate script to the other, so the islands write the mainland's characters and read them aloud in their own tongue.

Two island habits diverge. First, the reading of a character in Jürthāti is frequently not its Tāntsou reading, and educated Jürthāti hold **two** readings for many characters—the native word and the borrowed mainland pronunciation—with the choice between them carrying its own register. The realm's name, its ranks and castes, its titles and its rites are spoken in the mainland pronunciation, tones and all, while the same characters read in the native way give _Jenjai_ for _Jénchài_ and _dōzo_ for a great lord's _Daozhuo_. Second, calligraphy in the formal hand is a spiritual discipline in Jénchài rather than a bureaucratic qualification, studied by warrior-retainers alongside blade-work and tea-craft, so literacy runs higher among the island aristocracy than among the mainland rank they correspond to.

## Regional Dialects

**Court Jürthāti (Chài Kōwēn):** The prestige speech of the ceremonial Emperor's household and the great lords' courts. Slowest, most honorific-dense, and the standard for poetry.

**Coastal dialects:** The speech of the harbor provinces, with a considerable Valtári and Tāntsou trade vocabulary and much of the honorific grid worn smooth by use.

**Mountain dialects:** Conservative, keeping a closing _-ng_ the standard has lengthened away, and often unintelligible to a coastal speaker at speed.

**Shrine register:** Archaic, used in Celestial Order rites, and preserving mainland forms whole, tones and all, centuries after ordinary speech abandoned them.

## Sample Phrases

- **"Jen chai, kei tē."**—"Nine mountains, one heaven." (Proverbial; the claim to an undivided Mandate.)
- **"Ja ku wa."**—"The blade answers." (Of Ruìsing, the island form of ritual death, taken as a mode of speech.)
- **"Chai ku jō bo?"**—"Does the mountain fall?" (Of patience under pressure; the question needs no answer.)
- **"Kobei ku wanūrē."**—"This unworthy one humbly answers." (A formal acceptance, in the humble and polite forms.)
- **"Tānzo ku wadān bo?"**—"Does my lord answer?" (The furthest a retainer may press a lord for a decision.)

## Related Languages

Jürthāti's nearest relative by far is [[skill-tantsoulng|Tāntsou]], from which it descends; the relationship is close enough that a great deal of vocabulary is transparently shared, and distant enough that spoken mutual intelligibility is poor to nil, particularly in the honorific machinery, which Tāntsou simply does not have. Written communication is unaffected. Sustained trade contact has left Jürthāti with a stratum of [[skill-valtarlng|Valtári]] harbor vocabulary and a smaller one of [[skill-tantsoulng|Tāntsou]] administrative terms readopted in their mainland pronunciation.

## Naming Traditions

A Jürthāti name is house first, personal second—the reverse of western practice and a direct statement about which of the two matters. Personal names are granted rather than inherited among the retainer classes, most often at coming of age or on the occasion of a service worth marking, and may be granted again: a man who changes lords, takes vows, or survives a defeat that ought to have killed him may be renamed, and the old name is not used afterward. A granted name is two words, the second voiced as in any compound: _Rēwei_, dawn-jade; _Tsuija_, iron-blade.

Great Lords' houses take their names from features of their mountains—a peak, a strait, a hot spring, a stand of cedar—so that a house name is also a map reference: _Yanjai_, Cloud Peak; _Hojūjai_, Hot Spring Place. Retainer families take theirs from the office they have held longest, which is why so many Jürthāti surnames translate as things like Gate-Keeping, Second Fletching, and Records of the Salt, _Beshejū_.
