Ki'i'chek is a tongue of the Ki'i'chek (independent isolate) family. Fluency measures the sophistication of expression in Ki'i'chek, from the halting phrases of a traveler to the nuanced and learned discourse of a native speaker. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

The roman inventory also keeps the curly catch apostrophe `’` in established names such as Ishkul Ch’alish and Ne’ Ki’ahkul, as the phonetic rules below record. It has the same lexical catch role as the straight apostrophe, not a quotation or pause role. New ordinary words use the straight apostrophe.

## Overview

Ki'i'chek is the ancient language of the [[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik city-states]], a civilization that predates human settlement of [[place-ankrscntnnt|Ankaris]] by millennia. Spoken across an entire separate continent, Ki'i'chek stands alone—no known linguistic relatives exist anywhere on [[place-worldthlrn|Thalorna]]. The language is deeply connected to Ki'ich'chik cosmology, mathematics, and spiritual worldview. To the untrained ear, Ki'i'chek sounds exotic and mysterious: a cascade of glottal stops and sharp consonants interspersed with clear vowel sounds, with rising phrase endings in ceremonial performance; pitch does not distinguish everyday word meanings.

Ki'i'chek employs a complex hieroglyphic writing system that encodes both phonetic and logographic information. The language reflects the Ki'ich'chik obsession with order, hierarchy, astronomical cycles, and the sacred balance between the natural and divine worlds.

## How Ki'i'chek sounds

Ki'i'chek is clipped and glottal, built of short syllables with separately sounded catches in the throat. Its flavour palette favours k, ch, ts and sh, clear vowels, and endings such as -al, -ik, -ul, -mah and -bal. Common words stay short. Avoid dense new clusters, obligatory pitch marks and strings of apostrophes.

**The vowels.** _a_, _i_, _u_ and _e_, with _o_ rarer.

**The consonants.** _k_, _ts_, _ch_, _p_ and _q_ are ordinary consonants; an apostrophe following one adds a separate catch. _Sh_ is as in ship, _ch_ as in church and _l_ as in let. A plain _k_ is fine for _q_.

**Clusters.** Few. Syllables are a consonant and a vowel, closing on _-l_, _-k_, _-n_ or _-m_.

**Endings.** Ki'i'chek words end in _-al_, _-ik_, _-ul_, _-mah_ or _-bal_.

**What Ki'i'chek leaves out.** New ordinary words normally carry no more than two apostrophes, written with the straight apostrophe. Established compounds keep their spellings. No word requires an ejective consonant or lexical tone.

## Phonology

### Consonants

Ki'i'chek phonology is dominated by hard stops and sibilants, creating a percussive, rhythmic quality:

Glottal stops (')
: Fundamental to the language; often serve as syllable delimiters

Hard consonants
: K, T, P, Q, with their ordinary readings in the shared guide; a plain K is fine for Q

Sibilants
: SH as in ship, TS as at the end of cats, CH as in church; older descriptions using X or TZ do not supply alternative everyday spellings

Liquids
: L as in let, allowing for some softness amid the hardness

### Vowels

Five clear vowels, each pronounced distinctly:

- **A** (father)—bright, open
- **E** (met)—mid, unrounded
- **I** (machine)—high, clear
- **O** (go)—mid, rounded
- **U** (flute)—high, rounded

Vowels rarely diphthongize; each vowel sound stands alone. This clarity aids comprehension when chanting sacred texts or astronomical calculations.

### Stress and Prosody

Stress typically falls on the second-to-last syllable unless marked otherwise. Ceremonial speech may rise at phrase boundaries, while everyday speech is flatter. This is performance and context, not a lexical tone system: a pitch change does not create another word, and ordinary transcription needs no pitch marks. The canonical names are exceptions to the common vowel value for i: **Ki'i'chek** /kɪʔˈɪʔtʃɛk/ is kih–'IH–'chek, and **Ki'ich'chik** /kɪʔˈɪtʃʔtʃɪk/ is kih–'ICH–'chik. Both have three syllables, second-syllable stress and two separately sounded catches. K and CH remain ordinary consonants. Elsewhere the common vowel values apply unless an established word specifies an exception.

### Distinctive Phonetic Patterns

Analyzing the name corpus reveals consistent patterns:

- Names frequently begin with hard consonants (K', B, T, H, N, Z)
- Glottal stops often mark syllable boundaries, preventing consonant clusters
- Vowel-final words are common, facilitating fluid speech
- Three-syllable names are the norm; longer names denote importance
- The sequences -ALAM, -IKAN, -TSUL, and -KUL appear frequently in compounds

## Grammar Notes

### Sentence Structure

Ki'i'chek employs a **Subject-Object-Verb (SOV)** word order, fundamentally different from most Ankarian tongues:

Example
: _Ish'ik ha'nal nal'un_—"The lagoon (subj.) eats (verb) the river (obj.)"

This structure reflects Ki'ich'chik philosophical priorities: establishing who acts and what is acted upon before revealing the action itself.

### Word Order for Modifiers

Adjectives follow nouns; adverbs precede verbs. This allows the listener to ground themselves in the noun before applying descriptive qualities.

## Everyday speech

Everyday Ki'i'chek uses the same words across the city-states' mutually intelligible local dialects. The short forms below support a conversation, an ordinary letter or a work record. Temple titles and existing name compounds retain their established meanings. A bound stem is never spoken alone: ch'um- supports titles, while the ordinary free verb is ch'umun. Names do not automatically supply free vocabulary.

### Plain clauses and descriptions

Put the subject first, its object next and the invariant verb last. _Ni ne' ts'ik'an_ means “I drink water”; _ti nal'el nal'un_ means “You eat food.” The verb has the same form with I, you, a named person or a group, and in past or future statements. There is no required person, tense or mood ending. The separate words _ka_ and _na_ introduced below are location and source particles, not prefixes attached to a verb.

An adjective follows its noun: _ha'nal ch'akal_, a large river; _nalbal ishal_, a female child or daughter. A description ends with _k'alish_, be: _ha'nal ch'akal k'alish_, “The river is large”; _ni nal'in k'alish_, “I am healthy.” _Ha'nal k'alish_ says that a river exists. With an object, _ha'kan_ means have: _ni ne' ha'kan_, “I have water.” Its established descriptive homonym means cold: _ne' ha'kan k'alish_, “The water is cold.” The sentence roles make the difference clear. Neither word is respelled.

A manner word stands before the verb: _ni ts'anal k'ayulun_, “I watch attentively”; _ti sh'anik ch'umun_, “You speak quietly.” An adjective can serve as a manner word in this position without an ending. The dictionary gives each form one primary class; its gloss and usage note state any established descriptive sense or use as a request. Those uses keep the same spelling. Keep a recorded action group together after the object: _ni shil'ik ch'eshan p'itun_, “I buy a cup,” literally trade and take it; _ni shil'ik ch'eshan ch'iman_, “I sell a cup,” trade and give it. These groups share one subject and object. To change the actor, begin another complete clause.

A recipient has _ch'ul_ before it: _ni ne' ch'ul ti ch'iman_, “I give water to you.” The object and recipient both precede the final verb. A feeling can be something a person has: _ni ch'imal ha'kan_, “I feel gratitude”; _ni p'akal p'ishul_, “I feel pain,” states the bodily sensation. _Ni ti p'akan_ means “I hurt you,” causing pain; _ni p'akul_ means “I hurt,” experiencing it. The cause and experience are separate verbs.

### Reference, ownership and counting people

_Ni_ refers to the speaker, _ti_ to the addressee, _shi_ to another person and _sha_ to a nonpersonal thing. These same forms serve as subjects and objects. _Shi cham_ explicitly says he, and _shi ishal_ she; _shi_ alone leaves sex unstated. _Ni kesh_ is we, the speaker's group; _shi kesh_ is they, the relevant third-person group. In a group pronoun, kesh includes the stated group, not everyone in the world. _Nish_ refers back to the subject: _ni nish k'ayal_, “I see myself.”

_Chi'_ points to this and _tsi'_ to that; _chi' kesh_ and _tsi' kesh_ mean these and those. _Ch'ek_ is a thing, _ch'ekbal_ a person. With _kish_, some, these become _ch'ek kish_, something, and _ch'ekbal kish_, someone. _P'ekun_ leaves the choice open: _ch'ekbal p'ekun_, anyone. _Kesh_ gives everyone or everything in the relevant set. _Shu ch'ekbal kish_ means no one. _Shaq'ulik_ describes another or a different one, while _ts'omik_ says the same.

Place the thing possessed before _kil_ and its owner: _shil'ik kil ni_, my cup; _sh'ulum kil ti_, your house. The possession group can answer alone: _kil ni_, “Mine”; _kil ni kesh_, “Ours.” Body and kin words use the same construction: _p'ishum kil ni_, my hand; _nalma kil ti_, your mother. A following _kil_ group belongs inside the preceding relationship: _nalma kil nalma kil ni_ is my mother's mother. Keep each owner with its noun when the full group becomes an object: _ni shil'ik kil ti k'ayal_, “I see your cup.”

_Nalma ke kulma_ names the inclusive category parent, mother or father. In kin expressions the pair names that relationship, not a requirement that two parents are present. _Nalbal kil ts'ombal kil nalma ke kulma_ is a cousin, a child of a sibling of a parent. _Nalbal cham kil ts'ombal_ and _nalbal ishal kil ts'ombal_ are nephew and niece. If people in a discussion could be confused, use the actual parent or sibling's name in place of the category. _Ashbal_ is a spouse; _ashshubal_ specifically a surviving spouse after the other spouse's death. Merely being separated, shaq'al, does not make someone widowed. _Nalshubal_ is an orphan.

Nouns keep their dictionary form. A preceding number gives an exact count: _q'o ch'ekbal_, two people; _lishe shil'ik_, three cups. A following quantity gives an unnumbered group: _ch'ekbal tsal_, many people; _shil'ik kish_, some cups; _ch'ekbal kesh_, all the people under discussion. No plural ending is required. The head words in _p'ishum ts'il_, finger, and _shilakum ts'il_, toe, remain distinct: the first belongs to the hand, the second to the foot. _P'ishum nalum_ is the palm of the hand; no tree is meant.

### Places and movement

A location begins with _ka_: _ni ka sh'ulum k'alish_, “I am at home.” More specific relation words can replace it: _shil'ik ch'ikal sh'ulum k'alish_, “The cup is inside the house”; _shelem ts'ik shikmah k'alish_, “The bird is above the tree”; _ch'ekbal ch'al ts'anul k'alish_, “The person is under the roof.” _Ts'ik_ retains its established broader sense above or outside; _ts'ikal_ specifically says outside and _ch'ikal_ inside. _Ch'al_ can mean beneath or within a sheltering whole; these more specific words remove uncertainty.

_Chi' k'alish_ can point out “This exists,” while _sha ch'i k'alish_ says “It is here”: the final catch distinguishes _chi'_, this, from _ch'i_, here. _Ts'i_ means there. A destination follows _ch'ul_, a source _na_: _ni ch'ul ha'nal ts'akun_, “I go toward the river”; _ni na sh'ulum ch'ulun_, “I come from the house.” _Ch'ikal q'o_ gives between two stated things: _shil'ik ch'ikal q'o ha'tan ke lu'she k'alish_, “The cup is between the bowl and the pot.” _Ch'ikal kesh_ gives among a group.

Spatial _k'ayumal_, in front, and _ch'upumal_, behind, do not mean earlier and later. _P'ishil_ and _p'ishulash_ name left and right as directions. _Ts'en ch'ulun_, “the sun comes,” conventionally names east in a direction context and dawn in a time context. _Ts'en ishan_, “the sun sleeps,” similarly names west or dusk. _Nukal_ is north and _toshal_ south. A direction name does not identify another culture's people or extend Ki'i'chek to the continent's northern language communities.

### Time, event shape and quantities

A time phrase before the subject frames the clause: _ts'ena na'ish ni ne' ts'ik'an_, “Yesterday I drank water”; _ts'ena ch'ush ni ch'ul ha'nal ts'akun_, “Tomorrow I go toward the river.” _Ch'ish_ says now, _ts'ish_ then; _na'ish_ orders events before a time and _ch'ush_ after it. _Ts'enal_ is time, _ts'enik_ a moment, _ts'ena_ a day and _p'uqal_ a night. The noun _ts'en_ keeps its sun and solar-year senses, while _ish'uqal_ names the moon itself; the existing _ts'uqal_ remains moonlight and the lunar count.

No aspect ending is needed. _Ni ts'amesh nal'un_ says “I have already eaten”; _ni ts'anesh nal'un_, “I am still eating.” _Ni sh'ek nal'un_ says “I eat again.” _Ni shu sh'ekish nal'un_ means “I have not eaten yet.” Context supplies the relevant time, and an explicit day or moment can make it precise. A noun with _ts'enalik ch'akal_ lasts a long time; _ish'lanik_ describes spatial length. _Ts'enal tq'an_ marks the time until a boundary, and _ts'enal na_ the time since a stated starting point. These are whole relation groups: _ni ts'enal tq'an ts'ena ch'ush ts'anun_, “I wait until tomorrow.”

Counting remains base twenty. _Olek' lishe_ is thirteen; _q'o ts'ob lishe_ is forty-three. Multiply a named scale by the number before it, then add lower places in descending order: _lishe pel'ush ts'ishe ts'ob p'o_ is 1,325. A single unmultiplied _ts'ob_ followed by units can name twenty and those units: _ts'ob q'o_ is twenty-two. _P'o ts'ob_ gives one hundred, and _q'o pel'ush olek' ts'ob_ one thousand. _Ts'amal_ is zero, a real empty place rather than an omitted spoken digit. The place-unit word _k'aqil_ does not replace the ordinary number _pe'_, one.

Add _ts'ek_ after a complete cardinal to give its position: _pe' ts'ek_, first; _olek' pe' ts'ek_, eleventh; _ts'ob q'o ts'ek_, twenty-second. This applies to later ordinals without a new list of number words. _Q'o p'ek_ is one of two equal divisions, half; _mushe p'ek_ is one of four, a quarter. For more than one share, state the numerator with the portion noun: _lishe p'ekel kil mushe p'ek_, three portions of a quarter, or three quarters. _Pe' pe'_ distributes one by one; _kesh pe' pe'_ says every member. _Shu kish_ answers none, whereas _ts'amal_ is the numeral zero.

_Tsash_ gives more, _tsish_ less and _ts'ash_ enough for the relevant purpose. _P'ekik_ is equal. _Ha'nal ch'akal tsash na ts'ashal tsi' k'alish_ means “The river is bigger than that”: the standard follows the whole group _na ts'ashal_. _Nal'esh tsash_ means better, and _nal'esh tsash kesh_ best in the comparison set. _Kul'ukal tsil_, light in weight, does not describe colour; _k'inishik p'il_ explicitly means light in colour. _Tsal tq'an ts'ikal_ says too much, much beyond the limit; _tsil shu ts'ash_, too little, an insufficient small quantity.

### Questions, denials and the scope of a claim

A question word takes the place of the missing information: _ch'ib ne' ts'ik'an_, “Who drinks water?”; _ti ch'ik k'ayal_, “What do you see?”; _ti ch'ika ts'akun_, “Where do you go?” _Kil ch'ib_ asks whose, _ch'ul ch'ib_ to whom, _na ch'ika_ from where and _ch'ul ch'ikul_ for what purpose. _Ti ch'itsal shil'ik ha'kan_ asks “How many cups do you have?” Here the quantity question occupies the same preceding position as a numeral. The same _ch'itsal_ asks how much of a substance.

A final _chi_ makes a yes/no question: _ti ne' ts'ik'an chi_, “Do you drink water?” Answer _ts'e_, yes, or _shuk_, no. _Chi sh'ek_ asks for clarification or a repeat. The catch matters: _chi_ asks whether, _chi'_ points to this, and _ch'i_ locates here. The familiar punctuation in these transcribed examples is an aid to the reader; the question is carried by its words, without claiming that a reader's question mark is a native glyph.

_Shu_ is the negative, spoken as one syllable. Place it directly before the predicate it denies: _ni shu nal'un_, “I do not eat”; _ne' shu ha'kan k'alish_, “The water is not cold.” Before a participant or quantity it denies that group: _shu ch'ekbal kish_, no one. With a verb group it denies the group as a whole. To deny only one of two acts, state separate clauses. _Shu mah'ik_ denies an obligation; it does not prohibit the act. The prohibition word _shu'ik_ positively says must not.

_Ts'ekel_ is truth, a claim's agreement with reality. _Ish'tsal_ is certainty, the speaker's being sure. _Kul'ushal_ is confidence in ability or expected success, and _ts'ushal_ trust in another's reliability. _Ni ish'tsal ha'kan_ says “I feel certain,” without proving the claim true. _Ch'umel shu ts'ekal_ is a false statement that may be an honest mistake; _ch'umel p'uqik_, dark speech, conventionally means an intentional lie. _Nalichash_ asserts sincerity, while _na k'ayel_ reports apparent evidence and _na ch'umel_ reported speech. _Ish'umik shi ch'ulun_ means “Perhaps that person comes.” None of these qualifications silently turns uncertainty into falsehood.

### Connections, permission and quoted speech

_Ke_ joins people or clauses, _p'ekash_ offers alternatives and _shak_ introduces a contrast. _K'ul_ introduces a reason: _ni ka sh'ulum k'alish k'ul ne'chan ch'ekun_, “I am at home because rain happens.” A condition begins with _ch'esh_ and its consequence with _ts'esh_: _ch'esh ne'chan ch'ekun, ts'esh ni ka sh'ulum k'alish_, “If it rains, then I am at home.” _Ch'esh shu_ means unless, if not; _shaq'ash_ introduces the other case. _Ch'ul k'ul_ gives a purpose: _ni ch'ul ha'nal ts'akun ch'ul k'ul ni ne' ts'ik'an_, “I go to the river in order to drink water.” Repeat the subject in a full linked clause, especially when participants change.

A modal stands before its verb, after the object or place phrase: _ni ne' kul'ik ts'ik'an_, “I can drink water,” states ability; _ni ts'omi ts'akun_, “I may go,” states permission; _ni mah'ik ts'akun_, “I must go,” states obligation; _ni shu'ik ts'akun_, “I must not go,” states prohibition. _Ni shu kul'ik ts'akun_ says I cannot go; _ni shu mah'ik ts'akun_ says I am not required to go. _Ni ish'umik ch'ekik ts'akun_ says “I might go,” an event's possibility, not permission. _Nal'esh ch'ikan_ before a following action recommends it as something one should do: _ni nal'esh ch'ikan ts'anun_, “I should wait.” These are a few ordinary particles and recorded groups, not a person or mood paradigm.

Quoted speech keeps its complete sentence order. In common transcription a writer can set it after the speech clause with the reader's quotation marks: _shi ch'uman: “Ni ch'ulun.”_ means “That person says: ‘I come.’” The quotation's _ni_ refers to the quoted speaker. For a plain indirect report, name the speaker, use _ch'uman_, and then give the full claim with explicit participants: _shi ch'uman: shi ch'ulun_, “That person says that they come.” The transcription separates the clauses; this is not a newly invented native punctuation system. When ambiguity matters, repeat the person's name rather than relying on successive _shi_.

### Courtesy, affection and performance

An ordinary request uses the bare verb with the addressee understood: _k'ayun_, listen; _ts'anun_, wait. Objects and locations still come before it: _ni kul'iman_, “Help me”; _ni sh'alan_, “Forgive me”; _ch'i ch'ulun_, “Come here.” _P'ekun_ makes a courteous request: _p'ekun ni kul'iman_, “Please help me.” _Ch'ish nal'esh_, good now, greets; _ish'lan nal'esh_, good road, takes leave; _ch'ulun nal'esh_ welcomes. These fixed courtesy expressions keep their conventional meanings rather than requiring the listener to reconstruct a full clause. _Ch'imal ti_ thanks the listener, and _ch'imal ni ke ti_ accepts thanks. _Ni nal'ukel ha'kan_ expresses regret, “I am sorry.”

For conversation repair, _ni ish'ekan_ says “I understand,” and _ni shu ish'ekan_ “I do not understand.” _Ti ch'ik ish'antan_ asks “What do you mean?”; _ti ch'ishan ch'uman_ asks “How do you say…?” with the intended thing supplied by context or a pointing gesture. _K'elal kil chi' ch'ik k'alish_ asks “What is this called?” or literally “What is the name of this?” _Ts'ekal chi_ expresses surprise or asks “Really?” The four loves are separate nouns: _ch'ashal_ romantic love, _ts'omashal_ affectionate friendship, _nalashal_ family love and _keshashal_ selfless love extended without kinship or repayment. _Ch'ashul_ is lust. _Ts'omek_ names companionship, the experience of being with a companion, rather than treating it as another love. _Ni ch'ul ti ch'ashal ha'kan_ says “I feel romantic love for you”; replacing that noun with _ts'omashal_ gives affectionate friendship. Neither phrase silently asserts sexual desire.

Verse and formal performance can use these same invariant clauses, repetitions and explicit parallel noun groups. Keep the subject or relationship explicit when it is needed for meaning. Ceremonial phrase rises and deliberate pauses can shape delivery, but leave the written words and their lexical catches intact. New verse should be written from the recorded free words and usable constructions; these examples do not translate or replace existing poems.

## Script and Literacy

Ki'i'chek writing—[[skill-chlxplmscrpt|Ch'alish P'ilam]], "the glyphs of Ch'alish", after [[lore-ixilanchalixdty|Ish'ilan Ch'alish]], the goddess whom tradition credits with composing the first codex—records **meaning, not speech**. Each glyph is a concept, and two or three glyphs together make a further concept, the first usually the head and those after it modifying it, as Ki'i'chek puts adjectives after the noun. A text says what happened or what was meant. Nobody in it is quoted exactly and grammar is never written out; an ending such as _-ok_ or _-bal_ is a sound that a glyph may write but that context usually supplies. The script has no spelling. Every spoken sound is written in the roman transcription on this page, which is what lets a reader say a word.

Where the script does write sounds, as in a name, it uses syllable signs. A closing consonant takes a syllable whose vowel is silent, usually an echo of the vowel before it: _Ch'akul_ is _cha-ku-lu_. A catch is left to the reader.

Key script features:

Glyphs
: Signs for concepts (water, jaguar, maize, sky), combined two or three at a time into further concepts

Syllable signs
: Signs that write a sound, used for names and for the endings a scribe wants fixed

Common hand
: Simple signs built from a few strokes, each filling an undrawn square cell and set in columns two signs wide. A row of two reads left to right, then the next row down, then the next column. A miner writes in chalk on a shaft wall, a carpenter in chalk on a slate or wooden palette, a mercantyler with a stylus on bark paper, and a scribe with brush or pen on vellum, where the hand becomes calligraphy

Ceremonial hand
: Every sign drawn as its own picture inside a drawn box, on temple walls, monuments, pyramids and posts around a village common. It shares its readings with the common hand and is not an elaboration of it

Alongside the glyphs runs a second system entirely: the [[skill-ktzlcrdscrpt|K'otsel]], knotted cord records in colored thread, carrying tribute tallies, census and the day-counts, tied and read only by the priestly scribes.

Literacy is a sliding scale. Almost everyone knows the 24 core glyphs and a few more specific to their trade. Many people know a good number of two-glyph compounds and a couple of three-glyph ones. Scribes know the most: four- and five-glyph compounds, the lesser-known glyphs and the sound of every glyph, so reading by sound is a scribal skill. Priests know fewer glyphs than scribes but all the sacred glyphs, which even scribes may not. The northern nations do not use the script or the language, and Ki'ich'chik scribes write about them as foreigners. K'otsel records numbers, positions and conventional messages; it is not a writing of arbitrary spoken sentences. The [[skill-chlxplmscrpt|script note]] gives the core glyphs, the hands and their surfaces.

## Historical Development

Ki'i'chek emerged in the earliest Ki'ich'chik settlements, predating the development of their complex calendar and astronomical mathematics. Over millennia, the language absorbed vocabulary related to celestial observation, architectural achievement, and ritual practice, but its core structure remained stable.

The language preserves archaic elements in formal speech and sacred texts—older verb forms and syllable patterns not used in everyday conversation. This linguistic conservatism reflects Ki'ich'chik reverence for ancestral knowledge.

## Regional Dialects

The Ki'ich'chik city-states share one language, with mutually intelligible local dialects. Temples, shared religious practice, and communication along the white roads maintain intelligibility across the city-state network. The northern Indigenous nations are outside Ki'ich'chik culture and this language community; their languages are distinct and are not Ki'ich'chik dialects.

## Related Languages

Ki'i'chek exists in **complete isolation** on Thalorna. No other language shares its grammatical structure, phonological inventory, or root vocabulary. Scholars debate whether this reflects genuine historical separation (a separate diaspora) or merely the vast age of both the language and the Ki'ich'chik civilization.

Minimal borrowing occurs between Ki'i'chek and other Thalorna languages. A few terms related to exotic Ki'ich'chik goods have entered maritime pidgins (e.g., _ch'ishal_—the bitter bean drink), but Ki'i'chek speakers pride themselves on linguistic purity.

## Naming Traditions

### Structure and Convention

Ki'ich'chik names are **compound forms without gender distinction**—a person's name is equally valid regardless of sex. Names often function as brief poetic statements, encapsulating qualities, achievements, or circumstances of birth.

### Components

1. **Primary element**: A descriptor, animal, natural force, or deity name (e.g., K'ushi, Tsul, Ts'ultel, Ish'bal)
2. **Secondary element**: Modifies or completes the meaning (e.g., Ne'ok, Ts'alam, Ne')
3. **Tertiary element** (optional): For high-ranking individuals or complex biographical claims (e.g., Ch'akul Ts'ultel Ish'bal Tsul)

Names with three or more elements signal importance: nobility, priesthood, or exceptional deeds. Commoners typically carry two-element names.

### Philosophical Naming

Names encode philosophy. A child born during floods might be named _Ne' Ishkul_ ("water within the lake") to mark the auspicious conjunction. A warrior who survived wounds might be renamed _Tsul Ne'ok_ ("strength flows") to commemorate resilience.

### Titles and Honorifics

Titles are appended to names, not fused:

_Q'emulmah_
: "Overlord" (ruler of a city-state)

_Ha'nalmah_
: "Landholder" (chief of a district)

_Ts'omalil_
: "One Who Belongs" (full citizen)

### House Names

A name of three or more elements is too long for the household, and the Ki'ich'chik shorten it by taking its _first element whole_. The first element is the first name-word of the compound, spoken exactly as it stands in the full name, glottal stops included: it is never clipped to a syllable, never given an ending, and never replaced by a pet name, because each element is already a word with its own meaning. That short form is the _house name_.

- _Ch'akul Ts'ultel Ish'bal Tsul_ is _Ch'akul_ at home.
- _Ish'bal Ts'omalil K'inish_ is _Ish'bal_. A connector such as _Ts'omalil_ is never the house name, even where it stands second.
- _Shak'nal Mah'alil Shilan_ is _Shak'nal_. _Mah'alil_ is a rank word, and no title or rank word is ever the house name.
- _Ne' Ishkul_ and _Tsul Ne'ok_ have no house name. A two-element name is short already and is spoken whole by everyone.

The house name follows rank. Kin, the household and people of equal standing use it, and so does anyone of higher standing speaking to someone below. Someone of lower standing never does: a farmer addresses a lord by the full name, a priest of lesser grade addresses a hierarch by the full name, and a title is always appended to the full name and never to the house name. The full name is also the only name in the record. The K'otsel census cords and the Ch'alish P'ilam glyphs carry the full compound, and a house name is never knotted or written.

## Phonetic Rules

Syllables
: Ki'ich'chik words are typically 2-4 syllables, with occasional longer words for important concepts.

Consonants
: Common consonants include:

    Glottal stops
    : `’`

    Hard sounds
    : `k`, `t`, `p`, `q`

    Soft sounds
    : `x`, `ch`, `tz`, `l`

Vowels
: The five vowels (a, e, i, o, u) are pronounced clearly.

Stress
: Stress generally falls on the second-to-last syllable unless otherwise specified.

## Naming Rules

1.  **Structure**:
    - Names often follow a descriptive phrase format: _noun + verb + descriptor_, e.g., “Jaguar prowls beneath the moon.”

2.  **Gendered Themes**:
    - **Male Names**: Predatory animals, quick motion, violence, great things (e.g., mountains, storms).
    - **Female Names**: Prey animals, flowers, stillness, peace, gentle natural features.

3.  **House Names**:
    - A formal name of three or more elements is shortened for informal use to its first element, whole, as set out under House Names above; a two-element name is not shortened.

4.  **Titles**:
    - Titles are appended to names to denote rank or role, e.g., _Q’emulmah_ (Overlord), _Ha’nalmah_ (Landholder).

5.  **Connector Words**:
    - **Member of Tribe**: _Ts’omalil_ (“One Who Belongs”).
    - **Leader of Tribe**: _Mah’alil_ (“Lord of the Tribe”).
    - **Tq'an**
      - **Meaning**: _Edge, boundary, or connection_
      - **Usage**: Often used to describe a transitional or liminal state, such as the edge of a natural feature (e.g., horizon, riverbank, or dawn) or the point of connection between two elements. It can imply a sense of joining or being on the verge of something.
    - **Ch'al**
      - **Meaning**: _Beneath, under, or within_
      - **Usage**: Commonly appears in phrases that describe something located under or within a larger feature, such as beneath the moonlight, under a tree, or within sacred waters. It suggests immersion, protection, or positioning relative to something overarching.
    - **Ts'ik**
      - **Usage**: Represents something elevated, external, or outside of an encompassing space.
      - **Examples**:
        - _Ts'ik k’inish_ ("Above the sunlight")
        - _Ts'ik ha'nal_ ("Outside the river")
    - **Shaq'al**
      - **Usage**: Indicates disconnection, separation, or being apart from something.
      - **Examples**:
        - _Shaq'al shik'tel_ ("Separate from the sacred grove")
        - _Shaq'al k'inish_ ("Disconnected from the sunlight")

### Structure of the Ki'ich'chik Numerical System

Base Numbers (1–19)
: The numbers from one to ten are single words; eleven to nineteen are _olek’_ (ten) followed by the unit, as two words.

Multiples of 20
: Numbers from 20 onward are combinations of multiples of 20, with separate terms for higher powers of 20:

    K’aqil
    : 1 (20⁰)

    Ts’ob
    : 20 (20¹)

    Pel’ush
    : 400 (20²)

    Q’umpel
    : 8,000 (20³)

    Sholemash
    : 160,000 (20⁴) Higher powers follow similar patterns, though rarely used in daily life.

Positional Representation
: Each place represents a power of 20, with the first position for units, the second for 20s, the third for 400s, and so on.

## Ki'i'chek Lexicon {#lexicon}

A scribe of [[affiliation-ixilankul|Ish'ilankul]] learns two things before a single glyph: the counts, and the endings. A word in Ki'i'chek names a thing, and an ending says who stands toward it: _p'ilam_ is a glyph, and the _p'ilambal_ is the one who serves the glyphs; _ts'en_ is the sun, the _ts'enmah_ is the lord who rules under it, and the _ts'enbal_ is the priest who keeps its count. The same handful of endings make every title in a Ki'ich'chik court, so a ruler's whole household can be read from its titles alone.

This page is the word-hoard of the [[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik]] city-states: the words the language note uses, what each ending makes of them, the rules a new word is built by, and the register of every name the setting gives to the continent's realms, gods, rites and scripts.

The sound of Ki'i'chek is percussive. An apostrophe is a separately sounded catch in the throat, a glottal stop, including after an ordinary consonant: _k'ayik_, _ne'_, _ish'bal_. Stress falls on the second-last syllable, and the five vowels never glide into one another.

## How to read the dictionary {#how-to-read-the-dictionary}

An entry gives the word as it is cited: a noun bare, a verb bare. A form that closes on a hyphen is a bound stem, which never stands alone. A word made of smaller parts names the pieces the form is made of, joined by `+`, and the form is exactly what they make.

### Fields

```sql
SELECT name AS "Field", holds AS "What it holds"
FROM fields
WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kiichek'
ORDER BY position
```

A word opens on a vowel, on a single consonant or on the pair below, and closes on a vowel or on one of the consonants below. A bound stem, written with a closing hyphen, closes on anything.

## Building words {#building-words}

Two moves make every built word on this page. **A compound** sets two words side by side, the first naming what the second is about: _ne'_ water and _ts'ik_ above make _ne'ts'ik_, an island, what stands above the water. **An ending on a word** says what part the word plays, and the endings stand below. A title takes its ending last, so _ts'en_ with _-il_ and then _-bal_ is _ts'enilbal_, the prince, the one who serves what belongs to the sun.

### Suffixes

```sql
SELECT '`' || form || '`' AS "Suffix", attaches AS "Stem", gloss AS "Makes", coalesce(nullif(array_to_string(list_transform(examples, e -> regexp_replace(e, '^(\S+) > ([^\s,;]+)', '_\1_ > _\2_')), ', '), ''), '—') AS "Example"
FROM affixes
WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kiichek'
ORDER BY position
```

## Words {#words}

### Sky, sun and the count of days

Four counts run at once, and each has its word: _ts'en_, the sun and its year; _ts'uqal_, the moon; _ish'ilan_, the stars; and _tq'an_, the count of 260 days that is also the word for an edge or a joining. A date is written as the meeting of several counts, and the fifty-two-year _K'ayik Kul'al_, the Living Flame, falls where _ts'en_ and _tq'an_ come back into phase. Zero, _ts'amal_, is a shell: the empty thing that holds a place.

### Water and land

Water is _ne'_, and the land is named by what water does to it: _ne'ts'ik_, an island, is what stands above the water, and _ne'ok_ is land or a river running with it. The sacred heights are _kul'qat_, the peak a pyramid is built to stand for, and every great road is an _ish'lan_.

### Living things

The great beasts give their names to peoples and realms: the jaguar, _ish'bal_; the eagle, _shak'nal_; the bird, _shelem_; the serpent, _ch'akun_. Flowers carry the light they bloom in, so the sun blossom is _pakum_ and the moon bloom _ish'mel_.

### Rank, temple and court

A title is a word with an ending: _-mah_ makes the lord of a thing and _-bal_ the one who serves it. So the _ts'enmah_ is the Sun Lord and the _ts'enbal_ the priest who keeps his count; the _p'ilambal_ serves the glyphs, the _ch'alishbal_ serves the sacred, and the _ch'umbal_ carries a lord's spoken word along the roads. A people's members are _ts'omalil_, those who belong by the _ts'om_, the bond.

```sql
SELECT form AS "Form", class AS "Class", gloss AS "Gloss", built_text AS "Built from", coalesce(array_to_string(list_transform(attested, a -> '[[' || a || '|]]'), ', '), '—') AS "Attested"
FROM words
WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kiichek' AND field = 'Rank, temple and court' AND split_part(class, ' ', 1) IN ('name', 'stem', 'pref')
ORDER BY position
```

### House and hearth

The words of the house are plain nouns, and few of them take endings.

### Doing and being

A verb is cited bare. An adjective follows its noun: _ha'nal ts'uma_, a misty river. Three words here, _nelush_, _toshel_ and _puq'em_, are met mostly in names.

```sql

```

### Grammar words

Three short words say where a thing stands: _ch'al_ beneath it, _ts'ik_ above it, _shaq'al_ apart from it. The numbers count by twenties. One to ten are single words; eleven to nineteen are _olek'_, ten, followed by the unit as a second word, so _olek' lishe_ is thirteen. Above them stand the powers of twenty: _ts'ob_, _pel'ush_, _q'umpel_ and _sholemash_.

## Making names {#making-names}

**A Ki'ich'chik person** carries a compound of words, each element a word with its meaning, and the name reads as a short statement: _Ne' Ishkul_, water within the lake; _Tsul Ne'ok_, strength flows. A commoner carries two elements; three or more mark a noble, a priest or a person of great deeds, and such a name is shortened at home to its first element, never to a title or a connector. Titles are appended after the full name and never fused to it.

**A realm or a city** is named as a person is, by words set side by side: _Shelem_, the land of birds; _P'enqal Ch'akal_, the great grassland; _Ish'ilankul_, the seat of the stars, with _-kul_, the seat, closing the name of a city that rules.

**A god** is named by two words, a thing and its quality, and the Itsani faiths carry those names whole: _K'ayik Ch'alish_, the sacred flame; _Ts'en'ul Tq'an_, the sun at the world's edge.

## Scope {#scope}

The register below holds every name of every note of the Ki'ich'chik lands and their gods. A note is in the register when one of these values names it:

```sql
SELECT CASE kind WHEN 'paths' THEN 'path' WHEN 'addresses' THEN 'address' END AS "Kind",
       string_agg('`' || CASE WHEN kind IN ('addresses', 'skip') THEN regexp_replace(value, '^thalorna-note-', '') WHEN kind IN ('paths', 'types') THEN value ELSE regexp_replace(value, '^thalorna-(?:note-[a-z]+-|none-folder-)', '') END || '`', ' ') AS "Value"
FROM scope WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kiichek'
GROUP BY kind ORDER BY CASE kind WHEN 'paths' THEN 0 WHEN 'addresses' THEN 1 END
```

## Attested names {#attested-names}

Every name the setting gives a place, people, god, rite or script of the Ki'ich'chik lands, with the note where it is met. A name built from this page shows how; a name the tongue uses that is not built from it shows `—`, and a name in the reader's tongue is listed so that the register is complete.

### Tongues

```sql
SELECT '`' || tag || '`' AS "Tongue", meaning || coalesce(CASE tag WHEN 'kiichek' THEN ', [[' || tongue_language || '|Ki''i''chek Language]]' END, '') AS "Meaning"
FROM tongues WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kiichek' ORDER BY position
```

### Names

```sql
SELECT '**' || name || '**' AS "Name",
       coalesce(array_to_string(list_transform(notes, n -> '[[' || n || '|' || name || ']]'), ', '), '—') AS "Notes",
       '`' || tongue || '`' AS "Tongue",
       coalesce(array_to_string(list_transform(built, x -> '`' || x || '`'), ' + '), '—') AS "Built from"
FROM register WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kiichek' ORDER BY position
```

## Retired names {#retired-names}

A retired name is one the setting does not write: an Earth word, or a name built on one. Each stands below with the name written in its place, and no page writes it.
