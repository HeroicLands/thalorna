The Kelwanuk Language is the tongue of the [[affiliation-kelwanuk|Kelwanuk]], the Cedar People of the western coast. Fluency measures the range of what a speaker can say in it, from a southern trader's handful of words for oil, copper and price to the speech of a Speaker who can recite a house's names and seats, in order, at a feast. As with all specific languages, this skill inherits its mechanics from the general [[sohl-none-docskill-lang|Language]] skill.

## Overview

Every village of the Cedar People speaks the Kelwanuk Language, and every house speaks it a little differently. The fjord villages of the north close more of their words on a consonant and say them quickly. The villages of the outer beaches in the south let more words end on a vowel and draw them out, and a fjord speaker hears them as slow. Speech at a feast is a register of its own. A chief's Speaker uses the long, built-up forms, says every name in full with its house, and never uses the short words of the beach, and a commoner who copies the feast speech in ordinary talk is laughed at for putting on airs.

The tongue is related to nothing in the south and to nothing spoken over the mountains. A trader of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Grass People]] who comes over the passes in summer bargains with signs and with the few dozen words each side has learned of the other's speech, and the coast does the same with the [[affiliation-shuqash|Shell-Bead People]] to the south.

Nobody writes it. The Cedar People keep their names, their histories and their debts in memory, and the carving on a crest pole shows a house's crests without spelling a word.

## How the Kelwanuk Language sounds

The Kelwanuk Language is a tongue of long words, dense with _k_, _kw_ and _ts_, with an _a_ in almost every word. A sentence sounds clipped and busy to a southern ear, and the ear hears the same endings again and again, because the tongue builds most of its words from short roots by adding to them: _kita_ is to give, _kitala_ a giving feast, and _kitalas_ a gift.

**The vowels.** _a_ leads by far, then _i_ and _u_, with _e_ and _o_ much rarer. _ai_ and _au_ run as single sounds.

**The consonants.** _k_, _kw_, _g_, _gw_, _t_, _ts_, _d_, _p_, _b_, _s_, _h_, _l_, _m_, _n_, _w_ and _y_. _k_, _h_, _l_, _m_, _n_ and _ts_ lead, and _kw_ and _gw_ are as common as plain _p_ or _b_.

**The catch.** A few words break between two vowels with a catch in the throat, written `'`: _ye'asla_, a knife. A word holds at most one catch, and most words hold none.

**Shapes and endings.** Most words have two syllables, many have three, and the built words run to four. A frequent word is short. Syllables open on one consonant or on _kw_, _gw_ or _ts_, and two consonants meet inside a word where a syllable closes on _l_, _m_, _n_, _s_ or _k_: _tsilkas_, an eagle, _humkwa_, a debt. About a third of all words end on a vowel, most often _a_; another third end on _s_, as in _-is_ and _-las_; the rest end on _k_, _l_, _m_, _n_ or _t_.

**What the Kelwanuk Language leaves out.** It has no _r_ at all, and no _f_, _v_, _z_ or _j_. It has none of the rasping _kh_, _gh_ or _q_ of the mountain tongues and none of the ejective pops of Ki'i'chek, no _sh_, _ch_, _th_ or _ng_, and no word opens on two consonants other than _kw_, _gw_ or _ts_.

## Phonology

### Consonant System

**Stops:** p, t, k, kw, b, d, g, gw

**Affricate:** ts

**Fricatives:** s, h

**Nasals:** m, n

**Liquid and glides:** l, w, y

**The catch:** a stop in the throat between two vowels, written `'`

### Vowel System

**Vowels:** a, e, i, o, u

**Vowel pairs:** ai, au, each one syllable

### Stress

A word is stressed on its first syllable, whatever its length: _tsilkas_ is TSIL-kas, _kenwatukwa_ is KEN-wa-tu-kwa, and _kelwanuk_ is KEL-wa-nuk. A built word keeps the stress of its first root. No word of the dictionary needs an accent.

## Grammar Notes

### Sentence Structure

The verb comes first. Then comes the one who acts, then whatever is acted on: _kita hik lukwa_, I give a copper. A word of time or place stands at the end, and _hela_, not, stands before the verb it denies: _hela kita hik_, I do not give.

### Verbs

A verb does not change. _Kita hik_, _kita tsuk_ and _kita yikai_ are I give, you give and they give. Whether a thing happened, happens or will happen is said with a word of time, _tita_ for what is past and _kemal_ for what is to come, when it matters, and left to the listener when it does not.

### Nouns and Possession

A noun does not change for more than one. A speaker who needs to say so puts a number or _sotsis_, many, before it: _sotsis temla_, many houses. The owner stands after what is owned: _temla hik_ is my house, and _yekwa hemkis_ the name of the chief. Words that describe come before the word they describe: _awik temla_, a big house.

### Pronouns

_Hik_ is I and my, _hikai_ we and our; _tsuk_ is you and your, whether one or many; _yik_ is he, she or it, and _yikai_ they. Where the meaning needs one, a pronoun takes no ending: _temla tsuk_ is your house.

### Questions

A question opens with one of the question words: _lanen_ who, _koga_ what, _andes_ where, _galtan_ when, _tamyal_ why, _kek_ how. A question that asks only for yes or no opens with _muluk_: _muluk gimugwal tsuk lemas_ is "will you drink water?" The roman spelling marks the question with `?`, and the speaker marks it with the opening word.

### Building Words

Six endings make most of the built words. _-las_ makes a noun of an act or a quality: _kita_, to give, gives _kitalas_, a gift, and _nilkwa_, to see, gives _nilkwalas_, a sight. _-mis_ makes a person of a thing or an act: _talwa_, to speak, gives _talwamis_, a Speaker, and _temla_, a house, gives _temlamis_, a commoner, one of the house. _-anuk_ makes a people or a house's people: _kelwa_, cedar, gives _kelwanuk_. _-ala_ makes a gathering or a season, _-is_ a shore or a direction, and _-gas_ an ordinal. Two words set side by side make a compound, the first saying which: _kwemla_, a crest, and _gelas_, a pole, give _kwemlagelas_, a crest pole.

## Everyday speech

_Kwas_ is the greeting at a door, and _ikwauk_ the word at parting. _Kais_ thanks. _Eya_ is yes and _hela_ no, and _hela_ is also the word that denies anything else: _hela yaldu_, no wealth, is poverty, and _helayekwa_, no name, is a slave.

_Kamwek halkwas_ means "come here", and it is what a host calls down the beach to a canoe of guests. _Kalwas_ welcomes them in. _Koga yekwa gisa_ asks "what is the name of this?", and it is the first question a child learns to ask, because on the coast everything that matters has a name and an owner. _Hela kamis hik_, I do not understand, is said to a stranger, and _kamis hik_, I understand, ends an argument.

_Temla hik_ is my house, and a speaker who says it means his lineage as much as the building: its names, its people and its fishing places. _Tigwala_ is mother and _yolkas_ father, _tsolos_ a child, _hunka_ a grandmother and _bakal_ a grandfather. Every speaker can name the house they belong to and the seat of the highest name in it.

Numbers count by tens: _kes_, _nisa_, _yulas_, _lakwis_, _tulim_, one to five; _kwilis_, _wakis_, _mulsu_ and _enut_ carry on to nine, _hukas_ is ten, _nisa hukas_ twenty, _lela_ a hundred and _hukas lela_ a thousand. A Keeper of the Count counts the blankets of a great feast in hundreds and thousands. A number takes _-gas_ to give its place: _nisagas_, second, _yulasgas_, third, while first has its own word, _kenwa_.

## Script and Literacy

The Kelwanuk Language has no script. The carving on a crest pole, a house post or a box shows a house's crests and the beings of its histories, and a person who knows the house can read the history the pole stands for. No mark on a pole spells a word, and two carvers can carve the same crest in two different ways.

What the coast needs to keep, it keeps in memory. A house's Keeper of the Count holds every gift it has given and received, and the guests paid to witness a feast hold what they saw. When two memories disagree, the chiefs of both houses settle it, and the settlement is itself remembered.

## Naming Traditions

A name on the coast is property. Each house owns its names, and each name carries a seat at the feasts, a set of crests, and rights to particular fishing places, songs and dances. A person holds a series of names through a life. A child is given a small name at its first year; a young person takes a second name at a feast that its parents give; an adult claims the names of the house that come to it, by inheritance from either parent or through marriage, and each claim is made good with a giving feast. When a person dies, the name waits for an heir to lift it.

A name is made from the words of the dictionary, most often a thing of the house's crests or its wealth and a thing it does: _Lukwahakwa_ is Copper Lifted, _Kalunukwas_ is Counts the Tides. [Making names](#making-names) gives the parts a new name is built from. A house keeps some names for women and some for men, and a name passes from a man to a woman or the other way where the house has no other heir. A house's people are named with _-anuk_ on its crest: the _Tsilkasanuk_ are the Eagle House.

## Kelwanuk Lexicon {#lexicon}

A plank house is _temla_, a name _yekwa_, and its seat at a feast _tukwa_. To give is _kita_, a giving feast _kitala_, and a debt, a gift not yet answered, _humkwa_. This page is the word-hoard of the Cedar People: its words, the endings that build new ones, and the register of every name the setting gives the Cedar People, their houses, their villages and their offices.

The Kelwanuk Language is unwritten, and the spellings on this page are the setting's one roman transcription of it. The tongue has no capital letters, so a word or a name stands here in lowercase; the prose of these pages capitalizes a name as it does any other.

Two consonants meet inside a word where a syllable closes on _l_, _m_, _n_, _s_ or _k_ before the next; _kw_, _gw_ and _ts_ each count as one sound, so _humkwa_ holds three letters together and two sounds.

FROM clusters WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kelwanuklng'

## Making names {#making-names}

**A person** carries a name of two words set together, owned by a house: _lukwahakwa_ is _lukwa_, a copper, and _hakwa_, to lift.

A house makes a new name when it has more heirs than names, and the chief who makes it gives a feast to show it to the coast, because a name nobody has witnessed is no name. The new name sets a thing of the house's crests, its catch or its wealth before what it does, both words whole and unchanged: _yaldu_ (wealth) and _kita_ (to give) make _Yaldukita_, Gives Wealth, and _tuklas_ (a mountain) and _kapala_ (to return) make _Tuklaskapala_, Returns from the Mountain. Now and then the act comes first, as in _Kalunukwas_, Counts the Tides. A name is not a woman's or a man's by its parts; a house keeps some of its names for women and some for men. The parts below are the ones the houses' names already use.

```sql
SELECT '`' || form || '`' AS "Element",
       CASE element_position WHEN 'opening' THEN 'first' WHEN 'closing' THEN 'last' ELSE 'first or last' END AS "Stands",
       sense AS "Sense", array_to_string(examples, ', ') AS "Example"
FROM elements WHERE language = 'thalorna-note-skill-kelwanuklng' ORDER BY position
```

**A house** is its crest with _-anuk_: _tsilkasanuk_, the Eagle House. **A village** is named for its beach with _-is_: _tsilkasis_ is _tsilkas_, an eagle, and _-is_, the shore of it, Eagle Beach, where the first Tsilkas came ashore. Every village of the coast is named this way, for the crest of the house that first came ashore there, so _gwemlasis_ is Thunderbird Beach and _lamekis_ Bear Beach, whatever houses live there now. **A country** is named for what it is, with the word that describes it first: _tuyala hapas_, the Long Inlets, and _tsawis yumis_, the seaward beaches, the Outer Beaches.

**The people** are _kelwanuk_, the people of the cedar, and the name these pages give them is the Cedar People. The south calls them _Ish'bal Ki'ahk_, after the eagles carved on the poles at Eagle Beach, where its traders first met them.
