Ch'alish P'ilam—"the glyphs of Ch'alish," after **[[lore-ixilanchalixdty|Ish'ilan Ch'alish]]**, whom tradition credits with composing the first codex—is the writing of the [[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik]] city-states. It has two hands: a **common hand** built from a small stock of simple signs, and a **ceremonial hand** that draws each sign as a picture. They share their readings and are not copies of one another. Nearly everyone knows a few signs, and a scribe knows almost all of them. A character needs the script and [[skill-kiichek|Ki'i'chek]] to read unfamiliar text; recognizing a familiar warning or tally is a narrower accomplishment.

## What the script records

The script records meaning, not speech. A text says what happened or what was meant, and nobody in it is quoted word for word. Grammar is never written out: a Ki'i'chek tense, person or ending is carried by context, and the endings _-ok_ and _-bal_ are sounds a glyph may write but a reader usually supplies. Each glyph is a concept. Two or three glyphs together make a further concept, and the first is usually the head, with those after it modifying it, as Ki'i'chek puts an adjective after its noun: `MAN HIGH` is an elder or a chief, `HOUSE OUTSIDE` a village.

A glyph can also write its sound, and names are written this way. The roman transcription is where every spoken sound is set down, so that anyone can say a word; the script has no spelling of its own. Several glyphs can write one sound, and one word can be written by several different glyph runs.

## The common hand

The common hand is built from a small fixed set of strokes and components. Every sign fills a square cell that is never drawn, so a row of signs sits on an invisible grid. Signs are plain, quick to make and easy to tell apart: they survive a chalk stroke on a rough wall or a hurried stylus line.

The common hand is not a reduction of the ceremonial pictures. It has its own construction, and a common sign can be learned without ever having seen the picture that shares its reading.

**Columns of two.** Common writing runs in columns two signs wide. A row of two is read left to right, then the next row down, then the next column. Labels, tallies and ledgers are all set this way, and a compound may break across rows.

**Surfaces.** Each trade has its own:

- A miner writes in chalk on a shaft wall.
- A carpenter writes in chalk on a slate, or on a wooden palette.
- A mercantyler writes with a stylus on bark paper.
- A scribe writes with a brush or pen on vellum, made from animal skin.

**Vellum and calligraphy.** On vellum the common hand becomes calligraphy. A scribe writes the same signs neat, even and fine, so that a page is read as much for its beauty as for its content.

## The core glyphs

The common hand has 24 **core** glyphs, which almost everyone knows.

| Group                        | Glyphs                             |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| The heavens                  | `SUN`, `MOON`, `SKY`, `STAR`       |
| The elements                 | `WATER`, `FIRE`, `EARTH`, `STONE`  |
| Living things                | `MAN`, `WOMAN`, `PLANT`, `BEAST`   |
| The quarters                 | `EAST`, `WEST`, `NORTH`, `SOUTH`   |
| Position                     | `HIGH`, `LOW`, `INSIDE`, `OUTSIDE` |
| The household and the person | `HOUSE`, `HAND`, `EYE`, `MOUTH`    |

`MAN` is the unmarked person, and `MAN MAN` is people.

**The four quarters.** `EAST` and `WEST` are time: the sun's path across a day, and a life from birth to death. `NORTH` is the zenith, the world above, where the gods and the honoured dead dwell and where the noon sun stands at its height. `SOUTH` is the nadir, the world below: _Ch'al Ts'umaq_ beneath every cenote, the night sun's hidden journey and the soul's trials. Read in the order `EAST`, `NORTH`, `WEST`, `SOUTH`, they are the whole round. The sun rises, stands at noon, sets and travels beneath the world, as a soul is born, lives at its height, dies, passes the trials below and is born again. Where context calls for it, `NORTH` and `SOUTH` are also the plain directions.

## The ceremonial hand

The ceremonial hand draws every sign as its own picture inside a box, and the box is always drawn. Heads, figures, animals, plants and objects carry the signs. The boxes are set in the same columns two wide as the common hand, and rounded contours, inset smaller signs and a large illustrated figure standing beside the inscription fill the page around them. It appears on **temple walls, monuments, pyramids and posts around the village common**, as well as stelae and lintels. The arrangement and execution are the work of a trained scribe and carver.

A picture and a common sign can have the same reading without resembling one another. A scribe can choose among recognized pictorial variants of a sign. The added detail belongs to the visible composition; it does not authorize a different reading. Learning to read the elaborate pictorial variants takes further instruction beyond the common hand.

Images that frame an inscription can illustrate the event without themselves being words. Inside the boxes, a run may deliberately say one thing by its pictures and another by its sounds; choosing glyphs whose pictures and sounds both carry meaning is a scribe's art in poetry above all.

## Names and sounds

Context settles which of a glyph's associated meanings is meant, and a run of glyphs can sometimes be grouped in more than one way. Every glyph also has a sound. Where a name or a foreign word is to be written, the scribe chooses glyphs by their sounds and spells it syllable by syllable. A closing consonant takes a syllable whose vowel is silent, usually an echo of the vowel before it: _Ch'akul_ is _cha-ku-lu_. A catch is left to the reader. Endings and other small sounds are written only where the scribe wants them fixed.

Predictable stress and the rise or fall of a ritual voice have no sign. The apostrophe in the roman transcription shows a sound; it does not prescribe the drawing of a native sign.

## Surfaces and records

Chalk on mine walls, slates and palettes carry short practical records, and bark paper carries a merchant's tallies. Plaster and pottery carry dedications, ownership marks and the names of those who commissioned them. Stelae, temple walls and lintels carry dynastic accounts, accession dates, conquests and genealogies in the ceremonial hand. Vellum carries the scribes' finest work in the common hand.

Paper is also made from the inner bark of a wild fig tree. Codices made from it, folded in screens and kept in the _ish'ilan ch'umbal_, the star-houses, hold astronomical tables, day-counts and funerary texts. Their inks are mixed as a rite, and a codex has standing as an object as well as carrying its text.

## The count of days

Calendrical writing is a major part of learned scribal work. The [Ts'en]{.name data-type="lore"} of 365 days, the 260-day [Tq'an]{.name data-type="lore"}, the lunar [Ts'uqal]{.name data-type="lore"} and the stellar [Ish'ilan]{.name data-type="lore"} run simultaneously, and a formal date is written as the intersection of several of them. The fifty-two-year [K'ayik Kul'al]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the Living Flame, falls where two counts come back into phase.

An account or measurement keeps its quantity and the thing counted together. A date is identified as a date; an ordinary tally does not require a reader to compute the sacred calendar. Expertise in the overlapping counts goes far beyond the numbers a miner or workshop worker needs.

## Who has it

Literacy is a sliding scale.

- **Almost everyone** knows the 24 core glyphs, with a few more specific to their trade: a miner's warnings and shaft marks, a carpenter's measures, a mercantyler's goods and sums.
- **Many people** know a good number of two-glyph compounds and a couple of three-glyph ones.
- **Scribes** know the most: four- and five-glyph compounds, the lesser-known glyphs and the sound of every glyph. Reading by sound is a scribal skill.
- **Priests** know fewer glyphs than scribes, but they know all the sacred glyphs, which even scribes may not.

The **P'ilambal**, scribe and painter, keeps genealogies, codices and the monuments that assert them. The **Ts'enbal**, priest of the sun, keeps the count of days. Both rank with the high nobility of a city-state such as [[affiliation-ixilankul|Ish'ilankul]], and their offices are hereditary in practice. Running text, calendrical tables and ceremonial inscriptions are the work of priests and scribes. Learned literacy and access to temple archives are sources of priestly and scribal power.

**The northern nations do not use it.** [[affiliation-ixbalkiahk|Ish'bal Ki'ahk]], [[affiliation-teyumi|Teyumi]], [[affiliation-penqalchakal|P'enqal Ch'akal]], [[affiliation-xikulchakl|Shik'ul Ch'akal]] and [[affiliation-tzumanotun|Ts'uma No'tun]] stand outside Ki'ich'chik society. They are largely oral, and they refuse Ki'ich'chik speech and writing alike. Ki'ich'chik scribes write about them as foreigners, and the script gives them no emblem glyphs.

## In play

A shaft warning, a merchant's tally and a dynastic inscription demand different depths of reading. A miner can explain the familiar marks of the trade while being unable to read a temple frieze. A reader who knows the glyphs of a public monument may still need a scribe to interpret its elaborate pictures, dates and political claims. A dynastic inscription records the account commissioned by its patron.

See also [[skill-ktzlcrdscrpt|K'otsel]], the knotted record of numbers, accounts and conventional messages. It remains a distinct practice; a cord bundle does not spell out arbitrary speech in these signs.
