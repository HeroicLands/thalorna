Ch'alish P'ilam—"the glyphs of Ch'alish," after **[[lore-ixilanchalixdty|Ish'ilan Ch'alish]]**, whom tradition credits with composing the first codex—is the writing of the [[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik]] city-states. It has a compact **common form** for practical work and an elaborate **ceremonial form** built from pictorial signs. Both use the same word readings and phonetic values. A character needs the script and [[skill-kiichek|Ki'i'chek]] to read unfamiliar text; recognizing a familiar warning or tally is a narrower accomplishment.

## The common form

The common form uses compact, consistent signs that can be written quickly and distinguished without fine decoration. Mathematicians set down calculations, businesspeople keep accounts, engineers record measurements, craftspeople mark work, and miners chalk warnings and shaft marks on stone. Clerks use clay and wax tablets. A sign must remain recognizable in a shallow incision or a chalk stroke on a rough wall.

The useful parts of a sign are its plain outline and distinguishing strokes. Pictorial details can be omitted in this hand; the strokes that distinguish readings cannot. The written order of words follows the spoken sentence, and unfamiliar words can be spelled phonetically. Names, quantities and measurements can therefore be recorded without inventing a picture for each one.

Practical literacy varies with the work. A miner may recognize and write a small stock of warnings, names, directions and numbers. A craftsperson may read the labels, quantities and familiar instructions of a workshop. A clerk needs a broader working stock for accounts. These abilities do not imply competence in continuous unfamiliar prose, astronomical tables or ceremonial inscriptions.

## The ceremonial form

The ceremonial form is composed as pictorial relief: heads, figures, animals, plants and objects carry the signs, joined into rounded blocks and larger scenes. Curled contours and inset smaller signs fill the blocks; a large illustrated figure can stand beside the inscription it accompanies. It appears on **temple walls, monuments, pyramids and posts around the village common**, as well as stelae and lintels. The arrangement and execution are part of the work of a trained scribe and carver.

A pictorial form and its compact working sign can have the same reading. A scribe can also choose among recognized pictorial variants of that sign. The added detail belongs to the visible composition; it does not add sounds to a word or authorize a different reading. Phonetic complements remain legible within the design. Recognizing the common form of a sign helps, but learning to read its elaborate pictorial variants takes further instruction.

Images that frame an inscription can illustrate the event without themselves being words. The written signs within the composition retain conventional readings: a reader cannot substitute any word that the pictured animal or object happens to suggest.

## Words, syllables and blocks

A **logograph** writes a particular word, rather than a freely interpreted idea. **Phonetic signs** write vowels and open consonant-vowel syllables. A word can use a logograph, a phonetic spelling, or both together. Small phonetic complements confirm a word's reading or supply sounds not expressed by the larger signs. A new spoken word can be spelled with these signs without acquiring a new logograph.

Signs belonging together are fitted into compact blocks. Monumental text normally uses paired columns: read the upper-left block, then the upper-right, then the next pair below. Finish that pair of columns before moving to the next. Within a block, follow the components from left to right and top to bottom. Short practical labels can run in a single line; accounts keep entries and quantities in consistent rows. A ledger does not require a monumental layout.

## Spelling words and catches

The common spelling preserves every pronounced vowel, consonant and catch in order. A **consonant complement** supplies a closing consonant or a consonant before a catch without adding an unspoken vowel. These reduced forms are distinct from full consonant-vowel signs. A **catch complement** writes the glottal stop wherever the other signs leave it unexpressed. Its position fixes where the catch occurs; a reader does not have to infer it from decoration or from an altered spelling of a neighboring vowel.

In the common transcription, _k'ayik_ has a catch after _k_ and before the vowel, _ish'bal_ has a catch between _ish_ and _bal_, and _ne'_ ends with a catch. Each is preserved in phonetic writing. The two catches in _Ki'i'chek_ and _Ki'ich'chik_ are likewise retained. The complement adds a catch, not a syllable.

A familiar logograph can identify its word without spelling every sound again. Where its reading would be ambiguous, complements supply the needed distinction. Names, unfamiliar words and instructions requiring an exact reading can be written fully phonetically. Ceremonial forms preserve the same distinctions even when their principal signs become elaborate pictures.

Predictable stress and the rise or fall of a ritual voice do not require different word signs. The apostrophe in the common transcription shows a sound; it does not prescribe the drawing of a native sign.

## Surfaces and records

Chalk on mine walls and marks on workpieces carry short practical records. Clay and wax tablets hold accounts, calculations and instructions. Plaster and pottery carry dedications, ownership marks and the names of those who commissioned them. Stelae, temple walls and lintels carry dynastic accounts, accession dates, conquests and genealogies in the elaborate form.

Paper is made from the inner bark of a wild fig tree. Codices made from it, folded in screens and kept in the _ish'ilan ch'umbal_, the star-houses, hold astronomical tables, day-counts and funerary texts. Their inks are mixed as a rite, and a codex has standing as an object as well as carrying its text.

## The count of days

Calendrical writing is a major part of learned scribal work. The [Ts'en]{.name data-type="lore"} of 365 days, the 260-day [Tq'an]{.name data-type="lore"}, the lunar [Ts'uqal]{.name data-type="lore"} and the stellar [Ish'ilan]{.name data-type="lore"} run simultaneously, and a formal date is written as the intersection of several of them. The fifty-two-year [K'ayik Kul'al]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the Living Flame, falls where two counts come back into phase.

An account or measurement keeps its quantity and the thing counted together. A date is identified as a date; an ordinary tally does not require a reader to compute the sacred calendar. Expertise in the overlapping counts goes far beyond the numbers a miner or workshop worker needs.

## Who has it

The **P'ilambal**, scribe and painter, keeps genealogies, codices and the monuments that assert them. The **Ts'enbal**, priest of the sun, keeps the count of days. Both rank with the high nobility of a city-state such as [[affiliation-ixilankul|Ish'ilankul]], and their offices are hereditary in practice. Priests and scribes have the broad training to read and compose complex texts and recognize elaborate ceremonial variants.

Working people have more limited, practical abilities in the common form. The size of a person's useful sign stock depends on their occupation and instruction. Some recognize only familiar marks; others can keep an account or follow a written calculation. Learned literacy and access to temple archives remain sources of priestly and scribal power.

## In play

A shaft warning, a merchant's tablet and a dynastic inscription demand different depths of reading. A local worker may explain the familiar marks of a trade while being unable to read a temple frieze. A reader who knows the words of a public monument may still need a specialist to interpret its elaborate signs, dates and political claims. A dynastic inscription records the account commissioned by its patron.

See also [[skill-ktzlcrdscrpt|K'otsel]], the knotted record of numbers, accounts and conventional messages. It remains a distinct practice; a cord bundle does not spell out arbitrary speech in these phonetic signs.
