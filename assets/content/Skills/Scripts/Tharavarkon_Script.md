The Tharavarkon is the old carved script of the [[lore-vylarianclt|Vylarian]] people—angular, incised, and older than the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Empire]] that inherited it. The name is Proto-Pelwar: _thura-vark_, "graven work," the same compound that the [[lore-nordheimnclt|Nordmen]] still carry as [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thuravark]]. That two peoples separated by three thousand miles and as many years call their runes by cognate names is the strongest single piece of evidence that [[skill-vylarilng|Vylari Language]] and [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal Language]] descend from one nomadic tongue, and Vylarian antiquarians who would rather not be cousins to the north have spent a great deal of ink trying to explain it away.

Vylaria did not abandon it. It kept the runes for everything meant to outlast the person who ordered it cut, and coined the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Semaron]] for everything else.

Behind the Pelwar word stands an older and far more awkward question. The staves match [[skill-drthrkscrpt|Pirzath Script]] of the [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazár Folk]] too well for coincidence, and the likeliest account has the **Proto-Pelwar themselves as Khazari subjects**, taught a reduced work-row in the mountains before the migrations—so that Tharavarkon is not a borrowing Vylaria made but one it inherited, already twice simplified, from ancestors who cut tallies for somebody else.

Vylarian antiquarians have found parts of this congenial for reasons that have little to do with scholarship: it makes the imperial ceremonial script older than the empire, and it demotes the Nordmen claim to have received the runes from a god. The rest of it they handle very carefully indeed. An empire whose sacred letters descend from the tally-marks of a bound people, in the service of a race it has never conquered and cannot reach, is not a finding the [[affiliation-seldan|Seldan]] wishes published, and the two or three scholars who have put it plainly have found their careers proceeding no further.

## The letters

Straight strokes, no curves, cut cross-grain because that is what stone and hardwood will take. Tharavarkon is written in the letters of [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thuravark]]: the two are sister branches of the Proto-Pelwar row and share its staves. The imperial recension carries three characters beyond the twenty-eight staves of Thuravark, and its order is fixed. The recension formalized what had been a loose family of local rune-rows with strict proportions, and added two things the old carvers had no use for: ligature forms that let a mason fit a long dedication into a fixed architrave, and the **crowned runes**—special enclosing marks that must be cut around the name of a god, a founder, or a reigning emperor. Cutting a divine name without its crown is not an error of spelling. It is an impiety, and in [[affiliation-hlykngdmnrtm|Nartun]] a prosecutable one.

## Where it is found

Temple architraves and altar frontals; the lintels of tombs; boundary and league stones; triumphal arches and the bases of imperial statuary; oath-stones; the dedication panels of bridges, aqueducts and legionary fortresses; and the inscribed bronze tablets on which treaties are cut in duplicate so that neither party can quietly amend its copy. Almost none of it is on parchment. Cutting is slow, so a Tharavarkon inscription is short, formulaic, and chosen with great care—which makes the occasional inscription that is _not_ formulaic worth a great deal to whoever can read it.

## Who has it

The priesthoods first—the Holy Kingdom of Nartun conducts all its rites in the archaic ritual dialect and cuts all its dedications in Tharavarkon, and a Nartunite priest who cannot read runes cannot serve. After them: master masons and monument-cutters, who often cut the letters competently without following the sense; imperial antiquarians and the archivists of [[affiliation-imprlscrblgld|the Imperial Scribal Guild]]; and the scholars of the [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Thordimalon]], whose interest in pre-imperial inscriptions is not entirely academic. Ordinary literate Vylarians can pick out a god's name and a date on a temple front and no more.

## In play

Two difficulties compound. The script is durable, so the inscriptions a character actually finds are routinely centuries older than anyone living—and the Vylari cut into them is correspondingly archaic, so a reader's modern Language skill may simply not cover it. And the formality is a trap: a great deal of Tharavarkon is boilerplate, so a reader who guesses at a worn inscription from the expected formula will produce a fluent, confident, entirely wrong reading. Weathering is what the Script rules call a degraded or damaged document; the wrong century of Vylari is a matter for the Language test.

Tharavarkon is the [[sohl-none-docskill-runic|Runic]] tradition in its Vylarian recension. Each variety keeps its own skill, but a character who reads Thuravark knows the shapes and their order, which is often enough to know what kind of stone they are standing in front of.
