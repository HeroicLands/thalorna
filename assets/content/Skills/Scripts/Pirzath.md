Pirzath—"the cut letters"—is the writing of the [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazari]]: forty-one characters with **two forms**. **Carved Pirzath** is angular: short strokes cut across the grain of the stone, never along it, each letter standing separate and never joining its neighbour. The line of text follows a seam, a bedding line or a natural edge of the stone rather than a ruled baseline, so no inscription is a straight line, and the same letter is cut differently in slate and in granite. Carved Pirzath is for what must last—oaths, laws, tombs, the founding of a hold—in stone, metal or wood. Diacritics are notches, drilled dots and short scored lines, because those are what a graver makes cleanly on metal and what survives on rock. Monumental and sacred work uses an elaborated variant with deeper relief and inlaid metal, but the underlying characters are identical—the Khazari do not have a separate ceremonial alphabet, and would consider one an admission that the ordinary letters were inadequate.

Twenty-four of the forty-one write the sounds of Khazari as it is spoken. The other seventeen write sounds the language no longer makes: _sh_, _zh_, _ch_, _j_, _ts_, _q_, _ng_, the catch, _w_, _y_, _ny_, _hl_ and the joined vowels _ai_, _au_, _ei_, _oi_ and _ou_. The row keeps them unused, and they are the letters the Pelwar row kept in use. Carved Pirzath divides its words with a drilled dot and ends a sentence with the one cut a carver makes along the grain. It has no question mark, because a Khazari question carries its own mark in the word _zef_ or in the question word that opens it.

Numbers are written in place order with **sixteen numeral signs**, nought through fifteen, built from tally-strokes, so a number of sixteens is read in the same way in both forms.

**The hand form of Pirzath** is the everyday writing: flowing, curved and joined, in ink or scratched with a stylus. It is written on slate and chalk for teaching, tallies and notes; on wax tablets for drafts and accounts; in ink on hide or parchment for letters and books; and with a stylus on thin sheets of lead or copper for records meant to last without being monumental. It reads left to right and top to bottom, as the carved form does, and marks the skeleton of a word with a heavier stroke. Reading carved Pirzath well means reading the stone.

It resembles the [[sohl-none-docskill-runic|Runic]] traditions of the [[lore-pelwarpepl|Pelwar peoples]] because it is their **parent**. Pirzath is the elder script by some thousands of years, and the staves of [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thuravark Runic Script]] in [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal Language]] correspond to a subset of the Pirzath characters closely enough—same angles, same notch-and-drill diacritics, broadly the same sound values wherever the two languages allow the comparison—that the relationship is not seriously disputed by anyone outside the [[place-nrdlndsrgn|Nordlands]].

How the transmission happened is not documented anywhere. The likeliest account, and the one the Khazari offer without much interest in arguing it, is that the row was taught to **the Proto-Pelwar tribes, who were their subjects**—the mountain peoples who mined for them, cut their timber, drove their herds and swore to them, before those tribes ever went west and became the [[lore-nordheimnclt|Nordheimn]], [[lore-vylarianclt|Vylarian]] people and [[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan People]]. A reduced row was cut down to the sounds a Pelwar mouth actually made and stripped of the characters it had no use for.

That is not one remove from Pirzath but two, and the modern rows are further still. What the subject tribes carried out of the mountains was a single **Proto-Pelwar rune-row**, and the migrations broke it up: [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thuravark]] in the north, imperial [[skill-thrvrknscrpt|Tharavarkon]], highland [[skill-thrvrdhscrpt|Thurarth]], each a further simplification of a simplification, and at a fourth remove the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Semaron]] that most of the west now writes in. None of the three modern rows would be legible to a Khazari without study, and Khazari who have studied them describe the experience as reading a child's copy of a copy.

The evidence is entirely comparative. No dated intermediate inscription survives; the earliest Pelwar cuttings anyone has found are already fully human and already simplified; and the hold archives that would settle the question are in strongholds no human scholar has been invited into.

## The interference question

Teaching writing to a subject people sits awkwardly beside the Elder Races' long practice of leaving humanity to arrive at its own answers. The Khazari position is that nothing was uplifted: what the tribes were given was a **work-script**—tally, mark, measure, warrant and oath, the minimum a bound laborer and an oath-holder need—and that a means of keeping a count is a tool like a shovel, not a gift of civilization. What the Pelwar peoples subsequently did with it, up to and including an empire's chanceries, was their own doing and nobody consulted the Khazari about it. Not every Elder authority accepts the distinction, and the script is in any case only the most documentable strand of a much larger transmission—see [[lore-longnhrtnc|the Long Inheritance]].

## A literate people

Literacy is **near-universal among the Khazari**, which is true of no human culture on [[place-worldthlrn|Thalorna]]. Writing is not a scholar's specialization there but a component of craft: a piece of work is signed, dated, specified and warranted in carved Pirzath on the object itself, and a hold's genealogies, craft records, tolerances and assay results are kept as a matter of course in the hand form, on slate, wax, hide and soft metal. A Khazari smith who could not write would be unable to practice. The consequence is an archive of extraordinary depth, in stone, metal and sheet lead, in strongholds where no human scholar has been invited.

## Who has it, among humans

Almost nobody. Dedicated scholars, and those with Khazari kinship or long fosterage. There is no equivalent of the [[lore-flksinale|Sinale Folk]]'s occasional gift of a text to a bardic college—the Khazari do not distribute their records, and their memorial objects, the preserved tools and weapons of ancestors, are not to be handled by outsiders at all, let alone read and copied.

## In play

The script is genuinely easy to _recognize_ and hard to _read_: a party finds carved Pirzath on a bridge keystone, a tunnel lintel, a blade tang, a marker in a mine, and can tell at once what it is, and can do nothing with it. A lead sheet or a wax tablet found in a hold is the hand form, legible only to someone who learned it. What is written there is usually technical—a load rating, a date, an assay, a maker's warrant—and technical Khazari is a register with vocabulary that a conversational speaker simply does not have. Age is less of an obstacle here than elsewhere: Khazari orthography has barely moved in three thousand years, so a very old inscription is likely to be as legible as a new one.

The sentence end is always the last mark of a sentence, outside a closing quotation mark or any other mark, as in _Galdan bunát, "Dalík bant, maríg bant"._ Written in the script, no space stands on either side of the sentence end: the next sentence begins straight after it. A command is marked at its start: the imperative mark stands before the first word of the sentence it governs, or just inside the opening quotation mark when it governs only the quotation.

A handout, a shield device or a banner shows a whole word or a name in the script, never a single letter standing alone. Several letters, seen on their own, share their shape with runes that are used as hate symbols; inside a word they read as letters.
