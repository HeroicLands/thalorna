Kheperi-sesh is the sacred writing of [[affiliation-empirtkhpr|Ta'Kheperu]]: a mixed system of **logograms** that stand for whole words, **phonetic signs** that spell consonant clusters, and **determinatives** that carry no sound at all and exist to tell the reader which of several possible words is meant. Roughly seven hundred signs are recognized; two or three hundred are in common use; the rest are the reason a temple keeps old scribes.

A character needs **both** Kheperi-sesh and [[skill-kheperlng|Kheperi]] to read it – and specifically **Classical Kheperi**, frozen in written form some twenty-three centuries ago and no longer the language anyone speaks.

## What it is for

Temple walls, royal monuments, tomb lintels, stelae, and the mortuary rolls buried with the dead. It is not an administrative script and has not been one for eight hundred years; that work went to [[skill-demtkscrpt|Demotika]]. What Kheperi-sesh does is make an utterance permanent and efficacious, and its practitioners do not regard those two as separate claims. A tomb threshold carries a **curse cut into the stone**, with the warding worked into the shapes of the glyphs themselves; a name struck from every surface in a nome is a person unmade; a secret name written by a priest is a hold on the thing named.

## The apparatus

The script is maintained by the priesthood, the royal scribes and the esoteric orders – the scribal schools of [[place-khemenu|Khemenu]] in the [[affiliation-nomeprthth|Nome of Per-Thōth]] above all, where the king-lists, the calendar tables and the observatory records are kept under the patronage of [[affiliation-thoth|Thōth]] himself. The priesthood has resisted every orthographic reform proposed in living memory, and the resistance is not aesthetic. Simplify the sacred script and the class that reads it stops being necessary.

## Who has it

Priests, royal scribes, initiates of the esoteric orders; a small fraction of the nobility, fewer than can read demotic. Foreign scholars occasionally, at the Panepistemium's expense and with the priesthood's grudging permission. Effectively nobody else – the gulf between those who read it and those who cannot is itself a marker of magical and social authority, and is maintained deliberately.

## In play

The hard part is rarely recognizing the signs; it is that the glyph is doing three jobs at once and the determinative decides which. A reader with the script but weak Classical Kheperi will mistake a logogram for a phonetic spelling and produce nonsense; a reader with the language but shaky sign-knowledge will miss the determinative and read the right sounds as the wrong word. Both failures are common, and both feel like success at the time. Where the inscription is warded, a bad reading is not only wrong – it is a bad reading performed aloud in a tomb.
