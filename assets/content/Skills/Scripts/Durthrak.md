Durthrak—"stone-cut"—is the writing of the [[skill-khazarlng|Khazári]]: forty-one characters of straight lines, right angles and sharp vertices, which **never join**, each standing separate and square in its own space. It reads left to right and top to bottom. Diacritics are notches, drilled dots and short scored lines, because those are what a graver makes cleanly on metal and what survives on rock. Monumental and sacred work uses an elaborated variant with deeper relief and inlaid metal, but the underlying characters are identical—the Khazári do not have a separate ceremonial alphabet, and would consider one an admission that the ordinary letters were inadequate.

It resembles the [[sohl-none-docskill-runic|Runic]] traditions of the Pelwar peoples because it is their **parent**. Durthrak is the elder script by some thousands of years, and the staves of Nordmal [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thurávark]] correspond to a subset of the Durthrak characters closely enough—same angles, same notch-and-drill diacritics, broadly the same sound values wherever the two languages allow the comparison—that the relationship is not seriously disputed by anyone outside the Nordlands.

How the transmission happened is not documented anywhere. The likeliest account, and the one the Khazári offer without much interest in arguing it, is that the row was taught to **the Proto-Pelwar tribes, who were their subjects**—the mountain peoples who mined for them, cut their timber, drove their herds and swore to them, before those tribes ever went west and became Nordmen and Vylarians and Áelendans. A reduced row was cut down to the sounds a Pelwar mouth actually made and stripped of the characters it had no use for.

That is not one remove from Durthrak but two, and the modern rows are further still. What the subject tribes carried out of the mountains was a single **Proto-Pelwar rune-row**, and the migrations broke it up: [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thurávark]] in the north, imperial [[skill-thrvrknscrpt|Thâravárkon]], highland [[skill-thrvrdhscrpt|Thúravardh]], each a further simplification of a simplification, and at a fourth remove the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Sêmarion]] that most of the west now writes in. None of the three modern rows would be legible to a Khazári without study, and Khazári who have studied them describe the experience as reading a child's copy of a copy.

The evidence is entirely comparative. No dated intermediate inscription survives; the earliest Pelwar cuttings anyone has found are already fully human and already simplified; and the hold archives that would settle the question are in strongholds no human scholar has been invited into.

## The interference question

Teaching writing to a subject people sits awkwardly beside the Elder Races' long practice of leaving humanity to arrive at its own answers. The Khazári position is that nothing was uplifted: what the tribes were given was a **work-script**—tally, mark, measure, warrant and oath, the minimum a bound laborer and an oath-holder need—and that a means of keeping a count is a tool like a shovel, not a gift of civilization. What the Pelwar peoples subsequently did with it, up to and including an empire's chanceries, was their own doing and nobody consulted the Khazári about it. Not every Elder authority accepts the distinction, and the script is in any case only the most documentable strand of a much larger transmission—see [[lore-longnhrtnc|The Long Inheritance]].

## A literate people

Literacy is **near-universal among the Khazári**, which is true of no human culture on Thalorna. Writing is not a scholar's specialization there but a component of craft: a piece of work is signed, dated, specified and warranted in Durthrak on the object itself, and a hold's genealogies, craft records, tolerances and assay results are kept as a matter of course. A Khazári smith who could not write would be unable to practice. The consequence is an archive of extraordinary depth, in stone and metal, in strongholds where no human scholar has been invited.

## Who has it, among humans

Almost nobody. Dedicated scholars, and those with Khazári kinship or long fosterage. There is no equivalent of the Sinalë's occasional gift of a text to a bardic college—the Khazári do not distribute their records, and their memorial objects, the preserved tools and weapons of ancestors, are not to be handled by outsiders at all, let alone read and copied.

## In play

The script is genuinely easy to _recognize_ and hard to _read_: a party finds Durthrak on a bridge keystone, a tunnel lintel, a blade tang, a bracket in a mine, and can tell at once what it is, and can do nothing with it. What is written there is usually technical—a load rating, a date, an assay, a maker's warrant—and technical Khazári is a register with vocabulary that a conversational speaker simply does not have. Age is less of an obstacle here than elsewhere: Khazári orthography has barely moved in three thousand years, so a very old inscription is likely to be legible to anyone who can read a new one.
