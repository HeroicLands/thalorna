The Calathindë—the starlight-hand—is the writing of the [[skill-sinalelng|Sinalë]]: organic, curved, continuously connected letterforms that run left to right and top to bottom and give a page the look of vine-growth or moving water. Ligatures are constant rather than occasional; diacritics carry vowel length, stress variation and poetic emphasis, and in a language whose grammar encodes mood and philosophical stance through vowel gradation, those marks are not ornament—they are most of the meaning.

It carries Sinalë alone. A character needs **both** the script and the language, and the second is by far the harder acquisition.

## Made, not written

Sinalë do not draw a distinction between literacy and craft. A written passage is worked the way a woodcarving or a weaving is worked, and the same critical vocabulary applies to all three; a hurried hand is not merely ugly but incorrect, in the way an off-note is incorrect. Sacred texts—insofar as a people with no temples, no liturgy and no scripture have sacred texts—are written in a further-elaborated variant that a competent everyday reader cannot necessarily follow. What the Sinalë keep in writing is genealogy, song, treaty, and the long memory of places; what they keep in singing is everything else.

## Who has it

Literacy is near-universal among the Sinalë themselves, who are fewer than one in ten thousand of Thalorna's people and mostly unseen. Among humans it is **extremely rare**: a handful of scholars, the odd mage who has spent decades on it, and the occasional bard of the [[affiliation-bardicolgs|Bardic Colleges]] who has been given a text and taught to read it—though the Colleges are memory-institutions by preference and treat a written song as a poor copy of the real one. The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Ordo Arcanis]] has tried repeatedly to acquire the script systematically and has not succeeded.

## In play

Everything about this script is slow. Reading it is slow because the ligatures obscure where one letter ends; writing it is slow because a hand that betrays haste will be read as either insolent or false. Old inscriptions in ruined Sinalë places—Laurelossë, Lhénavren—are frequently in a hand two or three thousand years out of date, and the party's scholar will need Áelendi or Sinalë of considerable depth even to establish what kind of document is in front of them. And a passable forgery is essentially impossible: any Sinalë who sees it will know at a glance, the way one knows a badly made chair.
