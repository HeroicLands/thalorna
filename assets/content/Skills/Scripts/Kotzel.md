The K'otsel Cord Script—called K'otsel, "the knotted thing," from _k'ots-_, to knot—is writing that is tied rather than drawn: a hanging cord from which subsidiary cords depend, the record carried in the **color of the thread, the kind of knot, and the position of the knot along the cord**. It encodes numbers natively and everything else by convention—tribute owed and delivered, storehouse tallies, census, the day-counts, and the standing formulae that let a runner carry a message he does not himself understand.

It is a genuine script and is learned as one, but it is not read aloud in [[skill-kicheklng|Ki'ichek]] the way glyphs are; a bundle is _worked through_, and the reader needs the conventions of the office that tied it as much as the language.

## How a cord records

**The main cord and its pendants.** A record hangs from one **main cord**, a thick cord carried looped over a staff or the wrist. Pendants hang from it, one for each item the record counts: a kind of tribute, a storehouse, a household group. A pendant that must be broken down carries subsidiary cords, one for each source. A short **sum cord**, tied across a group of pendants, holds their total, so a reader proves a count by adding it.

**Knots and what they count.** Numbers are base twenty and positional, as in the spoken count. Each pendant has fixed stations, one for each place: ones nearest the foot, then twenties (_ts'ob_), four hundreds (_pel'ush_) and eight thousands (_q'umpel_) above them. A station holds a cluster of knots, and a place is never more than nineteen.

| Knot                                   | Counts                                 |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Plain knot                             | One of that place                      |
| Wrapped knot (extra turns of the cord) | Five of that place                     |
| Shell knot (a hollow loop)             | Nothing in that place: _ts'amal_, zero |

A place of thirteen is two plain knots and two wrapped ones. The shell knot is tied in an empty station, so a missing place is written and never merely left bare.

**Colour, spacing and position.** Colour says what is counted: the dye of a pendant names the goods, and a pendant plied from two colours names the goods and where they came from. The key belongs to the office that ties the cord, so the same colour means different goods in different cities. Spacing groups the record: a wide gap on the main cord separates the pendants of one source from the next, and a station keeps its place by its distance from the foot, so an empty place cannot be mistaken for a wider gap. A pendant's position on the main cord orders the record, the most senior or most valuable item first, from the free end.

**A day-count and a tribute record.** A tribute record is read as numbers, goods and sums. A day-count is read by position alone: its frame is fixed, one pendant for each twenty-day month with a short pendant of five for the closing days of the 365-day year, or thirteen pendants of twenty for the 260-day count. Each station on a pendant is a day, and a knot on it marks a day with something to remember: a feast, an eclipse, a debt falling due. The knot's colour names the kind of entry, and the priests move a marker cord along the frame to show the day that has come.

**Carrying and reading.** A runner takes the cord wound on a staff, with its free end sealed by a knot in the colours of the office that tied it, and hands it on at each relay post without unwinding it. Standing formulae are short pendants of set knots, such as arrived, owed, delayed or danger, which a runner learns without learning to read numbers. A priest reads it unwound. He checks the seal and the dye and ply first, takes the colour key of the sending office, reads from the free end, proves each group against its sum cord, and then copies the record into fresh cord for the archive. A bundle from a distant office waits until someone who holds that office's key can be found.

## How it moves

Along the _ish'lan ch'upul_—the white roads, raised causeways of packed limestone—in the hands of the **Ch'umbal** relay runners, and into the priestly scribal houses at the great centers, where bundles are decoded, copied into fresh cord, and archived. A cord bundle is compact, weatherproof, unreadable to an illiterate interceptor, and cannot be skimmed: an enemy who takes one has taken a knot of string.

## Who has it

The priest-scribes, and the stewards of tribute. It is a closer-held skill than the glyphs, because it is where the actual numbers are—what a city-state truly owes, truly holds, and truly harvested. The public account is cut on a stela in [[skill-chlxplmscrpt|Ch'alish P'ilam]]; the real one hangs in a store-room in colored thread, and the two are not always the same document.

## In play

A party that seizes a tribute bundle has seized something no outsider can read and few insiders will read for them. Conventions drift by city-state and by generation, so an old bundle may be partially undecipherable even to the priesthood that inherited it—the color key is oral and dies with the office-holder who kept it. Forging one is far harder than forging a glyph text, since a competent reader checks the ply and dye of the thread as readily as the knots.
