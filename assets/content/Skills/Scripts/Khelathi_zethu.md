Khelathi-zethu is the sacred writing of the [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Au'Khelathu]]: a hieroglyphic script of about a hundred and fifty signs, each drawn as a small picture. Every sign stands for a **concept**, not a letter. Some signs, though not all, are also used for a **sound**: the consonants of the word the sign names, one, two or three of them, which is how words and names are spelled. A sign set beside a word can show which word or which kind of thing is meant, and the neighbouring signs, the wall and the setting decide the reading. Each sign is drawn in two or three variants, about four hundred drawn forms in all, and a variant is the same sign, not another word. What a sign means on a given wall depends on where it stands.

A character needs **both** Khelathi-zethu and [[skill-khelathlng|Khelathi Language]] to read it—and specifically **Classical Khelathi**, frozen in written form some twenty-three centuries ago and no longer the language anyone speaks.

## What it is for

Temple walls, royal monuments, tomb lintels, stelae, and the mortuary rolls buried with the dead. It is not an administrative script and has not been one for eight hundred years; that work went to [[skill-qalzscrscrpt|Qalezu Script]]. What Khelathi-zethu does is make an utterance permanent and efficacious, and its practitioners do not regard those two as separate claims. A tomb threshold carries a **curse cut into the stone**, with the warding worked into the shapes of the glyphs themselves; a name struck from every surface in a selat is a person unmade; a secret name written by a priest is a hold on the thing named.

## The signs and their order

The scribal schools teach the signs in sixteen groups, in this order:

1. Men
2. Women
3. Gods and their attributes
4. The body
5. Beasts
6. Birds
7. River creatures: fish, crocodiles and serpents
8. Plants and grain
9. Sky, sun and stars
10. Land, water and the river
11. Buildings
12. Boats
13. Tools, weapons and measures
14. Vessels and offerings
15. Cloth, crowns and adornment
16. Strokes and figures

**Variants.** The two or three variants of a sign differ in drawing, in the care taken and in the wall they are cut for. A scribe learns them as one sign.

**The gods' signs.** Certain signs belong to particular gods. Beside a god's name, or in a god's temple, such a sign carries that god's sense, and the same sign elsewhere carries its ordinary one. The reading follows the setting: the sign, the neighbouring signs, the wall and the god whose house it is.

## The apparatus

The script is maintained by the priesthood, the royal scribes and the esoteric orders—the scribal schools of [[place-khelunu|Khelunu]] in [[affiliation-selatgrrthsr|the Selat of Gar-Reth'Sa'ar]] above all, where the king-lists, the calendar tables and the observatory records are kept under what its custodians regard as the patronage of [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'ar]] himself. The priesthood has resisted every orthographic reform proposed in living memory, and the resistance is not aesthetic. Simplify the sacred script and the class that reads it stops being necessary.

## Who has it

Priests, royal scribes, initiates of the esoteric orders; a small fraction of the nobility, fewer than can read Qalezu. Foreign scholars occasionally, at the [[affiliation-panepistmm|Epistémion]]'s expense and with the priesthood's grudging permission. Effectively nobody else—the gulf between those who read it and those who cannot is itself a marker of magical and social authority, and is maintained deliberately.

## In play

The hard part is rarely recognizing the signs; it is judging which job a sign is doing where it stands: naming a concept or spelling a sound, and, beside a god's name or in a god's temple, carrying the god's sense. Context decides, and the same sign reads differently on a different wall. A reader with the script but weak Classical Khelathi will take a concept sign for a spelling and produce nonsense; a reader with the language but shaky sign-knowledge will miss the god's setting or the neighbouring signs and read the right sounds as the wrong word. Both failures are common, and both feel like success at the time. Where the inscription is warded, a bad reading is not only wrong—it is a bad reading performed aloud in a tomb.
