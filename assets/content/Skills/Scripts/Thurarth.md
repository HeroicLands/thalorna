Thúrarth is the third branch of the graven work. Proto-Pelwar _thura-vark_ gives Nordmal [[skill-thuravarkscript|Thurávark]] and imperial [[skill-thrvrknscrpt|Thâravarkon]]; in the mouths of the [[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan]] highlanders it came out **Thúrarth**, and the letterforms softened with the name. The name is made of the tongue's own pieces. Áelendi has no `v`, so _vark_ loses its lip sound and is left as _ark_; the highlanders lenite where their cousins harden, so the final stop softens to `th`; and the vowel at the join falls away, so _thura_ and _arth_ close up as _thúr-arth_, stressed on the first syllable as every Áelendi word is. The ending is one the tongue gives to words of its own, as in _khêlmarth_, accursed. All three descend, at a distance no one can measure, from Khazári [[skill-drthrkscrpt|Pirzath]]—by way of a single Proto-Pelwar row taught to the mountain tribes when those tribes were Khazári subjects, if the usual account is right. The Áelendan find this unremarkable and say so: the Kindred Pact tradition holds that everything worth having was received from something older, and a people embarrassed by that has misunderstood its own position. The Nordmen refuse it outright. The staves are still cut rather than written, but curves are permitted, the terminals are hooked, and the whole row looks like something between a rune and a vine.

It carries [[skill-aelendlng|Áelendi]] and older [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]]. A character needs **both** the script and the language.

## A script in retreat

Élavendre writes in the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Sêmarôn]] now, and has for centuries—the imperial alphabet arrived with trade and stayed because it is faster, and a modern Élavendri book of hours or land grant is in Vylarian letters with Élavendri diacritics added for pitch. Thúrarth survives in three places: **magical texts**, where the older form is held to matter and where nobody willing to risk a substitution has yet proposed one; **poetry**, where the runes' associations are part of the effect; and **objects of great antiquity**, which is to say anything nobody has recopied.

That last is not a small category. The Áelendan highlands are full of cut stone, and the Áelendan habit of _not_ writing important things down—the Kindred Pact is deliberately unwritten, and the Ésalosa Lodge forbids writing in certain rooms altogether—means the inscriptions that do exist were cut by people who thought the matter exceptional.

## Marks the others do not have

Thúrarth carries a set of enclosing and binding marks that Thurávark lacks entirely and Thâravarkon has only in its crowned-rune form: **wards**, cut around a name or a threshold, which the Áelendan tradition treats as operative rather than decorative. It also has a convention for writing a **shadow name**—the true name, as against the sun-name a person is called by—in a form that can be read only by someone who already knows it, which is either a cipher or a piece of practical magic depending on who is explaining it.

## Who has it

Those training in the magical arts, for whom it is a working necessity rather than an accomplishment; a portion of the Élavendri priesthood; antiquarians; and the scholars of the [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Thôrdimâlon]], whose interest in Áelendan inscriptions is the same interest they take in pre-imperial ones. Ordinary literate Élavendri cannot read it. The [[affiliation-bardicolgs|Bardic Colleges]] mostly decline to, on principle.

## In play

The classic Thúrarth problem is that the character reading the inscription is a mage, the inscription is a ward, and reading it aloud is an act rather than an observation. Beyond that: the language in old cuttings is archaic Áelendi, not modern Élavendri, so a reader's Language skill is doing more work than they expect, and the two are close enough that a wrong reading will still make a kind of sense.
