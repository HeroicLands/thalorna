Vaarthari—"holding what came before"—is the Kaliharans' discipline of deep memorization, the learned art by which a people with no everyday writing keeps everything it knows. The Kaliharans prize it, and no other people on [[place-worldthlrn|Thalorna]] practices anything like it. A trained Kaliharan does not recall a recitation the way another person remembers a song. They settle into it, a state close to trance, and the whole of the account comes back in order and in full, down to the smallest detail: the name of a terrace's second tender, the week a stone first warmed, the exact words of a procedure.

## How it is learned

Vaarthari is taught, never picked up. A child begins with short accounts said aloud with an elder, a few lines at a time, until the lines can be said without a thought. The accounts grow with the child. The training teaches the settling itself: how to slow the breath, quiet the mind and let the account arise whole instead of being searched for. A learner is tested by reciting before people who already know the account, and one slip is heard at once. Years of this make a competent holder, and decades make the keepers' recall of the longest histories.

The discipline is open to any Kaliharan who is taught, and the household and lineage elders teach it. The [Ithakan]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"} keepers and the holders of record carry it furthest, since the longest accounts are theirs.

## Levels of mastery

Vaarthari grows like any skill. At low mastery a Kaliharan can memorize some things, and the details go muddy: the sense of an account stays, and a name, a number or a clause may not. As mastery rises the recall firms, and at very high mastery a memorizer holds vast amounts of detailed information, each piece exact. The masters of the skill are the ones who teach it to others.

## What a trained memorizer can do

- Recite a lineage, a pact, a season-by-season account of one place or a maintenance procedure in full, in the order it was taught.
- Bring back a minute detail from a very long oral history without running through the whole.
- Check a fresh recitation against what was taught and find where it has drifted.

## The holders of record

The high masters are the [Vaarnei]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the "holders of record". They are commissioned to record important documents and agreements, much as notaries public are elsewhere: the parties speak the terms before a holder, and the holder attests to the details of even a complex agreement. A master may hold the details of many hundreds of these, together with laws, decrees and histories, and a holder's recital is what a council, a lineage or a port warden relies on when a dispute turns on what was agreed.

## Why writing is unnecessary

What a Kaliharan holds in memory is exact, and an exact account does not need a page to keep it. A household has its looking, its lineage and its terrace in the people who hold them, so ordinary Kaliharans have no book to fill and no use for one. The people's archive is what its holders recite and remember.

## Why it is secure

Knowledge passes only by being taught, from a holder to a learner who must be willing, so there is nothing to take. A book can be stolen, copied or read by anyone who learns its letters, and an inscription can be traced. A recitation cannot be taken from a person who will not give it, and a stranger cannot learn it by watching. The keepers rely on this above all for the secrets of how the instruments the [[lore-theithari|Ithari]] left work: those are held by the keepers and taught only within their formation.

## The keepers and the marks

The keepers know the meaning of the few stone marks the Ithari left, and seldom need to. A mark is a sign, not text: what it stands for is held in the keepers' memory by Vaarthari and recited, and the mark calls it up for a keeper who knows it. To anyone else a slab is a few marks and nothing more.

## In play

A character with Vaarthari tests it to bring back a detail from an account they were taught: a name, a date, a clause of a pact. Mastery decides how exact the recollection is. A success gives the detail exact and with its context. A failure is not a blank: it yields a muddy or partial recollection, the sense of the account without the particular needed, or the particular with its neighbors confused, so that the character has to recite from the start to reach it or settle for what they have. A holder of record at high mastery rarely fails on an account they were commissioned to hold. It cannot be gained from a book or a stolen copy, and it grows under a teacher. An account the character was never taught is beyond it, however high the skill.
