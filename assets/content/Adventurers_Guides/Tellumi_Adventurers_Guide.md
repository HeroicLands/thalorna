> You come down to [[place-mehrnagord|Mehrnāgord]] past the salt pans at first light, when the crust underfoot is still cold and the whole dry lake glitters pink and white to the foot of the hills. Ahead the oasis is a dark line of date palms and a mud-brick wall, its lowest courses older and paler than the rest, as though the town had been rebuilt on top of itself many times over. Inside the gate the air is thick with the smell of boiling palm-sugar and wet clay. Salt-cutters stack gray blocks on a donkey's panniers. Two gardeners argue over a sluice in low, exact voices, each holding a clay tablet. At the spring a young mother in a saffron shawl lifts a swaddled baby before a small shrine, and the old woman beside her sprinkles water on its forehead from a copper bowl. Nobody hurries, and nobody raises a voice.
>
> A man in a long gray coat sits under an awning near the gate with a wooden board across his knees, pressing marks into a damp clay tablet with a reed. He finishes the line before he looks up, then studies your face and your boots as if entering both. "_Lirun gaben_," he says, a free day to you, and touches his chest. "Murelul. My family's tablets were kept at Kethramír. I keep tablets for the house of this oasis." He turns the board so you can see the tablet, sets a fresh one beside it and lifts the reed. "Your name, and the city you come from." He listens with his head tilted, says both back to you in his own soft accent, and presses them into the clay as they sounded to him.

The Tellumi are the oldest people of the [[place-khzryndsrtrgn|Khazryn]], and the one that writes everything down. Their country, [[place-swoasisbelt|Tellumel]], is a strip of springs, canals and gardens along the north foot of the [[place-graznmntns|Grazian Mountains]], forty to eighty miles deep where the snowmelt reaches it and about seven hundred miles long; north of it the black rock of Dikraqor begins within a day's ride. Seven walled cities stand in the strip, and the Tellumi built all seven. Ninety years ago the [[affiliation-tribestrzd|Ātárzád]], twelve tribes who had come to them as refugees and been given land under the desert's law of hospitality, turned on their hosts and took the four greatest. The Tellumi call that war Turem, the Breaking.

Three oases still answer to Tellumi princes, and four princely houses hold court in exile in [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Amradad]]. Most of the eight hundred thousand Tellumi live as craftsmen, clerks, physicians and water-engineers inside cities their grandparents governed, paying a tax nobody else pays for water their own engineers keep running.

**The one thing to understand first: a Tellumi keeps the record.** The exile houses work for revun, the return of their cities, and they keep the case for it ready: genealogies, deeds, temple registers, the boundaries of fields nobody has farmed in three generations. A household that lets its tablets lapse has done what the Breaking could not do by force. A Tellumi who asks which city your family comes from is asking which court kept its records, and the answer tells another Tellumi a great deal.

## Down from the Mountains

The Tellumi tell a stranger early that they came down from the mountains, because it is the one fact about them that no conqueror has been able to revise. About 2300 BF the first lineages came down by the [[place-wstrndscnt|Western Descent]], the last three hundred miles of the range, where the wall breaks into brown foothills, and settled the great springs one by one. Every old house roots its genealogy in a named peak above the Descent, and most of those peaks no living member of the house has seen. Some houses hold that their ancestors came instead through [[place-gurdi|Gurdi]], a labyrinth of cut passages that climbs into the north face from a plain doorway forty feet high, and the quarrel between the two accounts is old and bitter. Both sides agree that a house whose peak is lost has lost its claim.

That makes the mountains a place of business. A family with a dispute over precedence pays climbers to prove a peak, and an exile house pays a party to find its ancestral passage in Gurdi while a rival house pays to stop it. In the first mile of Gurdi crag lurkers drop from the ceiling onto whoever carries the lamp.

Travelers reach the Tellumel by three roads. The Descent brings them from Dunhara and Amradad, the one stretch of the range that needs no guide. The southern spur of [[lore-celestialroad|the Celestial Road]] brings them down from [[place-qisomrktcmp|Qìso]] after about twenty days on the stone plateau, buying passage from each dowek along it. A Grazian pass brings them from Vedyara, under hereditary guides, in a few weeks of late summer.

## Three Free Oases

[[place-mehrnagord|Mehrnāgord]], at the western end by the salt pans, is the largest oasis still ruled by a Tellumi garen (prince), with thirty thousand people in the city and a hundred and ten thousand more in the gardens, salt-works and villages round it. Every household clears the buried channels before the melt by turns, and the turns are recorded on tablets. Its prince, Pārván, is in his sixties and cautious, and Ātárzád merchants have sounded him out about a trade compact that the exile houses call treason. The Mihrān who hold Shirvan could take his city in a season, and they leave it alone because its prince is a channel to the Sultanate and its salt reaches markets they cannot.

[[place-ushtraber|Ushtra-bēr]], at the far eastern end under the Eastern Reach, is the last founded and the most austere. Its streets are swept every dawn, and its terraced fields climb a thousand feet up a glacier valley. Its garen, Vāhrām, keeps court forms forgotten everywhere else. A petitioner kneels at a marked place on the floor and waits while the Keeper of the Tablets reads the petition aloud, and a stranger who presents himself as he would in Amradad is made to begin again. Learn the forms before you go, because the house has no other hospitality.

[[place-vahurdash|Vahúrdash]], on the dry northern rim where the streams fail, is the poorest of the three and the proudest. Four thousand settlers live among the roofless streets of [[place-velkunar|Velkun]], the old city a drought emptied, and drink from two wells they found again by copying the city's plans from tablets in other cities' archives. Its head holds his title by the settlers' acclamation, and the tablets that would prove his house's descent lie somewhere under the old city.

## Four Held Cities

> In the lower town of Shirvan you wait with the Tellumi at the basin gate for the hour their quarter is given. The great spring breaks from the foot of a pale cliff into a basin the size of a market square, and on its far side an Ātárzád fire-temple, built from the stones of Tellumi temples, keeps a flame burning over the water. Nobody in your line looks at it. At the gate an assessor in a fire-warden's sash writes down each household's name before it draws, and the woman ahead of you pays her water-tax, counts her change twice and folds the receipt into her sleeve.

The Ātárzád hold the four greatest cities, a tribe to each.

- The [[place-oasishirvn|Oasis of Shirvan]], the first and holiest, sixty thousand people under the Mōrávar cliff, belongs to the Mihrān, the strictest of the twelve tribes. Tellumi Mōbads hold no temple there and hear cases in back rooms. The quarter swears that a Hidden Mōbad keeps the old rite somewhere near the spring, and the Mihrān fire-wardens have searched for him for ninety years.
- [[place-ashkarad|Ashkarad]], the craft city, belongs to the Ardashír, the merchant tribe, who buy what the workshops make and leave the craft councils alone. Each shaft of its deep water-tunnels is a family possession.
- [[place-zargandur|Zargandûr]], once the Tellumi war-city, is the garrison of the Bāhrām, with the highest walls in the belt and a picket at every weir. A Tellumi caught with a blade after dark is beaten and fined, the Tellumi quarter keeps a lamp burning at every door until dawn, and beyond the north wall lies [[place-henhulpedin|Henhulpedin]], the blood-field where the war-house died.
- [[place-kethramir|Kethramír]], where the southern spur comes down to the belt, belongs to the Zarvān, the scholar tribe, who rule by precedent and keep a small garrison. Its hospitality is stricter than its law: a merchant who cheats there finds that no guide will lead his caravan.

[[place-caldar|Caldar]], a later city grown round a university, is Ātárzád-held too, and the seven-city record does not count it.

## Under the Conquerors

Inside the four cities the Tellumi live in designated quarters, usually the old districts with lanes too narrow for a settler's cart. They have not been bondsmen for two generations, and they remain subjects in concrete ways: no land outside their quarters, no public worship of any god but the Sacred Flame, no arms outside the militias the Ātárzád permit, and the water-tax, a levy on city water that falls on Tellumi heads of household alone.

What the Tellumi hold is expertise. They are the water-engineers, palm-grove cultivators, physicians, scribes in three tongues, bankers and astronomers, and the cities do not run without the people who keep their canals. Every Ātárzád suppression of a rising, however brutal, has stopped short of emptying a Tellumi quarter for that reason, and neither people finds the arrangement comfortable.

A Tellumi under a magistrate is courteous, exact and reserved. He keeps two calendars, the old Tellumi year of twelve thirty-day months closed by a five-day festival at the spring equinox, and whatever the city uses. He pays what is assessed on time and keeps the receipt, because a subject with imperfect paperwork can be robbed legally. He does not discuss politics with an Ātárzád and discusses little else with another Tellumi. In Shirvan someone in the quarter reports to the Mihrān, and nobody knows who.

## The House Records

> Behind a carpenter's shop in Kethramír, a Mōbad in a plain brown coat hears a dispute over a dead man's hours of water. Each family has brought a tablet in a cloth bag. He reads both aloud, lays them side by side and sends for a third from the quarter's archive. It comes out of a niche in the wall, wrapped in palm fiber and older than either family. He reads it twice, gives his ruling, and has his clerk press the ruling into fresh clay while everyone waits.

Every Tellumi ninel (household) keeps records the way other households keep silver: genealogies, deeds, water-rights and temple registers. A tenyul (clay tablet) does not burn well, which is the only reason anything survived the fall of Shirvan, when the great temple's archive went up and the fleeing households carried out what they could. That clay is scattered across the exile courts now, and every house wants the tablets another holds. Literacy is expected of anyone above a laborer, and reading the hand written before the Breaking marks a serious person.

The seven princely houses are the spine of all of it. Every Tellumi knows which house his family stood to, which is to say which court kept its records, and a man who says he is from Kethramír tells another Tellumi "priest-house, theologically serious, took the worst of the killing of the clergy" without a further word. The three ruling houses govern their oases. The four balem (exile) houses keep full courts of a few hundred souls each in Amradad, where a prince can recite every ancestor back to the founding of his line. Once a year all seven meet as the Council of the Princes under the High Mōbad of Āhúrdáén, and coordinate without governing anyone. Khávar of Zargandûr, twenty-seven, trains with the Sultanate's soldiers, and the Council has neither endorsed nor forbidden what he is preparing.

Where there is no Tellumi court, the Mōbad is the government. He studies fifteen to twenty years before ordination, in astronomy, mathematics, theology, law and at least three tongues, and he registers marriages, judges inheritance and keeps the nartem (law and custom) of his community under a foreign magistrate who cares only that the taxes arrive. A family sends its ablest child to the Mōbadate ahead of any trade.

## Water, Faith and Trade

Water is wealth and law at once. In Shirvan a household owns the right to a few hours in a named channel on a given day, the right passes by deed, and the ledgers of those deeds are the most valuable documents in the city; the Tellumi water-masters who keep them know which ones the tax assessors have never seen. In Zargandûr whoever holds the weirs holds the horses, the grain and the dates, and in a dry year the Bāhrām cut the Tellumi plots off first.

The faith runs through the water. Every oasis and every Tellumi quarter keeps a shrine to [[affiliation-zurathra|Zúráthrä]], the Mother of the Oasis, at its principal spring; every wedding takes her blessing, and every child is presented to her within forty days of birth. Formal worship belongs to [[affiliation-ahurdaen|Āhúrdáén]], the Keeper of Harmony, and no other god, the Sacred Flame included, may take the weekly observance owed to him. The Ātárzád worship the Flame alone, and when they took Shirvan they pulled down Zúráthrä's great shrine and built fires over the holy spring.

An oasis lives on dates, palm-sugar, grain, wool and hides, and Mehrnāgord on salt as well. The great cities add metalwork, glazed pottery, books and a date wine the Sultanate prizes. Caravans come down the spur to Kethramír with silk, spices, tea and steel, and argue prices for days in three tongues. The Tellumi strike no coin of their own, and weighed silver and foreign pieces pass by their metal. The steppe tribes raid the outer gardens when they dare. The Tellumi keep their hospitality all the same. A neyin (guest) in extremity is fed, housed and asked nothing, and the Tellumi took the Ātárzád in under exactly that rule and have never amended it.

## What Gets a Newcomer into Trouble

- **Do not drink on the fire-temple's side of Shirvan's basin**, and do not ask a Tellumi why.
- **Keep every receipt.** A subject with imperfect paperwork can be robbed legally, and the assessor at the basin gate writes down every name.
- **Carry no blade after dark in Zargandûr**, and no copy of Yázdín of Kethramír's _On the Ungrantable Land_ anywhere the Ātárzád rule.
- **Never talk politics with a Tellumi in front of an Ātárzád**, or the other way round.
- **Learn the forms before you petition the court of Ushtra-bēr.**
- **Stay behind the whitewashed stakes north of Zargandûr**, and never name the field in Bāhrām hearing.
- **Give your true name and city.** A Tellumi records both, and a false one is a lie entered in clay.

## Ways In

A party can come down the spur with a caravan to Kethramír, over the Descent to Mehrnāgord, or up from Amradad in an exile house's service. A Tellumi character might be a water-engineer's apprentice in Zargandûr's quarter, a Mōbad's clerk who reads the old hand, a younger child of an exile court who has never seen the city the house claims, or a settler's child from Vahúrdash who knows every stretch of the channel.

At the first gate, three questions place a character among the Tellumi: **Which house did your family stand to? Where are its tablets? What do you owe the magistrate, and what do you keep from him?**

Campaigns start well from the record. Pārván wants an envoy to carry an offer to Amradad, and the exile houses want to be sure it never arrives. The house of Vahúrdash needs the old archive found, and a rival needs it lost. A Zarvān scholar wants a copy of the banned commentary so he can answer it, and a Mōbad in the quarter wants the scholar's answer so she can answer back.

:::secret
**For the GM:** The four held cities run on Tellumi hands, and both peoples know it. A party that learns which water-master keeps which unrecorded deed in Shirvan holds the quarter's real wealth; a party that learns who in the quarter reports to the Mihrān holds its life. The Hidden Mōbad may be a person or a useful story, and the quarter's faith in him works either way.
:::
