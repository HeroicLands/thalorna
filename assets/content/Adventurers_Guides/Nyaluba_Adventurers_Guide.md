> The baobab comes into sight long before the town does: a gray trunk as wide as a house, its bare branches spread against a white dry-season sky like roots turned upward. By the time you reach its shade the heat has pressed the color out of everything but the red dust on your legs. Round the tree spreads a market. A tall Ruwozi herder with a single lion's tooth on a thong at his throat leans on his staff beside a knot of cattle, debating the price of a heifer in long, unhurried sentences. A woman whose hair is braided close in a pattern of lines and knots sets out baskets of sorghum. Kolembozi fishermen from the eastern wetlands have spread papyrus mats with smoked fish and gray cakes of salt; a Sengala trader in a cloth of northern blue lays out iron hoe-blades and glass beads, and a quiet Ngonzi smith keeps his small gold ornaments wrapped until a buyer asks. From somewhere beyond the grass comes the sound of a drum, rising and falling in phrases like speech, and from nearer a second drum answers it.
>
> Under the baobab sits a young woman in an ochre wrap, an old man asleep beside her with his chin on his chest. She rises when she sees you, puts her right hand flat to her breastbone, and says, "_Nyalu mi-bóo_," the Nyáluban greeting: the Nyáluba greet you. Then, slowly, so that you can follow, "I am Zenda, of the Ndánga-Sólembe, of the Lobwezi, under the Pact." She asks your name, and your father's, and where you slept last night. She repeats all three back to you exactly, twice, her lips moving a third time after she has finished.

The [[affiliation-nylbtrblntn|Nyáluba]] are not one people. They are five clan-nations of the [[place-sthrnsvnhs|Southern Savannahs]] of [[place-xerathia|Zerathia]]—Ruwozi cattle-herders of the central plains, Kolembozi river-people of the eastern wetlands, Ngonzi highlanders and goldsmiths, Sengala traders of the northern margin, and Lobwezi cultivators around their ancient trees—who differ in dress, dialect, ritual and marriage custom. Nine generations ago, under a baobab in Ruwozi country, their ancestors swore the Long Pact: no arms raised between the five, a spear against one a spear against all, the pastures, rivers and rains shared, and the Pact renewed in person every fourth dry season. There is no king, no capital, no army and no priesthood. There is the Pact, and the people who keep it remembered.

**The one thing to understand first: what you say in front of a griot is entered in the record.** The Nyáluba write nothing down. Griots carry the genealogies, the boundaries, the terms of the Pact and who owes whom what, and a griot's whole standing rests on remembering exactly. Zenda repeated your name because she was entering it. A careless boast or a promise made in her hearing will be recited back to you, word for word, years later.

## Councils, Drums and Spirits

Each clan governs itself under its paramount and its elders, and every fourth dry season the Great Lubemo meets at [[place-hetolubemo|Heto Lubemo]] to settle what touches all five. Councils do not vote. They talk until the objections stop, in an order fixed by custom, with the senior speaker last; the slowness is the method, and a decision reached that way holds. Between Lubemos the paramounts consult by drum: drum-speakers relay messages in a tonal register of the language across all five territories in about two days.

Faith runs through totems and spirit guides. Each clan is kin to its totem—lion, crocodile, eagle, leopard or baobab—and does not hunt or eat it, and totem law shapes marriage, diet and the greeting of strangers. Every spirit is venerated, appeased or warded against according to its nature, and spirit-speakers negotiate with them on a clan's behalf. Above all of them is [[lore-shorinyadty|Shorinya Ndafo]], the Great Brightness, which receives no offering.

## Places and Dangers

- [[place-hetolubemo|Heto Lubemo]], an ancient stone enclosure in the southeastern highlands, holds the Great Lubemo for six weeks every fourth dry season.
- The Old Hetos of the highlands—curving walls of fitted stone and raised platforms left by a people who built before the Nyáluba—belong to the Ngonzi, and their guardians admit no one else.
- The Kolembozi wetlands are a maze of papyrus channels and crocodile pools whose river-spirits are numerous and unforgiving.
- [[place-fenjaravlg|Fénjara Village]], under the gallery forest, is home to trail-readers who follow tracks across the spirit world.
- The Sengala, on the Nyáluba's northern margin at the rainforest's southern edge, are the only door most outsiders ever use, through the rainforest peoples to the north.

## Ways In

Most outsiders arrive with Sengala traders, and a party that carries goods south may find itself asked to carry word north. A Fénjara trail-reader may need hired spears for a hunt whose quarry does not leave footprints. A spirit pact broken in one village wants a spirit-speaker, a witness and someone willing to go where the spirit lives. And the griots have entered in the record that a Kolembozi spirit-speaker walked into an Old Heto at her initiation and came out speaking the Stone Tongue, which no living person understands; what the tongue is for, nobody will say.
