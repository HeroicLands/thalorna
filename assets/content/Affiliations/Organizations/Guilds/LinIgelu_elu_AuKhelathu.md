## Overview

**Lin'Igelu elu Aû'Khelâthu** is the guild of the rivermen and sea-captains of [[place-aukhelathrgq|Aû'Khelâthu]], open to any master who meets its standard of seamanship. Its charter tells a cargo-owner that a master has been examined, and it gives the master a body to argue for him against the merchant house that hired him. The guild also sets the safety requirements cargo must meet before a vessel carries it.

## Character

The old captain does not say "welcome aboard." He says, "Show me your charter," and then he reads it, front and back, with the patience of a man who has been sued. The **Mariners' Guild** is the chartered body of the empire's rivermen and sea-captains, and it admits any master who meets its standard of seamanship. An Igelar, as its members are called, is someone who holds that charter, and it travels with him wherever the cargo goes.

The guild is professional and protective. It sets the standard of seamanship and the safety a cargo must meet before a vessel carries it, and it speaks for its captains when the merchant houses that hire them dispute a loss. A young rower learns early that the charter is both a license and a shield: it tells a cargo-owner that the master has been examined, and it gives the master a body to argue for him against the house.

A captain without a charter can find work, and plenty do. The cargo-owners who care about recourse look for the charter first.

## Relations

The guild stands between two parties, and its position is the point. The merchant houses hire its members, and they are the party the guild argues against in a dispute. Cargo-owners look to the charter for recourse when goods are lost, so the guild's reputation for fairness is also the owners' security.

## Commerce and Currency

Say a house sends forty sacks of grain downriver and two arrive wet. The owner goes to the guild's charter, which names the captain and the standard he was examined against. The guild hears the dispute, and its decision settles the loss. A captain who has no charter offers a lower rate and no recourse; the owner who accepts it has priced the risk himself.
