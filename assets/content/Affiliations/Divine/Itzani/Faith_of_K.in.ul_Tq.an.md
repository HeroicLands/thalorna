**Domain:** Order, Cosmic Law, Solar Cycle

K'in'ul Tq'an is the greatest and most terrible of the Itzáni gods—the sovereign of the Fifth Age, the maintainer of cosmic order, and the celestial emperor whose constancy alone prevents the return of chaos. He is not worshipped with comfort or mercy. Rather, K'in'ul Tq'an demands absolute fidelity to the cosmic cycle, and his worship is one of rigid duty, sacred obligation, and the grim knowledge that upon his shoulders rests the continuation of existence itself. The sun does not rise by divine benevolence—it rises because blood has been spilled, because the gods were satisfied with sacrifice, because mortals have kept faith with their burden. His priests preach that there is no grace in the cosmos, only the reciprocal cycle of gift and obligation.

## Worship

The solstices are the most sacred astronomical events of the solar year. At the winter solstice, the K'inmah himself ascends the pyramid and draws his own blood into the eternal flames while priests chant the binding spells that hold the sun to its path of return. At the summer solstice, priests sever the hearts of sacrificial captives at the pyramid's peak, collecting the blood in jade vessels and pouring it down the temple's great stone channels, carved with astronomical glyphs. Personal bloodletting in honor of K'in'ul Tq'an—by piercing the tongue, the earlobes, or the genitals with obsidian blades—is considered a privilege of the honored, a way to enter into direct communion with the solar god. Warriors who have captured enemies are expected to perform such ceremonies, dedicating their captives' coming sacrifices in advance.

Between the solstices the god is served by an unbroken rhythm of small observances that the priesthood insists matter as much as the great rites, because the debt accrues daily. Every dawn a Ch'ul'bal stands at the pyramid's eastern stair and speaks the sun's name as the first light reaches the summit; if the words are late, the omission is recorded and expiated. Households mark the same hour more modestly, the head of the family pressing an obsidian flake to the earlobe and letting a few drops fall on the threshold. Merchants opening a stall, masons setting a first stone and farmers breaking new ground each give a token of blood, and the day's work is held to be unsanctioned without it.

The **Warden of the Debt** keeps the ledger against which all of this is measured. Blood given is entered in bark-paper registers alongside the celestial events it was meant to answer, and the surplus or shortfall is announced publicly at the close of each twenty-day month. A city in surplus enjoys a season of confidence; a city in deficit sees its festivals lengthened, its captives' sacrifices brought forward, and its lay faithful pressed toward the Debt-Bearer's vow. Foreign observers have noted that the ledger is never audited by anyone outside the priesthood, and Ki'ichek skeptics have occasionally said as much aloud—though rarely twice.

Vows made to K'in'ul Tq'an are the heaviest a Ki'ichek can undertake, because the god is understood to keep his own accounts with perfect precision and infinite patience. A warrior who dedicates a captive and then ransoms him instead has not merely broken faith with a temple but has left a hole in the cosmic reckoning, and the priesthood teaches that such holes are filled from the debtor's own household, in the god's own time and by the god's own choosing.

## Organization

The great solar pyramids of K'in'ul Tq'an are always the tallest structures in any city, their peaks the first to catch dawn light. The K'ul Tq'an of K'in'ul Tq'an's temple is the most politically powerful religious figure in any city-state—the supreme voice of cosmic order. When a king dies, it is the K'ul Tq'an who determines whether the gods approve the heir's succession, a power that has elevated and destroyed dynasties.

Beneath him the solar branch is the largest and the most rigidly ordered of the specialist branches, and alone among them it is organized as a chain of command rather than as a college of experts. The **Ah K'in** and his computers hold the solar calculations; the **Keeper of the Binding** and the **Master of the Channels** hold the solstice rites themselves; the **Master of Ascent** holds the terraces, and with them the visible order of precedence by which every noble house in the city learns each year where it stands. That last office is trivial in doctrine and enormous in practice, and its holder is courted accordingly.

Recruitment favors the well-born, in deliberate contrast to the meritocratic astronomers of [[affiliation-ixilanchalix|Ix'ilan Ch'alix]]. A solar Tq'an'ik is usually the second or third son of a noble house, given to the temple young; the branch argues that the rites require men who can stand unmoved before a crowd of thousands and that such bearing is bred rather than taught. The astronomers reply, privately, that the solar priests need well-born initiates because they borrow the calculations they cannot make themselves. Both claims are partly true, and the friction between them is a permanent feature of temple politics.

The branch's wealth comes from the tribute of the K'inmah's own estates, from a share of the spoils of every campaign, and from the fees paid by families seeking an auspicious day for a marriage, a building or a journey. It maintains no experimental gardens, no observatories of its own and no library beyond the ritual and succession registers—the solar priests are not scholars, and they do not pretend to be. What they hold is the sanction, and every other branch's work reaches the city through them.

## Relations

The solar branch's closest and least troubled partnership is with the fire-keepers of [[affiliation-kayikchul|K'ayik Ch'ul]]. The two priesthoods share a single theological premise—that the sun is the fire at the world's edge and the hearth-flame its lesser kin—and they share the eternal flames themselves, which the fire-keepers tend and the solar priests use. At the New Fire Ceremony the two act as one body, and the Presiding K'ul Tq'an ascends the pyramid with the highest fire-priests beside him rather than behind him, a precedence granted to no other branch.

With the astronomers of Ix'ilan Ch'alix the relationship is cooperative in fact and resentful in tone. The solstice hours, the eclipse warnings and the fifty-two-year realignment all come from the observatories, and the solar priesthood cannot function without them; but the goddess of knowledge is held to know when the Fifth Age will end, which is precisely the knowledge the solar branch claims to guard. Presiding K'ul Tq'ans have more than once ordered an astronomical prediction suppressed, and the astronomers have more than once let it be known that they were overruled.

Toward [[affiliation-puqilchaqun|P'uqil Ch'aqun]] the solar branch maintains a posture of wary containment. The storm-serpent's binding rites are necessary and the solar priests fund them, but the serpent-keepers' business is the end of the age, and every ceremony they perform reminds the city that K'in'ul Tq'an's sovereignty is temporary. The two branches negotiate constantly over the calendar: how many days may be given to the Binding of the Serpent before the city's mood turns from reverence to dread.

The god's oldest theological quarrel is with [[affiliation-tzuqilixbal|Tz'uqil Ix'bal]], and it is not a quarrel of priesthoods but of premises. The solar god teaches that the cycle must be held; the death jaguar teaches that everything within it is meant to end. The two branches cooperate scrupulously—the death-walkers bury the solar priests, the solar priests sanction the death-walkers' festivals—and neither has ever conceded a point of doctrine to the other. It was the solar god's refusal to spend cosmic resources destroying a mere functionary of the death god's household that left [[affiliation-pikultzumaq|Pik'ul Tz'umaq]] at large, a decision the orthodox priesthood does not discuss and has never repudiated.
