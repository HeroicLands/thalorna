**Domain:** Dreams, Visions, Prophecy, and the Moon

Nal'ik Tz'uqal is unique among the Itzáni gods: she is one of the few who does not demand blood, accepting instead offerings of cacao, flowers, incense, and the spoken word. She is gentle where other deities are fearsome, and she teaches and illuminates where others demand sacrifice. Yet she is profoundly powerful, for she governs the visions that shape the decisions of kings, the prophecies that alter wars, and the dreams that provide glimpses of possible futures. In Ki'ichek understanding, dreams are not mere illusions but genuine perceptions of reality – alternate timelines, possible futures, the movements of the gods through the celestial spheres. Her celestial influence is most powerful during the dark of the moon, when the boundary between waking and dream grows thin and visions are most reliable.

## Worship

The faithful sleep in sacred chambers to receive visions, prepared through specific prayers and breathing practices, sometimes given mild soporifics under careful priestly supervision. A petitioner is admitted for a stated question, sleeps under supervision, and on waking recites what he saw before speaking to anyone else; the account is written down as given, including what the dreamer thinks is nonsense, since the archive's value depends on the record being untidied.

The priesthood teaches that words are the most precious offering – a carefully composed prayer or beautifully spoken poem is more pleasing to Nal'ik Tz'uqal than a thousand cups of blood. Offerings are accordingly made aloud, and the **Master of the Word-Offering** keeps those judged worth preserving. This has made the dream-keepers popular among artists, poets, and scholars who feel alienated by the bloodthirsty demands of the other branches, and the branch's festivals are recitations rather than sacrifices – an entire night of composed speech, cacao and flowers, which foreign visitors routinely mistake for a secular entertainment.

The dark of the moon governs the branch's calendar as the solstices govern the solar priests'. The **Warden of the Dark Moon** fixes those nights, and admission to the chambers is heavily oversubscribed on them; a merchant deciding a venture or a general deciding a campaign will wait months for a place rather than dream on an ordinary night.

Ordinary devotion needs no temple. A household sets flowers and a cup of cacao by the sleeping mat before an important day and speaks the question aloud into the dark. What comes is remembered and, if it seems to matter, carried to the temple to be entered in the archive.

## Organization

The Tz'uqal'ob (dream-keepers) operate vast dream-temples – places of comfort and beauty built with soft materials, textiles draped from walls, sleeping platforms positioned where starlight and moonlight enter through carefully placed openings. They are the only Itzáni temples built for the comfort of the people who use them, and the builder-priests who raise them treat the commission as a change of trade.

The priesthood maintains the **Codex Tz'uqal**, a vast library of recorded dreams organized by symbol, outcome, and reliability. Over centuries, patterns emerge – dreams of jaguars precede moments requiring courage, dreams of water concern fertility or danger, dreams of fire involve passion or divine intervention. A **Reader of Symbols** interprets against those patterns and is required to state the reliability with the reading, which is the branch's central discipline and the source of its credibility: it will say when it does not know, and it says so often.

The Tz'uqal'ob are selected for artistic sensitivity and psychological insight rather than military capability. Their training emphasizes pattern recognition, lucid dreaming, and compassion. The branch takes women and men in roughly equal number, takes them from any rank, and is the usual refuge of the Ki'ichek who cannot stomach the blood the other branches require – a fact the other branches note without warmth.

Its political weight is real but indirect. A **K'ul Tz'uqal** who carries a vision to the K'inmah can move a decision no other priest could touch, and every branch knows it; consequently the dream-temples are watched, and a reading that happens to favor a faction is examined with great care by everyone else. The branch's defense is the archive itself, which records the reading and the outcome alike and is open to the priesthood.

## Relations

With [[affiliation-tzuqilixbal|Tz'uqil Ix'bal]] the branch keeps its closest working relationship. Dreams of the dead are common and are taken seriously; a dream-keeper who receives one refers the dreamer to the Ix'bal'ob rather than reading it alone, and the death-walkers in turn send the branch the bereaved whom the doctrine of death-as-passage has failed to console. The **Keeper of the Sleepers** exists largely for them.

With [[affiliation-ixilanchalix|Ix'ilan Ch'alix]] there is an amiable and unresolved rivalry. Both branches keep enormous classified archives of recorded phenomena and both claim to derive prediction from them, and they argue without rancor about whether a pattern found in dreams is knowledge of the same kind as a pattern found in the sky. In practice they share methods freely, and the Codex Tz'uqal's system of ordering by reliability was borrowed from the observatories.

Toward [[affiliation-kinultqan|K'in'ul Tq'an]] the branch is a standing theological irritant. The solar priesthood teaches that there is no grace in the cosmos, only reciprocal obligation; here is a goddess who takes flowers and gives visions and asks nothing in blood. The solar priests do not dispute her standing – she is one of the Ten – and they do not much like explaining her.

[[affiliation-xaqikhanal|Xaq'ik Ha'nal]] and the dream-keepers are natural allies, being the two branches that ask least and give most, and in many cities their temples adjoin. Women come to one for healing and the other for counsel, and the priestesses and dream-keepers pass petitioners between them without ceremony.
