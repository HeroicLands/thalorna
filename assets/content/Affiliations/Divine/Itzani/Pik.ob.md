The **Pik'ob**—the owl keepers—represent the contemplative branch of [[affiliation-tzuqilixbal|Tz'uqil Ix'bal]]'s service. Where the Ix'bal'ob serve the jaguar who kills swiftly, the Pik'ob serve Pik'ik, the owl on the death god's brow, who watches as the jungle slowly reclaims what death has taken. They maintain the **Ch'ulba K'anal** ("Sacred Groves"), sanctuaries where the natural cycle of decay is allowed to proceed with minimal interference. In regions where the owl-aspect holds particular reverence, sky burial is practiced—bodies exposed on platforms where birds and insects reduce them to bare bone, accelerating the return to the earth.

The Pik'ob teach that the proper way to live is to create beautiful things knowing they will decay, to build communities knowing they will crumble, and to love people knowing they will die—and to embrace this not as tragedy but as freedom.

## Practice

The order's central discipline has no liturgy and no fixed hours. A candidate is brought to a grove and asked to sit in a place where things are rotting, and then asked, over months and years, what they find objectionable about it. There is no correct answer and no examination; a **Teacher of Dissolution** instructs chiefly by sitting alongside and correcting almost nothing. This makes the Pik'ob the slowest-forming body in the Itzáni priesthood and the one least able to explain to the other branches what its initiates are actually doing.

The groves hold no images. They are bounded, not built: a **Grove-Keeper** marks the edge, keeps the living out where the practice requires it, and otherwise ensures nothing is done. The discipline is entirely one of restraint, and the register a keeper maintains records what the forest took and when—the fall of a limb, the collapse of a trunk, the year a platform gave way—which the order reads as the god's own account of itself.

Where sky burial is practiced the **Platform-Master** conducts it and the **Reader of the Birds** attends, reporting what came, in what order, and how long it took. The bared bone is gathered by the **Keeper of the Bone** and returned to the family; what no family claims stays in the grove's ossuary, unsorted, which visitors from other branches find more disturbing than the exposure itself.

## The Abandoned Temple

Among the Pik'ob, some philosophers argue for the ideal of the "Abandoned Temple"—a deliberate choice to build a sacred structure and then, after a period, cease maintaining it and allow it to be reclaimed by the forest. This practice disturbs the other branches of the priesthood, who view it as a rejection of proper temple stewardship. Yet the Pik'ob point to the great abandoned cities being consumed by jungle as visible proof that their theology is correct—that even the grandest monuments are temporary, and that accepting this is wisdom, not despair. These tensions between preservation and acceptance of dissolution play out constantly within the unified priesthood, a theological argument with real consequences for how resources are allocated and which buildings are maintained.

A **Warden of the Abandoned Temple** holds such a structure and, in the only duty of its kind anywhere in the tradition, prevents anyone from repairing it. The office is not popular. Wardens are routinely petitioned by the families who paid for the building, and occasionally by the builder-priests who raised it, and the order's answer—that the surrender was the point and was agreed to in advance—satisfies almost nobody.

## Recruitment

The Pik'ob take few initiates and take them late. A candidate is usually a person who has already lost a great deal, and the order is frank that it recruits from grief. A **Grieving** newcomer is permitted to sit in a grove under no obligation and is asked for nothing; most never come back, and the order regards that as the practice working rather than failing. Those who do return are received for the long formation, and profession comes years afterward, if at all.

Because the order takes the bereaved and teaches them the impermanence of everything, it sits permanently near a dangerous edge—and it knows it. The **Warden of the Drifting** exists for exactly that: to watch those whose philosophy is moving from acceptance toward acceleration, and to put them out of the groves before they go further. It is the office that lets the rest of the priesthood tolerate the order's existence.

## Relations

Within the death faith the Pik'ob and the Ix'bal'ob divide the god between them and rarely quarrel: the death-walkers hold the recitations, the descent and the codices, and the owl keepers hold the groves and the long return. The **K'ul Pik'ob** answers to the death-temple's K'ul Tq'an for what the order's philosophers say in public, which is the whole of the supervision the branch receives and, most years, more than it needs.

With the builder-priests of [[affiliation-piqalkulqat|P'iqal Kul'qat]] the order carries the tradition's most distinctive standing argument. The builders hold that a raised structure is a participation in the goddess's unfinished work; the Pik'ob hold that surrendering one is a participation in the same truth from the other side. The quarrel is fought out annually in the allocation of masons between new work and repair, and neither branch expects to win it.

The order's real difficulty is that its theology shares a root with the tradition's worst heresy. Both the Pik'ob and the [[affiliation-tzumaqkalanil|Tz'umaq K'alanil]] emerge from Tz'uqil Ix'bal's domain, and both grapple with the meaning of dissolution and the impermanence of all things. The Pik'ob draw the line at acceptance; the Serpent Awakeners cross it into acceleration. But the boundary between "accepting that all things end" and "hastening the ending" is thinner than the orthodox priesthood would like, and Pik'ob philosophers who drift too close to that line sometimes vanish into the jungle, where the Awakeners welcome them—and where [[affiliation-pikultzumaq|Pik'ul Tz'umaq]], once the greatest of the celestial owls the order serves, is understood to be waiting for them.
