**Domain:** Storms, Chaos, Rain, Destruction, and Cosmic Dissolution

P'uqil Ch'aqun is the most terrifying and most desperately worshipped deity in the Itzáni pantheon—the feathered storm-serpent who brings both the rain that sustains life and the catastrophe that ends it. He is depicted as a vast serpent of jade and obsidian scales, plumed in iridescent feathers the colors of storm-cloud and lightning, coiled within a thunderhead so massive it blots out the sun. His open jaws crackle with lightning. His wings are hurricane winds. His coils are earthquake tremors. He is not two gods but one—the same divine power that sends gentle rain to nourish the maize also sends the hurricane that flattens cities. This dual nature is the central theological problem of his worship: how do you venerate a god who feeds you with one hand and destroys you with the other? The answer is that creation and destruction are not opposites but aspects of the same cosmic force. Rain and hurricane are born from the same sky.

## Worship

Worship operates on two levels reflecting his dual nature. In his nourishing aspect, worship is most intense during the dry season, when cities conduct the **k'ixkan ch'alix** (rain-calling ceremonies) on the highest pyramid peaks. Priests ascend in ritual procession, chanting in archaic Ki'ichek while pouring sacred water and making rhythmic motions to mimic rainfall. Some ceremonies involve fasting and sustained incense exposure, inducing altered states in which priests commune with the serpent's sleeping form. When the first rains come, the celebration is immediate and ecstatic—water drums beat to harmonize with the god's own thunder-voice, and bathing ceremonies fill the newly replenished cenotes.

The rain-calling escalates by fixed stages as the dry season lengthens, and every Ki'ichek can read the stage from the street. A short procession and poured water is routine. A procession that returns without descending—the priests remaining on the peak through a second night—tells the city the rains are late. When the **K'ul Ha'tq'an** himself ascends, fasting, the city understands that the ordinary measures have failed, and the granaries begin to be rationed before any official word is given. Beyond that stage lie the captive sacrifices, and beyond those, in living memory only twice, the abandonment of the city.

In his destructive aspect, worship takes the form of the **Tz'umaq'tzuqalil** ("Binding of the Serpent"), conducted when the god is believed most restless. The ceremony requires mass bloodletting, offerings of jade and maize, and ritual recitation of the names of all four previous ages and their destructions. The purpose is twofold: to symbolically bind the serpent with chains of blood and word, and to provide an outlet for the existential anxiety of living under an apocalyptic shadow.

That second purpose is the one the serpent-keepers actually manage, and they manage it deliberately. A city that never hears the ages recited grows complacent and stops giving; a city that hears them too often stops working. The **Reckoner of Ages** therefore does not recite on a fixed calendar but on the K'ul Tz'umaq'ob's judgment, and the judgment is political as much as theological. Earthquakes, unseasonable storms and the failure of a harvest all bring the recitation forward. So, the other branches note sourly, does any season in which the serpent-keepers' funding is in question.

Ordinary devotion is simpler and more constant. Households keep a jade chip or a green feather at the doorpost against the wind; travelers caught in the open give blood where they stand rather than wait for shelter; and a hurricane season passed without loss is answered with offerings at the next rain-calling, not with thanksgiving, since the god is not thanked for restraint he may withdraw.

## Organization

The ha'tq'an (water-priests) spend significant time at high-altitude temples, enduring exposure to lightning and storm. Many cultivate distinctive appearances—long hair woven with feathers and storm-talismans, visible scars from lightning strikes worn as insignia. A priest struck by lightning and surviving is considered marked by P'uqil Ch'aqun for special purposes, though the experience often leaves them scarred or slightly mad. Such a **Lightning-Marked** priest holds no fixed place in the hierarchy and is, in practice, outside it: he may contradict a K'ul in open ceremony without sanction, and the branch treats whatever he says as data to be recorded rather than as insubordination.

The Tz'umaq'ob (serpent-keepers) manage what might be called "controlled terror"—keeping knowledge of the coming apocalypse alive while preventing it from tipping into panic or rebellion. They maintain the sacred calculations predicting the celestial configuration of total dissolution, recorded in codices bound in serpent skin and stored in triple-sealed chambers. Only the K'ul Tz'umaq'ob, the Warden of the Sealed Chambers and the presiding hierarch of the city may read them, and the seals are inspected at every turning of the Tz'uqal Cycle before witnesses from two other branches—a precaution against forgery that says a good deal about how much the rest of the priesthood trusts this one.

The temples of P'uqil Ch'aqun's binding aspect are built to withstand destruction—walls unnaturally thick, sanctums anchored into bedrock, interiors packed with offerings to keep the god sated. These are repositories of sacred knowledge designed not to prevent the apocalypse but to survive it. Duplicates of the most important codices from every branch are lodged in them, and the builder-priests who raise them work to specifications the serpent-keepers supply and will not explain.

The two branches recruit from opposite ends of Ki'ichek society and do not mix easily. The ha'tq'an take farmers' and fishermen's children, people who have grown up reading weather because their families ate by it. The Tz'umaq'ob take from the scribal and noble houses, because their work is calculation, secrecy and the handling of powerful men. Both answer to the same K'ul Tq'an, and both consider the other to be serving half a god.

## Relations

The rain-callers and the priests of [[affiliation-kanixhakan|K'anix Ha'kan]] are, in ordinary years, a single working partnership: the water comes from one and the seed from the other, and the two branches jointly bless the Chuqtik planting. In a drought the partnership frays fast, since the agricultural priests must tell the city its stores are failing precisely when the water-priests must tell it the god has not yet answered, and neither wishes to be the branch that spoke first.

With [[affiliation-kinultqan|K'in'ul Tq'an]] the relationship is one of funding and containment. The solar branch pays for the binding rites and resents every day of the calendar they consume, because each Tz'umaq'tzuqalil reminds the city that solar sovereignty is a lease. Negotiations over the festival calendar between the two branches are annual, protracted and rarely cordial.

The serpent-keepers depend on the observatories of [[affiliation-ixilanchalix|Ix'ilan Ch'alix]] for the very calculations they keep sealed, and the arrangement is uncomfortable for both. The astronomers compute; the serpent-keepers interpret and conceal. More than one Ix'tq'an has pointed out that a branch which cannot check its own arithmetic is poorly placed to guard it, and more than one Warden of the Sealed Chambers has replied that a branch which publishes everything is poorly placed to guard anything.

The god's shadow falls hardest on the tradition's worst heresy. The **Tz'umaq K'alanil** ("Serpent Awakeners") teach that the Fifth Age is irredeemably corrupt and that hastening P'uqil Ch'aqun's awakening would be a mercy—that destruction would wipe clean a stained world and let the Sixth Age begin pure. They are hunted and suppressed, their temples destroyed and their priests executed, and they resurface repeatedly under different names. The Tz'umaq'ob are the branch charged with running them down, which is an irony nobody inside the priesthood finds funny: the serpent-keepers and the Awakeners read the same calculations and disagree only about what to do with them. See [[affiliation-tzumaqkalanil|Tz'umaq K'alanil]] for the heresy, and [[affiliation-pikultzumaq|Pik'ul Tz'umaq]] for the fallen celestial owl behind it.
