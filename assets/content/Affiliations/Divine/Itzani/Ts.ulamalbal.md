# terran_analog: the Mesoamerican ball game as rite and as the settling of disputes; the order, its offices and the rules of a suit are invented, and the name joins ts'ulam (to play) to -al and -bal

## The Keepers of the Court

Beside the solar pyramid of every Itsani city lies a long, narrow alley of white plaster between two sloping walls, with a marker stone set at each end and a third in the middle, cut with the glyph of the sun. On a festival day the court's own players fill it with noise: a heavy ball of black tree-gum, struck with hip and thigh and forearm and never with the hand, goes up and down the alley while the city watches from the walls. On another day the same court is silent except for the ball, and two lord-houses are watching from opposite walls, because the game is deciding which of them owns a valley.

The **Keepers of the Court**, _Ts'ulamalbal_ in Ki'i'chek, those who serve the court, are the priests who hold these courts. The game is a rite, a lawsuit and a sacrifice at once, and the Keepers keep all three. Every Keeper is an ordained priest of [[affiliation-tzenultqan|Faith of Ts'en'ul Tq'an]], whose sun crosses the sky as the ball crosses the court, and whose order the court upholds.

## The Suit

A dispute that would otherwise be fought is played. That is the court's whole purpose, and the rules of a suit are short.

**Who may bring a case.** A lord-house against a lord-house, a ward against a ward, a craft ward or a merchant family against another, and a city against a city through its Ts'enmah. The case is over something the two sides could go to war for: land, water, a boundary stone, tribute owed, a broken marriage contract or a debt. A priest may not bring one, because quarrels within the priesthood go to the Q'emul Tq'an of [[affiliation-itsanihierarchy|the Itsani Hierarchy]]. A slave may not bring one, and a commoner's case against a lord goes to the city's judges, not to the court.

**How it is brought.** The side bringing the case comes to the Keeper of a court, gives blood at the middle stone, and pays the court's fee in cacao. The Keeper may refuse a case he finds frivolous, or one that touches the priesthood. If he accepts, a road priest of [[affiliation-xilanixlan|Faith of Shilan Ish'lan]] carries the challenge to the other side, which may settle before the day or play. A side that has neither settled nor come onto the court by the third day the Keeper names has lost. A suit between two cities is never played in either city. The **First Keeper** names a third city's court, and the sides learn which only when the challenge has been accepted.

**How the game decides it.** The Keeper fixes the day by the city's tables, on a day the sun's priests hold to be open, and the count the winning side must reach. Each side fields seven players, its own or hired. A point falls when the ball crosses the other side's end of the alley or strikes the other side's marker stone, and a **Caller of the Count** calls each one aloud. The Keeper rules on every disputed stroke from the middle of the wall, and his ruling ends the dispute. The side that reaches the count first has won the case. No appeal lies from the court, because the case has been put to the god and he has answered it.

**What binds the loser.** The Keeper records the judgment, and the **Keeper of the Judgment Stones** cuts it into the court's walls: the two sides, the thing at issue and what the loser owes. A cord with the same judgment goes by [[affiliation-chumishlan|Ch'um Ish'lan]], the Speaking Road, to the Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an of each side's city. The loser has until the close of the twenty-day month to pay. A judgment unpaid at the month's close is entered by the Warden of the Debt against the losing side's account as blood owed, and the temple collects it as it collects every debt of blood. A house that still refuses is **Denied the Court**: no court in any city hears a case it brings or lets it answer one. Every neighbor of a denied house is then free to take what he claims from it, because it can no longer play to keep it, and almost every house pays long before this.

**A suit of blood.** Where a killing lies between the sides, the case is a suit of blood. The captain of each side vows himself before the game, and when the count is reached the captain of the losing side is given to the Dedicator of Captives and goes to the altar at the next rite. The killing is then paid, and the two sides may not raise it again in war or in court.

## Membership and Standing

A Keeper is chosen by his city's Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an from among the sun's priests who have played. Most were players of the court in their youth, and many were knights of the captive-hunts first. The court's own players are commoners and younger sons who play well enough to be offered to the god on festival days, and the best of them are hired by the sides in a suit as champions, often for more cacao than a craftsman earns in a year. The captive-takers of [[affiliation-takingsocs|the Taking Societies]] are the most sought-after champions of all, and the Keepers allow a side to hire them, as long as it hires no more than three.

The Keepers stand high in their cities, above the city's judges in any matter the court has taken up, and below the Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an in everything. A Keeper found to have taken a gift from a side, or a player who sold a stroke, is **Struck from the Court**, and every judgment he played or kept is opened and played again.

## Rivals

**The judges of the cities.** Every case the court takes is a case the lord's hall does not hear. The judges hold that a suit over a debt belongs in their courts, where witnesses can be heard, and the lords prefer the ball, where they cannot be.

**The road priests.** A dispute between two cities is commonly opened by a road priest of Shilan Ish'lan, who carries terms between them, and a dispute settled on the road is one the court never plays. The Keepers hold that a road priest who settles a suit has taken its fee, and the road priests answer that they have saved the sides the price of seven champions and perhaps a captain.

## What a Party Can Ask of Them

- **Carry or answer a challenge.** A party in a house's service may be sent to bring a case before a Keeper, or to learn which court the First Keeper has named before the other side does.
- **Play.** A side hires champions where it finds them. A party that can play, or a warrior who can learn quickly, may be hired onto a court, and the Keeper does not ask where a champion came from.
- **Find a sold stroke.** A side that has lost and believes a player or a Keeper was paid may ask a party to prove it. A proved charge opens the judgment, and a false one is an insult to the god's answer.
- **Read the stones.** The judgment stones of an old court record what every house in the city has lost and owes, and a Keeper of the Judgment Stones reads them aloud for a gift.
