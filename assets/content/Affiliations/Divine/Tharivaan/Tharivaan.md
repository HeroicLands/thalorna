## Thári'vaan: The Living Whole

In the hour before sunrise, at every sanctuary in the interior of the [[place-klhrcntnnt|Kalihara Continent]] and at hundreds of lesser sites, a custodian walks the perimeter of a gray structure older than any kingdom, speaks a sequence of words whose meaning is half lost, and lays a hand on its surface in an order fixed eighty centuries ago. It is done in every weather, every day, and it is the nearest thing to a rite that the island has. Thári'vaan, the Living Whole, is the tradition of the Kaliharan people, and it is not a religion: it has no gods, no deities who answer prayers, grant blessings or demand sacrifice, no doctrine of an afterlife, and no reward or punishment beyond death. It has instead an understanding of the world, three habits every adult keeps, and a custodianship, the **[Ithakan]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"}** ("Keepers of What Was Left"), that keeps the works left behind by [[lore-theithari|The Ithari]].

The understanding is that the world is one living whole, and the Kaliharans hold it as observed fact. The canopy and the reef, the volcanic soil and the monsoon, the household and the birds that nest in its gardens are parts of one thing, and the health of each answers to the health of all; several thousand years of close attention to a single island have confirmed it to their satisfaction, and the evidence a custodian offers is the rainfall records. What follows is a reverence without worship: a deep respect for the world as it is, with an obligation attached to keep its balance. Kaliharans do not pray; they observe. They do not make offerings; they tend.

### The Three Observances

Kaliharan practice, as taught to children from the earliest age, is organized around three foundational observances that the Ithakan describe as the minimum discipline by which any adult participates in Thári'vaan.

The first is **[sílhari]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}**—roughly "clear looking." Every Kaliharan is expected to spend some portion of each day in attentive observation of the world: a few minutes at dawn watching the movement of insects in a garden, an hour at dusk tracking the return of a particular flock of birds, the [[being-dsksft|Dusk Swift]] most often, a full day once a season surveying a stretch of forest or coastline for changes since the last visit. The purpose is not scientific cataloguing in the Ankarian sense but the cultivation of the habit of noticing—of maintaining a working familiarity with one's particular corner of the whole, so that imbalance, when it begins, is seen early enough to be corrected.

The second is **[vaan'ari]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}**—"right tending." Every Kaliharan is responsible for some small portion of the world. For most, this is the household garden, the family orchard, the stretch of stream or coastline closest to home. For others, it is a specific grove, a particular reef, a hillside terrace, honeycombed by its [[being-trrcml|Terrace Moles]], worked by a single lineage for thirty generations. The Ithakan hold vaan'ari responsibilities over the ancient structures themselves, but the principle is identical: to tend what is yours to tend, at the appropriate intensity, neither neglecting it nor over-working it. A Kaliharan who takes no vaan'ari responsibility is regarded with the same quiet concern that an Ankarian community might extend to someone who refuses to wash or eat.

The third is **[kaan'vaar]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}**—"holding what was left." This is the observance most closely associated with the Ithakan, but in diluted form it is practiced by every Kaliharan. It consists of the preservation—through exact repetition, through careful teaching of the next generation, through refusal of improvisation in matters of significance—of inherited practice. A Kaliharan farmer plants according to patterns that their grandmother's grandmother planted. A Kaliharan fisher sets nets at depths and in sequences that have not changed in living memory. The underlying theory is simple: if the balance has held for ten thousand years, the practices that produced it are not to be lightly altered. Change is not forbidden—the Kaliharans are not fools—but it is treated as a weighty decision, to be undertaken only when the old way has demonstrably failed. The observance and the custodian who lives it share one word: a kaan'vaar of the Ithakan is the man or woman who has made kaan'vaar a life's work, and the Kaliharans hear no ambiguity in calling both by the same name.

### The Ithakan

The custodial priesthood, the **Ithakan** ("Keepers of What Was Left"), forms the spiritual and institutional heart of Kaliharan society. Their role is to preserve the knowledge and practices of the Ithari precursors, to maintain the ancient structures, and to keep the balance of Thári'vaan from being disrupted.

Over eighty centuries, the original understanding of what they are maintaining has inevitably shifted. Procedures that the Ithari may have understood as practical engineering—the tending of specific structures, the performance of specific sequences of action at specific times—have acquired layers of ritual meaning. The Ithakan perform these maintenance liturgies to the letter, believing them essential to the health of the island, even as the original technical purpose has been forgotten or transformed into something closer to ceremony.

#### Ranks and Formation

The Ithakan are organized into four ranks, each defined less by hierarchy than by the kind of responsibility held, and a custodian of any of the four stands at the third level of the island's order, that of Ithakan.

A **kaan'sili**—"one who watches"—is the entry rank, taken by young Kaliharans selected for custodial formation between the ages of eight and twelve because a work answers them, and taken whatever their lineage. Kaan'sili spend fifteen to twenty years in apprenticeship, living at one of the interior sanctuaries and absorbing the accumulated knowledge of ecology, agriculture, liturgical procedure, [[lore-itharisul|Ithasul]] maintenance, and the specific patterns of their assigned region. They do not perform the great liturgies themselves; they assist, observe, and commit to memory the sequences they will one day execute.

A **kaan'vaar**—"one who holds"—is the working rank, and the one that most Kaliharans encounter in daily life. A kaan'vaar has completed formation, performs the maintenance liturgies at a specific sanctuary or for a specific stretch of structures, and carries the responsibility for teaching the next generation of kaan'sili. The rank is taken for life; retirement is not recognized, though an aged kaan'vaar may have their practical duties reduced as their strength fails.

A **kaan'thari**—"one who keeps what came before"—is the senior rank, reached after forty or fifty years of service. Kaan'thari are the memory of the tradition: the Kaliharans to whom other Kaliharans turn when a practice has drifted, when a procedure has been half-forgotten, when a structure has begun to fail in a way that no living kaan'vaar has seen before. There are perhaps two hundred kaan'thari in Kalihara at any time, scattered across the major sanctuaries, and their counsel is sought on matters well beyond the strictly custodial.

A **silkan**—"clear-seer of the keepers"—is the rarest rank, held by perhaps a dozen individuals at any time. These are Kaliharans of extraordinary discernment and encyclopedic knowledge. They coordinate custodial practice across the whole island, they convene the councils that decide when an ancient procedure must be adjusted in the face of structural failure, and they carry, in strict confidence, the knowledge of how deep the crisis of the failing structures has become.

#### The Sanctuaries

Ithakan formation and practice are anchored at a small number of interior sanctuaries, each associated with a particular cluster of ithasul structures and with a particular branch of custodial knowledge. The Sanctuary of **[[place-veshvaan|Veshvaan]]** in [[place-klhrbowl|The Great Bowl]] is the oldest and largest, housing the most extensive archives of inherited procedure; the Sanctuary of **[[place-thalari|Thal'ari]]** on the western slopes is the formation-center for custodians whose vaan'ari will concern coastal and oceanic structures; the Sanctuary of **[[place-mirthaan|Mirthaan]]** in the eastern river valleys trains custodians of the agricultural installations. There are perhaps two dozen sanctuaries in all, each functioning as a kind of combined seminary, archive, workshop, and working site. Foreigners have never been permitted to visit any of them.

#### The Sílhari Circle

[[affiliation-silharicircle|The Sílhari Circle]] is a fellowship of custodians, with a few Elders of lineages among them, named for the first observance, clear looking. Its members hold that kaan'vaar has failed: the procedures alone will not keep the works, and the principles behind them must be sought, abroad if the island cannot supply them. What the circle wants follows from that: the slabs whose procedures have been lost read, someone found who can read them, and, if it comes to that, one outsider let in. The rest of the Ithakan hold to the tending and to the procedures as they were handed down.

#### The Custodians and the Island's Bodies

A custodian of any rank belongs to the Ithakan before any lineage, and stands at the third level of the island's order. Custodians sit in [[affiliation-councilisland|The Council of the Island]] for the works in their charge; the Council decides what the procedures may become, and the silkan convene the council that settles the question. Custodians formed at Thal'ari tend the current-stones on which [[affiliation-wardensapproaches|The Wardens of the Approaches]] and their pilots depend, and those formed at Mirthaan tend the wind-stones and the works of the terraces. The custodians of dark works, whose works have gone white, serve at [[place-kuomethei|Kuomethei]] in [[affiliation-keepershouse|The Keeper's House]].

### The Answering {#the-answering}

The works answer people, and not by rank. A Kaliharan whom a work answers feels the state of that one work, near it or a long way from it—whether it is working, working badly, stopped or dead—and nothing more: the work does nothing for that person, lends nothing and tells nothing of any other work. Nobody on the island has found the rule of it. Blood does not decide it, nor office, nor merit, so a bondservant may be answered by [[place-sulvaan|The Warm]] and the Elder of a lineage by nothing at all. The answering is set out as an ability in [[mysticalability-hearingstone|Hearing the Stone]].

Tending stands apart from it. Every tending-act is a trained procedure; any trained custodian performs it whether a work answers that custodian or not, and a work accepts a procedure correctly performed from anyone. Being answered makes nobody a better custodian. The custodians take their kaan'sili from the children a work answers, whatever their lineage, so the Ithakan is the one body on the island a bondservant's child can rise in. The Warden of the Approaches chooses pilots the same way, from those the current-stones answer.

The archive holds that in the first generations after the Ithari the offices went to the answered. The offices have since become hereditary in the lineages and the answering has not followed the blood, so the island's order of standing and the map of who is answered do not agree. Every Kaliharan knows it and none says it, because kaan'vaar forbids the thought that the order might be adjusted. An answered bondservant is held in service to a lineage whose Elder must still send for him when the lineage's stone drifts.

The failing scrambles who is answered. A dead work answers nobody, and those it answered feel it gho: they are not Severed, the island's lowest standing, and they are treated as though they were, so a village whose stone has gone white can be a whole village of the unspoken. A work working badly answers new people, by no rule anyone has found. A fisher's child at [[place-reithovani|Reithovani]] is answered by [[place-telvaristone|The Telvari Stone]], and a dock-hand at [[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]] by a current-stone he has never seen. The order of standing does not move; the people do.

### Sacred Objects

Thári'vaan has no idols, no devotional images, no reliquaries in the Ankarian sense. What it has instead is a small and carefully maintained set of objects that the Ithakan regard as load-bearing—objects through which the practice of custodianship is organized and transmitted.

**Ithasul**—"precursor stone"—is the most important of these, and every piece of it in a sanctuary's keeping comes from a structure that has died: a living work cannot be cut, broken or taken from at all, and only a failed one gives. A piece taken from a dead work is a dead **held stone**, and every sanctuary keeps at least one; the living held stones, each belonging to a working work, stay with the lineages that hold their works until the work dies, and then the lineage gives its stone, dead from that day, up to a sanctuary. Custodians still call it a fragment as often as not—the older word, from a time before anyone thought about what it implied, and inaccurate now that it is understood that nothing is ever broken from anything living. The two words mean the same thing. A dead held stone is bone-white and hard, never the uniform gray of a living work and with none of its give—the difference is plain in the hand, which is how a custodian knows one at once. Held stones are kept as reference samples for the study of what becomes of the substance after a work dies. Nothing touches living ithasul—no tool, no fire, no working of any tradition, and not time either, which leaves no mark of age on it at all—so everything a sanctuary records is a dead work's and no other: the whitening, the hardening, and the cracks that come to some whitened works and not to others. They serve also as meditative focuses for new kaan'sili, who learn by feel to tell dead stone from living, a held stone in the hand against the structures they will spend their lives tending. Intact ithasul structures themselves are not "sacred" in the sense of being set apart from daily life; they are integrated into it, tended like an orchard or a field. But a held stone, carried from the place where its parent structure failed, becomes a kind of testament—a reminder that what was given is finite, and that the custodians have their work cut out for them.

**The Kaan'sari'val**—"the keepers' threads"—are cords of plaited palm fiber, dyed with specific pigments and knotted according to specific patterns, that each Ithakan receives at their elevation to kaan'vaar. The pattern encodes the custodian's formation lineage: the name of the teacher, and the teacher's teacher, back through the generations to one of the founding custodians whose name is preserved in the original oral record. A kaan'vaar wears their thread at all times, and it is buried with them.

**Sílhari manuals** are the small leather-bound observation journals kept by every Kaliharan, custodian or not. A child is given their first at age six or seven and keeps it for life; when filled, it is stored in the household archive. The contents are almost entirely naturalistic—dates of flowering, counts of particular birds, records of weather—but the practice itself is understood as sacred: the daily act of recording what one has seen is one of the principal ways a Kaliharan participates in Thári'vaan.

**The first records** are carved stone slabs and inscribed metal plaques held in the deepest archives of the interior sanctuaries. They are Kaliharan work and always were—ordinary stone and ordinary metal, cut by the people who were taught, setting down what they had been given. Nothing of the Ithari's own making bears writing, and nothing could: the substance takes no mark. Only silkan and the most senior kaan'thari have access to the slabs. Most carry procedural instructions for the maintenance of specific structures; a minority are philosophical or historical, and much of both is now unreadable, the hand being the oldest Kaliharan writing there is and the language having moved a long way from it across eighty centuries.

### Ceremonies and Maintenance Liturgies

Kaliharans do not celebrate ceremonies in the festive, communal sense that characterizes Ankarian religious life. What the Ithakan call **sari'kaan**—"the tending-acts"—are closer to what an Ankarian engineer might recognize as scheduled maintenance, performed with the solemnity that the passage of eighty centuries has invested in procedures whose original technical purpose is not understood.

**[The Dawn Tending]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}** is the most commonly observed. At every sanctuary, and at many ithasul sites throughout the interior, a kaan'vaar or kaan'sili walks the perimeter of their assigned structures in the hour before sunrise, speaking a sequence of words in an archaic form of Kalihari whose exact meaning is partially lost, and laying a hand briefly on specific surfaces in a specific order. The whole sequence takes between a quarter-hour and two hours depending on the installation. It is performed without exception, in every weather, every day.

**[The Seasonal Recitations]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}** are longer liturgies performed four times a year at the cardinal turns of the monsoon cycle. These involve the gathering of all the custodians assigned to a particular structure, the re-speaking of the full maintenance sequence for that installation in front of witnesses, and the deliberate re-transcription—onto fresh palm-leaf sheets—of the procedure from memory, so that any drift in the sequence is caught and corrected against the archived record.

**[The Tenth-Year Walk]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}** is performed at each sanctuary once every decade. Senior custodians walk the full circuit of the region's ithasul structures, in a sequence established in the oldest records, performing an extended tending at each. The walk takes between three weeks and three months depending on the region, and serves both as an inspection—occasions for the silkan to survey the actual state of the installations—and as a reaffirmation of the custodial lineage: the kaan'sili who carry the elders' tools along the route are thereby introduced to every structure they may, in time, come to tend.

**[The Rite of Last Tending]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}** is the most quietly weighted ceremony the Ithakan perform. It is the last office for a work that is already dead, and it cannot be performed on any other. When a structure is judged to have failed beyond recovery—when the procedures produce no observable effect, when the gray has gone bone-white and hard, when cracks have opened in it—the senior custodians of the region assemble to perform one final tending, take a single held stone from the installation to carry back to the sanctuary, and record the failure in the master archive. The taking is possible only because the work has died: no custodian has ever removed so much as a chip from a living structure, and none has ever tried. No mourning is permitted; the Ithakan hold that grief for a structure is grief misdirected, and that the proper response to loss is renewed attention to what remains. In the last three generations, the Rite of Last Tending has been performed more often than in the previous three centuries combined, and the silkan who carry the count of failures speak of this to no one outside their rank.

### The Reckoning by Walks

The island counts its years in Walks. Each sanctuary keeps the count of its own Tenth-Year Walks, and the count of Walks since the departure of the Ithari is kept at Veshvaan. A Kaliharan dates a thing to the year of a Walk or to so many years after one, so a spring that failed two Walks ago failed some twenty years back.

### Seasonal Observances

The island's year is marked by four turnings of the monsoon, and each is the occasion for a quiet, widely observed rhythm rather than a festival in the Ankarian sense.

**[Dry-Turn]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}** (the end of the wet season, roughly the fourth month of the Ankarian calendar) is the time of the first Seasonal Recitation. Households clean their thresholds, replace worn sílhari manuals, and in the evenings gather to review the rainfall records of the year just ended. Children are presented with their first manual at the age of six or seven during this turn.

**[Stillwater]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}** (the mid-dry month, the height of the sun's strength) is the occasion for the Tenth-Year Walks when the cycle falls in that year, and for the long survey-days when it does not. Kaliharans spend as much of the daylight hours as possible in silent observation of particular places—a practice that outsiders have sometimes mistaken for meditation, but which the Kaliharans describe in strictly practical terms as "looking properly while the light is good."

**[Wet-Turn]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}** (the return of the monsoons, the seventh month or thereabouts) is marked by the Second Seasonal Recitation and by a round of household visits: every Kaliharan spends the week after the first rain visiting the households whose vaan'ari borders their own, to compare observations and coordinate the season's work. This is one of the few times of year when ordinary Kaliharans have routine contact with the Ithakan, who visit the households in rotation as part of their teaching responsibilities.

**[Deepwater]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}** (the heart of the wet season, late in the year) is the most inward turning, when travel in the interior is impractical and households keep to their own thresholds. The Ithakan perform the Dawn Tendings in the rain without variation; the laity spend the time on archival work—copying worn sílhari manuals, teaching children the names of plants, reviewing the records of the lineage.

### Relationship to Other Faiths

Thári'vaan has no counterpart in any other Thalorna tradition. It is not a pantheon, not animism, and not ancestor worship. Ankarian scholars have struggled to categorize it, variously labeling it a philosophy, a nature cult, or a form of ancestor veneration—none of which accurately captures what it is. The Kaliharans themselves are sanguine about the confusion. They do not seek converts, they do not debate doctrine, and they regard the entire question of comparative religion as a distraction from the work of tending.

The port cities of Kalihara (notably Ilovanu, [[place-vashuran|Vashurán]], [[place-nalurithan|Nalurithan]], and [[place-telvari|Telvari]]) host [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian]] temples serving foreign merchants and the ports' mixed populations, but the interior follows Thári'vaan exclusively. Kaliharan merchants who have spent their adult lives in the ports occasionally take up Aureldian devotion in some form, and this is tolerated without comment by the interior; such people are regarded as already living in a different world, and the Ithakan see no profit in chastening them.

The [[lore-flksinale|Sinale Folk]] who first sought settlement on Kalihara, and were refused it, reportedly recognized something familiar in Thári'vaan, though the nature of that recognition has never been recorded in any Kaliharan source. Whatever the Sinale saw, it did not persuade the Ithakan to open their door any wider.
