The Faith of Morvana teaches that [[lore-morvanadty|Morvana]], goddess of decay, endings, and transformation, oversees the natural cycle of death and renewal, reminding mortals of life's impermanence.

## Worship

The worship of Morvana is solemn and contemplative, characterized by acknowledgment rather than celebration, respect rather than exultation. Her temples are built in autumnal colors—deep golds, burnished reds, and blacks—and are adorned with symbols of endings: fallen leaves, withered flowers, empty branches, and the images of creatures in their final seasons. Where temples of other deities often feature bright light and open spaces, Morvana's sanctuaries are deliberately shadowed and introspective, places where one encounters the reality of impermanence and accepts it with grace.

The greatest festival honoring Morvana occurs in late autumn as the year wanes and the land begins its retreat into winter's sleep. The [Festival of the Waning Moon]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} lasts seven days and is marked not by celebration but by remembrance and acceptance. The faithful process through cities bearing candles of deepening darkness—bright on the first day, dimmer each subsequent day, until the last night is kept in near-total darkness. Families create altars to deceased relatives and loved ones, speaking their names and recounting their deeds, understanding that as they themselves will one day be remembered and then forgotten, so too is the cycle just and natural. In hospices and places of healing, Morvana's priestesses perform special rites for the dying, affirming that their passage is sacred and that their decay returns them to the earth that fed them.

Individual worship of Morvana is most often private and personal, undertaken by those confronting loss, aging, or mortality. The sick in their final days receive visits from priestesses of the goddess, who bring comfort and perspective rather than false hope. Those grieving may come to Morvana's temples to pour out their sorrow before her altar, where they are heard and acknowledged but not comforted with false promises of reunion. Philosophers and scholars often maintain personal devotion to Morvana, honoring the goddess as one who teaches the deepest truths about existence.

Offerings to Morvana are the gifts of decay itself: autumn leaves, withering flowers, fruit past ripeness, wine that has begun to turn, grain that has molded. The symbolism is clear—these are offerings made not of what is valued but of what must be let gho, life returned willingly to the cycle. Some nobles maintain Mórvanic shrines in family crypts, making annual offerings and speaking their ancestors' names, asking Morvana to give them rest and to grant their own eventual passing the dignity they showed to those before them.

## Organization

The priesthood of Morvana is led by the **Valmena**, the High Priestess or High Priest. This figure is typically an older priestess who has spent decades in service to the goddess, often someone who has survived personal losses and has transformed grief into compassionate service to others. The Valmena is a counselor to the dying, a teacher of the faith, and an interpreter of the goddess's will.

Below the Valmena stand the **Sarnori of Twilight**, ordained priests who serve as healers for the terminally ill, counselors for the bereaved, and keepers of Morvana's temples. These priests are chosen for their capacity for empathy, their comfort with mortality, and their ability to speak honestly about death without cruelty. They are trained in the care of the dying, in funeral rites, and in the pastoral work of comforting those who grieve. The finest Sarnori of Twilight are renowned for their ability to help the dying achieve peace, to help families accept loss, and to transform grief into something that can be borne.

The third rank consists of **Acolytes of Decay**, young initiates who train in the practical care of the dying, in the preparation of bodies for funeral rites, and in the theological understanding of Morvana's place within the cosmic order. These acolytes often serve for years in hospices and places of death before advancement to Sarnor status is even considered. Many never advance to full ordination, instead dedicating their lives as nurses and caregivers while maintaining their connection to Morvana's priesthood.

The priesthood operates a significant network of **Hospices of the Waning Moon**, institutions dedicated to the care of the dying that are found in every major city. These establishments employ priests and servants, provide shelter and comfort to those in their final days, and perform the funeral rites that prepare the dead for their passage. These hospices are supported by donations from grateful families, by endowments left by the dying themselves, and sometimes by direct patronage from nobility who understand that Morvana's blessings ensure that they, too, will one day die with dignity.

## Relations

Morvana maintains a partnership with [[affiliation-selmoros|Sélmoros]], together forming the theological understanding of death among the Aureldian faiths. In their theology, where [[lore-selmorosdty|Sélmoros]] represents the absolute, implacable fact of death itself—the silent judge who cannot be negotiated with—Morvana represents the process of decay, the gentle fading, the natural return to the earth. Sélmoros is the gate; Morvana is the passage through it. The priesthoods often work together, with Sélmoran priests invoking Morvana's acceptance as they guide souls toward Sélmoros's realm.

The relationship with [[affiliation-lusinia|Lusinia]] is acknowledged with quiet acceptance rather than tension. Lusinia teaches creation and growth; Morvana’s faith teaches endings and return. In their theology, the two goddesses represent the complete cycle: what [[lore-lusiniadty|Lusinia]] causes to grow, Morvana eventually brings to compost. The two faiths respect each other's role, understanding that neither is complete without the other. Some temples maintain joint altars where both goddesses are honored, with Lusinia's fresh flowers placed beside Morvana's withered ones, symbolizing the eternal wheel.

Morvana's relationship with [[affiliation-lizelas|Lizelas]] is one of gentle contrast. In their teaching, [[lore-lizelasdty|Lizelas]] celebrates fertility, joy, and abundance; Morvana reminds mortals that all such abundance must eventually wane. Where Lizelas brings celebration, Morvana brings perspective. The priesthoods do not conflict; instead, they teach complementary truths—enjoy the feast because it will not last forever; accept the ending because you have had the feast.

The relationship with [[affiliation-karnavos|Karnavos]] is somewhat strained, for in their theology [[lore-karnavosdty|Karnavos]], the chaos god, represents the violent, unexpected disruption of life's plans, while Morvana represents the natural, expected conclusion of things. However, both understand transformation—Karnavos through violent upheaval, Morvana through gentle decay. Some theologians suggest that together they represent transformation itself: Karnavos the sudden shock that tears down what was, Morvana the gradual return to earth that follows.

With the [[affiliation-zunelas|Faith of Zunelas]], Morvana’s faith shares a subtle affinity. That faith honors [[lore-zunelasdty|Zunelas]], the dreamer-goddess. Death is sometimes described as the greatest dream, and Zunelas's priestesses sometimes work alongside Morvana's to help the dying achieve peace through visions and dreams. In their theology, the two goddesses offer comfort in transitions that waking mortals struggle to accept.
