Goddess of dreams, celestial visions, and the veil between worlds. Aethería brings divine inspiration and omens to mortals through their dreams. She is patron to seers, oracles, healers of the mind, and all who seek truth in the world beyond waking.

"Aethería" is the Aurèldían name for [[lore-goddreams|The God of Dreams]]—a deity far older than any human pantheon, worshipped by the [[lore-flksinale|Sinalë]] and [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazári]] since before humanity existed. The Asguardian peoples know the same deity as [[affiliation-baldr|Baldr]].

## Worship

The worship of Aethería is contemplative and private, centered on dreams, visions, and states of profound rest. Her temples are designed as sanctuaries of silence and shadow—spaces intentionally constructed to encourage sleep, meditation, and the vivid dreaming that brings divine insight. Temple chambers are draped in soft fabrics in shades of midnight and silver; the air is perfumed with herbs that encourage sleep; the very architecture seems designed to quiet the waking mind. Unlike the processional grandeur of temples honoring other deities, Aethería's sanctuaries are intimate spaces where the faithful come to rest, to dream, and to seek communion with the goddess through visions.

The priesthood maintains the temple as a place of healing sleep for the sick, the injured, and the spiritually troubled. Those suffering from wasting illnesses, deep wounds, or psychological torment may spend nights or even weeks in Aethería's temple, cared for by priestesses while they rest and dream under the goddess's protection. Such rest is understood as more healing than any mundane remedy—for Aethería can mend what waking physicians cannot touch.

Oracular shrines dedicated to Aethería exist in major cities, where the faithful may come to seek divine wisdom through dreams. A supplicant comes to the shrine with a question or concern, sleeps within the temple's sacred precinct after performing purification rituals, and awakens with dreams that often contain divine guidance. The priestess-oracles interpret these dreams, helping the faithful understand the goddess's counsel. Some shrines are renowned across kingdoms for the accuracy of their omens—the Oracular Sanctuary at Argenna in Provènzia attracts pilgrims from across the continent who trust that dreams received there come from the goddess herself.

There is no great public festival of Aethería in the conventional sense, but rather a gentle, widespread celebration during the darkest nights of winter when the veil between waking and sleeping grows thin. During these nights, the faithful deliberately spend extended time in sleep and dreams, while temple priestesses maintain vigils, tending the sacred flames that keep the temples luminous through the dark hours. Many families sleep together in their homes' most comfortable chambers during these nights, understanding their rest as a shared benediction from Aethería.

Offerings to Aethería are subtle and personal—a lock of hair bound with silver thread, a small stone from a beloved's grave, a cup of milk left before sleep, a written prayer sealed and buried beneath a sacred tree. Aethería accepts gifts from the dreaming mind and the unconscious heart.

## Organization

The priesthood of Aethería is led by the **Somniatrix**, the High Priestess or High Priest—a title used regardless of gender. This figure is typically an elderly priestess of legendary visionary power, someone whose dreams have guided kingdoms and whose wisdom is sought by monarchs and councils. The Somniatrix serves as the chief interpreter of the goddess's will and as a teacher and mentor to younger priests.

Below the Somniatrix stand the **Oraculi**, ordained priestesses (and occasionally priests) who serve as dream-healers and interpreters of divine vision. The Oraculi are trained for years in the techniques of lucid dreaming, in the interpretation of symbols and omens, and in the pastoral care of those seeking Aethería's guidance. They maintain the temple sanctuaries, oversee ritual purifications, and guide supplicants through the process of seeking divine dreams.

The third rank consists of **Noctaries**, acolytes and servants of the night, young initiates who maintain the temples through the hours of darkness, tend the sacred lamps, prepare the sleeping chambers, and assist the Oraculi in their ministry. Training as a Noctary typically involves a long apprenticeship—often five to seven years—during which the initiate learns to enter and maintain deep meditation, to recognize and record their own dreams, and to understand the symbolic language through which the goddess speaks.

The priesthood maintains a significant body of written knowledge: dream journals maintained by priests across centuries, interpretive texts explaining the meaning of common symbols and visions, and ritual manuals for inducing states of lucid dreaming. These texts are kept in temple libraries, and scholars sometimes spend months in residence at major temples, studying the extensive archives of recorded dreams and their interpretations.

A distinctive institution within Aethería's faith is the **House of Sleep**, major temple complexes dedicated specifically to the healing of the afflicted through sacred rest. These establishments employ dozens of priestesses and servants, and they often serve the dual purpose of temple and hospital. They are supported by donations from grateful patients, by the sale of dream-interpretation services to wealthy clients, and sometimes by direct patronage from nobility.

## Relations

Aethería is universally respected within the Aurèldían pantheon, for she represents a realm that transcends the everyday conflicts of waking deities. Nearly all the other faiths acknowledge her wisdom and occasionally consult with her priesthood on matters of great importance. Her role is often that of the quiet advisor, the voice of deeper wisdom speaking from dreams and visions when mortal councils are deadlocked.

The relationship with [[affiliation-janus|Jánus]] is one of complementary opposition. Jánus demands rigid order and clear contracts; Aethería offers wisdom through the ambiguous, symbolic language of dreams. Yet they serve complementary roles: Jánus brings clarity to waking disputes, while Aethería provides insight into deeper truths that lie beneath conscious debate. Some theologians suggest that Jánus and Aethería together represent the complete wisdom—justice balanced with mercy, order balanced with intuition.

Aethería's relationship with [[affiliation-menerva|Ménérva]] is one of deep respect and frequent cooperation. Both goddesses value knowledge and wisdom, but Ménérva pursues it through conscious study and rational thought while Aethería reveals it through dreams, visions, and the unconscious mind. Priesthoods of the two faiths often coordinate: libraries of Ménérva sometimes employ priests of Aethería to interpret the deeper symbolic meanings of texts; oracular shrines of Aethería sometimes consult with scholars of Ménérva to understand what the goddess's messages mean.

With [[affiliation-morvana|Mórváná]], Aethería shares an understanding of transitions and transformations. Death itself is sometimes described as a kind of sleep from which there is no waking, and Aethería's priesthood sometimes works with Mórváná's priests to comfort the dying, offering visions of peace and rest rather than fear of the end.

The relationship with [[affiliation-karnavos|Karnavos]] is strained but not hostile. Karnavos represents chaos and wild disruption in the waking world; Aethería represents the chaos of dreams and the unconscious mind. While related, they are fundamentally different—Karnavos seeks to shatter civilization itself, while Aethería merely reminds mortals that there are truths beyond rational order. Some renegade priests of Karnavos have attempted to corrupt worship of Aethería into a cult of dangerous ecstasy, but the mainstream priesthood regards such efforts as perversion of the goddess's true nature.
