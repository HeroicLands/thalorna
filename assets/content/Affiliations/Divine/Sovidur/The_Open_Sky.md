The Open Sky is the old faith of the [[lore-sowidesclt|Sovidur]], the herding tribes of the [[place-khzryndsrtrgn|Khazryn Desert Region]]: a sky-father above, an earth-mother below, the shamans who stand between them, and the dead laid out with their faces upward so that the sky can find them. Nobody joins it. A child of the steppe belongs to the Open Sky as it belongs to its tribe, by being born under it, and the faith has no temple, no book and no priest who answers to anyone beyond the tribe's own wells.

Most riders call it nothing at all. "The Open Sky" is the caravan masters' name for it, after the phrase they hear at every bargain they strike on the steppe: sworn under open sky.

The tribes honor [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|the Twelve of the Flame]] as well, loosely and on top of the older faith. A camp that takes in a caravan priest of the twelve gives his fire a place and his gods a word, and goes back to its own shaman when the grass fails or a child is dying. Two of the twelve matter on the steppe above the rest, and the shamans account for both in the old faith's terms. [[lore-bazhramisdty|Bazhrámiš]], the storm and the protector, is the sky-father's storm by another name. [[lore-khorsadradty|Khórsadra]], the eternal sky-light, is the light he sees by.

## The Sky-Father and the Earth-Mother

The sky-father is everything above the ground: the sun and the stars, the wind, the rain that decides the year's grass, and the lightning. The shamans call him [Evurakhaq]{.name data-type="lore/deity"}, sky-father, and most riders say only "the sky", as they say "the dovaq" of a chief everyone knows. He sees the whole steppe at once, and the tribes hold that nothing done under him is done in secret. That is why a bargain is struck outdoors, why a dovaq is invested with the elders standing and no roof overhead, and why a treaty sworn before lightning is the strongest promise the steppe can make: the one who would punish its breaking is close enough to hear it.

The earth-mother is everything under the ground: the roots of the grass, the stone, the bones of the dead who were never laid out, and the water. The shamans call her [Jonkhiqekon]{.name data-type="lore/deity"}, earth-mother. Every well on the steppe is water she has given up, and a tribe holds a well as a son holds what his mother gave him, by keeping it. The tribes teach that she took the old lake of the sands into the ground and keeps it there. A drought is the earth-mother keeping her water, and fouling a well is the one wrong against her that no payment of horses ends.

Between them lies the living steppe and the spirits (_oqlaq_) that move in it: the spirits of the winds, of particular springs and stones, and of beasts, such as the [Jackal Spirit]{.name data-type="lore/spirit"} the [[affiliation-rqdrtrb|Oroqidur]] hold to be the protector of wanderers. A shaman deals with the spirits directly, and with the sky-father and the earth-mother only through them, by asking, reading and paying.

## The Shaman

A shaman (_zökhem_) is someone the sky has marked and the tribe has tested. The marks are few and plain: a rider struck by lightning who lives, a child who comes through a fever that killed the others in the tent, a herder whose dream of a storm arrives on the day he named. The tribe's shamans take in the one who is marked and teach what can be taught (the weather, the herbs, the words of the rites) for as long as it takes. Then the candidate goes out alone into the deep desert for forty days with very little water, and comes back or does not. [[being-rznshmrvrn|Razanash Mervaran]] of the [[affiliation-jbzrtrb|Jobizur]] went out at twenty-two, after a fever that nearly killed her, and is now the best-known healer and diviner in the south.

The one who comes back is a shaman for life, and the work has four parts:

- **The weather.** A shaman reads the sky for the dovaq and the moot before a camp moves or a raid rides. Where a tribe keeps several shamans, as the Jobizur do, they read as a circle, and the circle can halt a migration for an omen.
- **Healing.** A shaman treats people and beasts alike, with herbs, the knife and the rites, and is the one sent for when a camel sickens as often as when a child does.
- **The dead.** A shaman keeps the tribe's laying-out ground and lays out its dead.
- **The sky's hearing.** A shaman stands as witness when a tribe swears, watches the sky while the words are said, and says afterward whether the sky heard.

A shaman belongs to one tribe and answers to its dovaq. Shamans of different tribes meet at the markets, trade herbs and readings and argue about both, but there is no council of shamans on the steppe, and no shaman speaks for a tribe that is not his own.

A shaman goes bareheaded in every weather, sun and hail alike, because nothing is to stand between a shaman and the sky. The bare head is also a shaman's safety in a raid, since women, children and shamans are not killed. A man who bares his head to save himself and is found out has lied to the sky, and the tribe that finds him out does not lay him out when he dies.

## The Laying-Out and the Calling

The dead are carried to the tribe's laying-out ground (_yeloz_), a stretch of stony ground well away from any water, so that nothing of the dead reaches a well. There the shaman lays the body on its back, face to the sky, with nothing over it. The eldest of the dead person's kin then calls the name (_oqod_) to the four winds, one wind at a time, and the children of the family answer each wind. The vultures do the rest. The tribes hold that a soul whose name is called is taken up by the sky, and that a soul nobody names is not found by the sky-father and is found first by the hunters.

A body goes into the ground for one reason only, and the reason is blood. A grave is dug for the one who was killed unavenged, so that he stays where his kin can find him. An Elder Shaman digs it, and the dead man's spear is set upright in the mound. When the feud is ended, in horses or with a marriage, the kin open the grave, carry the bones to the laying-out ground and call the name at last. A spear standing alone in open steppe is an unpaid feud, and the tribe whose dead it marks watches the ground around it.

Where a great many died at once and nobody laid them out or named them, the ground is a kedok (blood-field), and the steppe holds that its dead are still waiting to be called. The steppe's own is [[place-wemaq|Vomek]], where the second orghun killed the tribe that refused his banner and forbade its laying-out. The [Kesukur]{.name data-type="affiliation/polity"} elders speak the names of the killed in their own camp, a day from the water and in the cold season, in [[lore-wemaqcalling|the Calling at Vomek]].

## Under Open Sky

A Sovidur swears by what is over him and what is under him. An ordinary oath (_yemok_) is said standing, outdoors, with one hand raised and the other turned open toward the ground, and its words are always the same in substance: the sky above me, the ground below me, and my word between them. A tribe's oath is the same words said by the dovaq before the moot, with a shaman as witness. The strongest form waits for a storm, because a treaty sworn before lightning binds a tribe for as long as the tribe stands. The witnessing shaman reads the sky through the oath; a bolt close by while the words are said is the sky hearing them, and a dry sky is a reason to swear again on another day.

For a stranger, this decides where business is done. A bargain struck in a tent is talk until both parties have stepped outside and said it again. An oath sworn under a roof binds nobody, so a rider who swears inside a caravanserai has not sworn, and a promise made to him there has not been given. An oath-breaker is the sky's business before he is anyone else's: the tribe he cheated sells him nothing again, and when he dies no shaman will lay him out.

## At the Water

The wells belong to the earth-mother before they belong to any tribe, and the rites of the water follow from that. A shaman cuts the tribe's sign into a new well-stone at dawn and in the open, so that the sky and the ground both see whose water it is. Blood is never let at a well. Owners who find a man with steel drawn at their water take him away from it before they kill him, and no feud is fought, settled or sworn within sight of a well-stone.

In a dry year, when the wells drop, the tribe's shamans go to the lowest well the tribe owns and sit at its stone for a night and a day, asking the earth-mother for the water she is keeping. Until they come away from the stone, the tribe sells no water to anyone. A caravan that reaches a tribe's range in the middle of that vigil waits for it to end, however thirsty it is.

## What a Guest Keeps

A guest who has eaten a tribe's salt is protected to the edge of its country, and the faith asks very little of him in return. What it asks, it asks without exception, and a sovid riding with a caravan tells it once:

- **Do not dig.** A hole in the ground is a grave, and a grave is for the unavenged. A stranger seen digging is burying a man the tribe has not been told of, or opening what the earth-mother keeps. Ask the dovaq before putting a spade in the ground, and expect to be refused.
- **Keep away from the laying-out ground,** and take nothing from it: no bone, no bead and no blade.
- **Do not kill a vulture** (_budaq_). It does the sky's work at the laying-out.
- **Do not pull up a spear standing in open ground,** and do not camp beside one.
- **Swear outdoors,** and take no promise sworn under a roof as given.
- **Do not cover a shaman's head,** even to keep off the sun or the hail.

## The Storm Cults, the Fire and the Twelve

[[affiliation-strmcltsbzhrms|The Storm Cults of Bazhrámiš]] in [[place-dunharargn|Dunhara]] call the Open Sky a corrupt variant of their own faith, and the quarrel between them is about who may speak for the storm. In Dunhara the storm god speaks through readers trained in five lineages and through vessels whom the elders recognize at the [[place-stoneofranaz|Stone of Ranaz]], and the cults cross every tribal border. On the steppe any rider the sky marks may become a shaman, a shaman speaks for one tribe only, and nobody recognizes a vessel, because the steppe holds that the sky speaks to everyone under it and that no one person carries its voice. The cults fear a shaman nobody has examined. The tribes fear a vessel who can summon warriors over the chiefs' heads, which is very close to what an orghun does.

That difference is what crosses the border. A Dunhari herder who will never be admitted to a lineage can be marked by the sky on the steppe side, and the steppe shamans who preach in Dunhara find their listeners among exactly those herders. The cults have organized formal denunciations of such shamans. A denounced shaman answers to his dovaq first, and the denunciation reaches his tribe as a message it may choose to ignore.

The [[affiliation-ataros|Faith of Ataros]], the fire-faith of the [[affiliation-tribestrzd|Tribes of Atarzád]], is a different kind of thing: one priesthood, fire-temples with roofs over their altars, and a ladder that answers to correct technique. The tribes trade with the fire-priests and let a caravan priest kindle his flame at their camps. Their shamans find the temples strange, and say that a god kept indoors cannot see what is sworn in front of him. In the south, where the oasis cities are close, some shamans also keep the [[affiliation-zurathra|Faith of Zúrathra]], and take the Mother of the Oasis for the earth-mother under a settled people's name.

## Plot Hooks

- **The Spear in the Grass.** A feud spear stands beside a caravan route, its grave a generation old and its feud never ended. The tribe whose range it is will not let anyone dig a new well within sight of it, and offers the caravan free passage for a year if the party finds the dead man's living kin and brings them to end the feud.
- **The Dry Oath.** Two dovaqs swear a treaty before a storm that never breaks, and the witnessing shaman calls the sky dry. One tribe wants to swear again and the other says the oath stands, and the party's caravan needs guides from both.
- **The Bareheaded Dunhari.** A Dunhari herder came north to be marked, went out for the forty days and came back a shaman of the tribe that took him in. The Storm Cults want him brought before the Stone of Ranaz, his dovaq has refused, and the cults have hired riders to fetch him from the caravan he travels with.
- **The Dig.** A scholar has hired the party to excavate in the sands. The tribe whose range it is will sell water and guides but not leave to dig, and the scholar wants the digging done at night.

# The Truth of the Open Sky {#truth .secret}

**What the calling does.** The steppe's belief about its dead works as the tribes say it does, whatever the sky-father's part in it. A Sovidur laid out under the sky with the name called is gathered to the tribe's own dead, a company large enough to keep its members from the hunters of the spirit realm, and the calling is what lets a newly dead soul find that company. Whether the sky-father sends for anyone is theology, and nothing in the world settles it. A soul nobody calls is one of the unreceived that [[lore-bloodfield|Blood-fields]] describes, and a tribe that keeps its names keeps its dead.

**The unavenged grave.** The buried dead are held in the ground by their own will. A man killed unavenged lingers near his grave because the feud holds him, and while the will holds, the hunters do not take him. Most graves are opened within a generation and their dead go when they are called. A grave whose feud is never ended keeps a ghost that wears down over the years to the one wish to be paid for, and the tribes are right to watch the ground around an old spear. Laying out its bones and calling the name lets it go, late or not, and whoever does it needs only the name and the four winds.

**The forty days.** The weather-reading is mostly craft, the long watching of cloud, wind, beasts and the color of the dawn, and a good Dunhari wind-reader reads the same sky as well. The forty days add something the craft does not: a candidate who comes back has learned to leave the body in spirit and return to it, which is how a shaman speaks with the spirits and how the best of them find the lost. Some candidates die of thirst, and some do not return in spirit and are found living, with nobody behind the eyes.

**The oaths.** The sky does not answer oaths. A bolt near an oath is weather, and the thing that holds a steppe oath is the tribes: no guide sold, no salt given and no laying-out. The witnessing shaman's reading is a judgment, and an honest shaman who calls a sky dry is giving a dovaq the chance to think again. That is the steppe's one check on a bad treaty, and both the dovaq and the moot know it.

> [!TIP]
> The Open Sky explains nothing in the sands. What its shamans say about the old works of the desert is what the tribes say, and no shaman knows more of them than the tribe does. The taboo on digging belongs to the earth-mother and the graves; it guards no particular place.
