## Rinho Daowéng—The Lord of Wood

The Elemental Lord of Wood manifests in the celestial records as a figure of organic complexity and patient growth—an official whose authority extends across all states and uses of wood, yet whose administration is marked by a peculiar tolerance for variation and branching. Unlike Daotou Wochai (the Lord of Metal), who maintains absolute categorization, Rinho Daowéng permits multiple simultaneous interpretations of his domain, accommodating different regional traditions and craft-lineages as long as they remain within defined boundaries. Some Tanchéi scholars suggest this flexibility reflects the nature of wood itself: alive in its growing state, yet capable of rigid structure once worked; subject to rot and decay, yet capable of being renewed through replanting and regrowth.

Like all Elemental Lords, Rinho Daowéng answers directly to the Jade Sovereign rather than to a bureau overseer. His authority encompasses wood in all its manifestations—living trees, harvested timber, worked wood used in construction and craftsmanship, wood burned as fuel, and wood in ritual contexts. In the Tantsou cosmological framework, wood occupies a unique position: it is the only element that bears life within itself, and therefore overlaps significantly with the Bureau of Creation (Tsai Sei). This overlap creates both opportunity and constant administrative friction.

### The Classification of Wood

The Lord of Wood maintains the boundary between five administrative categories, each governed according to canonical protocols and delegated to subordinate clerk-spirits:

**Living Wood (Rinho Zha)**—Trees rooted in the earth, forests, groves, and all growing timber. This category is supervised jointly by Rinho Daowéng and the Earth Lord (Wéngdao Zhouwo), with the understanding that Rinho Daowéng claims authority over the wood's _life force_ and reproductive capacity while Wéngdao Zhouwo claims authority over the _soil_ from which the wood grows. In practice, this distinction dissolves at the roots, and disputes over the proper classification of a growing forest are filed with regularity. The clerk-spirit **Zhawochan** maintains the registry of all significant forests and their spiritual allocations, coordinating with regional Touwo (territorial officials) to ensure that harvesting permits are filed correctly.

**Harvested Timber (Ho Rinho)**—Felled wood, lumber in the rough, and timber awaiting processing or sale. This category is supervised by the clerk-spirit **Mougui**, who maintains records of all active logging operations and ensures that harvested timber remains classified as "separated from the life force" rather than deteriorating into unclassified organic matter. A logging operation must file a petition with Rinho Daowéng's office before felling significant timber, both to secure administrative blessing for the harvest and to ensure that the wood does not drift into jurisdictional ambiguity (i.e., is it still part of the forest? has it already been classified as separate?).

**Worked Wood (Ho Tsaimou)**—Finished timber used in construction, furniture, tools, and decorative objects. This category overlaps sharply with the Bureau of Creation (Tsai Sei), whose overseer **Neitsai** claims that worked wood maintains the "continuity" function of its original classification (a wooden house-frame shelters generations; a wooden chair endures across years). Rinho Daowéng disputes this interpretation, arguing that once wood is worked into a human-designed form, it has been _transformed_ and therefore falls under his jurisdiction. The formal compromise, established through repeated precedent rulings, grants Rinho Daowéng authority over the wood's _material integrity_ during the working process while conceding to Neitsai the authority over its _functional continuity_ once the working is complete. This boundary is notoriously unstable, and disputes arise constantly.

**Ritual Wood (Tsaimou Rui)**—Wood used in ceremonial contexts, temple structures, altar furnishings, and sacred vessels. This category is supervised with special care, since ritual wood is a physical bridge between the mortal and celestial administrative orders. Petitions to use wood in novel ritual contexts are reviewed by Rinho Daowéng personally, and the Lord of Wood frequently denies permission on the grounds that unprecedented ceremonial applications create unclassifiable boundary conditions. Established ritual uses (temple frameworks, altar platforms, offering vessels) are permitted under license, with annual renewal required.

**Fuel and Waste Wood (Wo Nenho)**—Wood burned as fuel or decomposing into component materials. This category overlaps with both the Bureau of Transformation (Ho Sei) and the Bureau of Returns (Nuo Sei), creating a triangular jurisdictional dispute that has never been formally resolved. When wood is burned, is it being _transformed_ (Zhenho's claim)? Or is it being _returned_ to its component elements (Nuowo's claim)? Or is it being _released_ from classification and drifting back toward the Henléi (Genhén's claim, though this is the most controversial interpretation)? Rinho Daowéng himself maintains that fuel-wood is still his responsibility until the moment of ignition, and that the subsequent combustion is a separate administrative matter. Most practitioners acknowledge that this position is philosophically weak, but it allows Rinho Daowéng to claim deference while declining to adjudicate the deeper dispute.

### Authority and Jurisdictional Tensions

Rinho Daowéng's primary administrative relationships are with the **Earth Lord** (Wéngdao Zhouwo), with whom he shares jurisdiction over forests and living wood, and with **Neitsai** (Overseer of the Bureau of Creation), whose claims over the "continuity" function of worked wood Rinho Daowéng resists. The conflicts with the Earth Lord are relatively formalized; the conflicts with the Bureau of Creation are more ideological—Rinho Daowéng argues that the Bureau of Creation claims authority over wood's _function_ rather than its _material nature_, a distinction that Neitsai rejects.

A secondary but persistent tension involves the **Bureau of Transformation** (Ho Sei) over fuel wood and the burning of wooden objects. When wood is burned in ritual contexts (to transmit documents to the celestial filing system), both Rinho Daowéng and Zhenho claim jurisdiction—Rinho Daowéng arguing that the wood itself remains under his authority until combustion, Zhenho arguing that the ritual burning is fundamentally a transformation and therefore his domain. The formal compromise permits Zhenho to conduct the burning while requiring Rinho Daowéng's office to pre-certify the wood as "eligible for ritual combustion."

Rinho Daowéng also maintains an uneasy relationship with the **Bureau of Entropy** (Huen Sei), whose overseer Genhén treats forests as sites where controlled dissolution can be managed (forest fires, disease clearing deadwood). Rinho Daowéng resists this interpretation vehemently, arguing that managed forest renewal is fundamentally different from entropy's uncontrolled unraveling, but his arguments lack institutional force—Genhén can invoke the authority of Wéizhuo Daonéi directly.

### Mortal Engagement

Mortals petition Rinho Daowéng through offerings at temple shrines, through the burning of spirit-documents stamped with the Lord of Wood's seal, or through mediation by the Tanchéi priesthood. Unlike petitions to the rigid Daotou Wochai, petitions to Rinho Daowéng permit greater variation in form and language, though all must still follow essential protocol. The Lord of Wood is considered more accessible than the Lord of Metal, though still fundamentally bureaucratic in demeanor.

**Foresters, Loggers, and Timber Merchants** form the primary constituency. A master logger will petition Rinho Daowéng before beginning work in a new forest region, requesting both administrative blessing for the harvest and assurance that the timber will remain categorized correctly during transport and sale. Logging communities maintain shrines to the Lord of Wood and observe seasonal festivals marking the transition between growing season and harvest.

**Carpenters and Woodworkers** petition Rinho Daowéng when beginning significant projects, particularly when the project involves wood from a new source or when the wood is being worked into unprecedented forms. Unlike the Fire Lord, who petitioners approach for technical reliability, carpenters petition Rinho Daowéng for _permission_—acknowledgment that the worked wood remains under administrative sanction and will not slip into unclassified status as the project progresses.

**Shipbuilders and Boat-makers** petition both Rinho Daowéng (for the wood's integrity throughout the building process) and Wodou (Overseer of the Bureau of Passage, under whose jurisdiction water-transit falls). A ship is a particularly complex category—is it wood? Is it a vehicle? Is it a temporary structure? The ambiguity requires coordination between multiple celestial offices, and disputes over a ship's administrative classification are not uncommon.

**Farmers and Agricultural Workers** petition Rinho Daowéng for blessing over wooden tools and wooden elements of the farm (gates, fencing, storage structures), viewing the Lord of Wood as a protector of the sustained productivity of the working landscape.

**Temple Authorities** coordinate with Rinho Daowéng's office regarding the maintenance of wooden temple structures and the licensing of novel ritual uses of wood. Annual petitions are filed to maintain approval for wooden temple furnishings and ceremonial vessels.
