On the first night of the Festival of Deep Sleep a Lem'Nelgir of Nehle'ât lays a pilgrim on a reed mat in the temple's long dim hall and says, quietly so as not to wake the rows around you, "Whatever you see tonight, do not get up to write it down. Lie still and let it finish. In the morning tell me what you remember, and I will tell you whether it was a message or only your supper."

That is the whole of the god's work in one exchange. **Nehle'ât** is the Khelâthi name for [[lore-goddreams|the God of Dreams]], a deity far older than any single civilization. The [[lore-flksinale|Sinalë]] have known him since time immemorial, the Asguardian peoples call him **Bjartr**, the Aurèldían peoples know him as **Aethería**, and countless peoples across the lands of Thalorna worship him. What the Khelâthi add is intimacy: for them he is no distant celestial force but a companion who walks the night beside each sleeper.

## The Guardian of the Threshold

Under the long southern sun and a river whose floods reshape the land in cycles both predictable and mysterious, the Khelâthi treat sleep as more than rest. It is **a sacred passage into the otherworld**, when mortals commune with the divine and the dead, when prophecy runs like water and truth shows itself free of the constraints of daylight. Nehle'ât stands guard at the threshold between the worlds and shields the sleeper from hostile spirits and darker forces that stalk the night. His crescent moon is the night and also the rhythm by which mortals renew themselves.

The eye at the center of his symbol is the All-Seeing Eye, eternally vigilant and never closing while mortals rest. It watches dreams for signs of divine communication and tells the true visions the gods send from the meaningless phantasms of an unsettled mind. In that role Nehle'ât is an intermediary: he filters and interprets the gods' messages so that a mortal can take them in without a mind shattered by unmediated revelation.

All ranks of Khelâthi society venerate him. The poorest farmer sleeps under the stars in the open fields, exposed to physical and spiritual dangers, and Nehle'ât protects him. The wealthy merchant sleeps in a secure chamber ringed with servants and guards, and Nehle'ât still watches his dreams and may deliver visions that guide his trade. The Gar-Aû himself, the divine anchor of the kingdom, relies on Nehle'ât to bring true prophecy in sleep so that he can rule with divine wisdom.

Among the herders of the western grazing country and the peoples of the borderlands, his role grows. Travelers who rest under the open canopy of stars invoke him for protection against real perils of the night: predators, bestial and human, treacherous ground invisible in the dark, and the cold that kills an unwary sleeper. His protective eye becomes a literal guardian that warns of danger before it strikes, and many herding songs show him as a celestial companion riding beside them, so that rest in the wilderness restores them instead of killing them.

In the cities and temples he is honored above all by people whose work turns on dreams: the temple seers who divine the gods' will through prophetic sleep, the physicians who use dream-knowledge to heal, and the astrologers who read the night sky for omens. They hold that Uqa'â lights the waking world with divine fire while Nehle'ât lights the inner world of consciousness, and that the two are equals, one lighting the external realm and the other the internal.

## Worship

Worship centers on the evening, on the passage from waking to sleep. In his temples priests and supplicants perform ritual preparations for sleep and chant invocations asking Nehle'ât to stand guard through the night. The commonest prayer is whispered just before the eyes close:

> Nehle'ât, Guardian of the Threshold, keep vigil while I rest. Guide my visions with your All-Seeing Eye. Grant me dreams of truth and protection from the shadows of night.

The most sacred night in the Khelâthi calendar is the **Festival of Deep Sleep**, held at the height of the inundation, when the igelu's flood closes much of the land to travel and work stops. For three days and nights the land falls into a restful suspension that mirrors the cosmic sleep from which creation emerged. His temples stay open day and night as safe places where pilgrims sleep under his direct protection, and priests keep vigil around the sleepers, ready to interpret any dream that comes with unusual clarity or power. The interpreted dreams are recorded and studied, and some are proclaimed as divine guidance for the coming year.

Every home keeps a small shrine to Nehle'ât, often a carved crescent moon or an amulet near the sleeping quarters. Before bed the family offers small tokens: a cup of milk or water, a pinch of incense, a whispered prayer. Parents teach children to ask his protection before closing their eyes, and elders pass on that a bad dream, once the All-Seeing Eye has marked it as one, becomes a lesson instead of a curse. That household habit probably makes him the most frequently invoked god in daily Khelâthi life, more than the great gods in their stone temples.

## Organization

His priesthood is small and specialized beside the clergy of Wethûr or Qe'âret. All priests answer ultimately to the Gar-Aû as high priest of all the gods, and the Thâz'Lekhau (High Priest) of Nehle'ât oversees a smaller body than theirs. The smallness follows from the god's nature: he is met alone, in the privacy of sleep, so his priests work less as administrators of grand temple complexes and more as guides and interpreters, in shrines and nightlong vigils.

The Lem'Nelgir (ordained priests) train at length in dream interpretation, studying the symbolic language in which the gods speak to mortals. They learn to tell true vision from anxiety, prophecy from fantasy and divine warning from the mind's random wandering. That knowledge cannot be taught to everyone: it demands natural aptitude as well as years of disciplined study. The Wazu (acolytes) serve them by keeping the shrines, preparing incense and offerings and assisting at the nightlong festival vigils.

Many seers, oracles and dream-interpreters who were never ordained by a temple still work within his faith, their authority resting on real visions and a record of guiding others toward truth. The formal priesthood tolerates them, since Nehle'ât is held to speak to mortals directly without priestly intermediaries. Those who abuse the freedom, using dream-interpretation as cover for fraud or manipulation, are quickly denounced by the legitimate clergy.

## Relations

Nehle'ât works with the [[affiliation-rethsaar|Faith of Reth'Sa'âr]], god of wisdom and divine knowledge. Reth'Sa'âr records and preserves truth in the waking hours; Nehle'ât delivers truth in visions during sleep. Reth'Sa'âr's ibis-quill writes on golden scrolls, and Nehle'ât's eye reads the symbols the gods write in the night sky and in mortal consciousness alike. Many theologians hold the two to be aspects of one cosmic principle, the preservation and communication of divine truth.

His bond with Wethûr, Lord of Silent Passage, is subtler and matters just as much. Both preside over transitions: Wethûr over the passage from life to death, Nehle'ât over the nightly passage from waking to sleep. Khelâthi theology calls sleep "the lesser death," and dreams are understood as rehearsals for the greater journey after death, so those who learn to move through their dreams with awareness and acceptance under Nehle'ât's protection are better prepared for the final passage under Wethûr. Some advanced priests teach that the two may guide the same soul at different thresholds.

He also stands in protective relation to Uznêra, goddess of healing and renewal. Uznêra restores the body by direct intervention and healing touch; Nehle'ât lets the body restore itself through undisturbed, protected sleep. Pilgrims who come to a temple of Uznêra for healing often spend their nights in sanctuaries of Nehle'ât, believing that physical healing and protected sleep together restore wholeness.
