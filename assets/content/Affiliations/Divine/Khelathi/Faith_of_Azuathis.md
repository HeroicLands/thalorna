"Not at the table," a Lem'Nelgir of [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]] tells a child who has asked about the serpent over supper, and lowers his voice. "Say it where the sun can hear you, in the forecourt, at noon. Say it in the dark and he hears it first."

That is the first lesson of **Azu'âthis**, the Devouring Shadow, and the rest follows from it. He is the primordial chaos that existed before creation and strives without end to reclaim the cosmos. Nobody worships or loves him. The Khelâthi fear him, propitiate him and ward against him, and his name is avoided in polite speech. The temples picture him as an enormous serpent of impossible length, scales darker than the deepest night, eyes lit with malevolent intelligence; sometimes he coils around a shattered star, the moment of destruction he pursues forever. Plague and pestilence, famine and drought, the slow entropy that claims all things and the sudden catastrophe that ends prosperity in an hour all lie inside his coils.

## The Nightly War

Every night [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]], the sun god and supreme deity, passes through the underworld and meets the serpent there. Azu'âthis tries to swallow the sun whole and plunge the cosmos into permanent darkness. Uqa'â, with the other gods gathered to his defense, throws him down, binds him and drives him back into the abyss. Azu'âthis is eternal and can be conquered but never destroyed, so the battle is fought again the next night and every night after. **Sunrise in Aû'Khelâthu is a victory, not a fact of nature**, and the temples teach it as the deepest truth of existence: chaos is not beaten once but resisted continually, and vigilance is a permanent duty.

The Khelâthi lay every catastrophe at his door. A plague that empties a quarter of a city is his venom. A famine after failed crops is his doing, whether he corrupted the waters or withered the sun's nourishing rays. He is behind the tempest that comes from nowhere to smash a caravan, the flood that fails or comes too violently, and the political unrest that tears a province's social fabric. He is also behind the slow decay: the rot that takes a limb, the madness that takes a mind, the corruption of an authority and the degradation of an institution.

The temples also teach that he is necessary. A cosmos without challenge would be static and sterile, courage exists only because threats do, and devotion to Uqa'â means something because it is kept up against real opposition. Philosophers and priests argue over whether Azu'âthis is truly evil, a conscious malevolence, or the embodiment of natural forces indifferent to morality. The argument is open, and the ambiguity deepens the dread around him.

## Worship

You will find no temple of Azu'âthis, no priesthood devoted to him and no festival in his honor. What exists is propitiation: appeasing his wrath and turning his attention elsewhere. A household hides protective symbols in its walls and beneath its doorways: serpents bound in magical chains, stars ringed with defensive barriers, images of Uqa'â triumphant. They are made quietly and understood as charms, never as religious objects.

In plague, famine or war a community performs rites designed to turn the serpent's gaze from its region. They are held at night, with torch processions that borrow the sun's power and effigies of the chaos serpent smashed or burned. The priesthood of Uqa'â oversees them and frames them as strengthening Uqa'â and his allies in their nightly battle. Offerings are never made to Azu'âthis, which would be an abomination; they are burned or buried so that he cannot claim them.

Some people, touched by tragedy or corruption, grow an obsessive fear of him that borders on a twisted worship. They keep elaborate systems of protective ritual and fill their homes with symbols of binding and restriction. The priesthood treats them with pity and wariness, because excessive fear of Azu'âthis is itself a kind of chaos, a corruption of the mind by the thing feared.

## Organization

Within the orthodoxy of Aû'Khelâthu a priesthood of Azu'âthis cannot exist. Knowledge of him lives instead in the higher echelons of other temples. The Thâz'Lekhau of Uqa'â keep secret records and treatises on his nature, his manifestations and his methods of attack, so the priesthood can counsel the Gar-Aû and prepare the realm for catastrophe. The scribes of [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]] record everything bearing on the chaos god, since knowledge is understood as a powerful defense against chaos.

Outside those temples stand two groups. The first is the frontier shamans and wise folk, the herders of the western grazing country and the hill-dwellers of the eastern ranges, who understand Azu'âthis as an aspect of storm, earthquake and drought and work protections against him. They sit outside the official priesthood and hold an implicit sanction, because their work protects communities and their understanding is held to be differently framed rather than wrong. The second is the heretics, people who turned to worship in the belief that appeasement buys personal power or that destruction is better than the constraints of order. Civil and religious authority hunt them as threats to the state itself, and a person found keeping a shrine to Azu'âthis faces execution, since the shrine is held to invite catastrophe on the realm. Rare texts hint at cults in underground chambers that see the serpent as liberation; if such groups exist they leave virtually no record, and scholars dispute that they do.

## Relations

Azu'âthis and Uqa'â are in eternal enmity, and Uqa'â is supreme in part because he is the serpent's opponent. The other gods serve him in that war with magic and with weapons forged specifically to wound and bind Azu'âthis. [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]]'s creation stands against entropy, and Reth'Sa'âr's knowledge exposes the serpent's methods so defenses can be raised. Even [[lore-thubaidty|Thubâ'i]]'s prosperity counts as evidence: while his blessings flow the land is fertile and peaceful and the serpent is held at bay, and the moment his abundance dries up and famine strikes, the victory shows cracks and the serpent's influence is recognized.

With mortals Azu'âthis is adversarial. Where the other gods work through devotees, he works through corruption and destruction, turning people into vessels for his will or tools of calamity. He is also the god whose catastrophes teach mortals the value of what they hold and the fragility of civilization, and every hardship endured in the service of order brings a person closer to understanding why the struggle is eternal.
