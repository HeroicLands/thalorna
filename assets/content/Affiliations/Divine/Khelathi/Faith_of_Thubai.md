Thubâ'i embodies the abundance that flows through Aû'Khelâthu like the river itself, bringing fertility to the fields, prosperity to traders, and joy to those who know how to celebrate life. He is not a god of restraint or ascetic virtue, but of exuberant plenty—the laughter at a feast, the swell of passion, the glint of gold in lamplight. Depicted as a corpulent figure crowned with cornucopiae and adorned with precious metals, or sometimes as the golden waters of the Khelâthi River itself flowing with treasures, Thubâ'i represents the divine promise that the world is abundant and meant to be enjoyed. His temples overflow with the scents of flowers, incense, and roasted meats; his festivals are famous throughout the civilized lands as occasions of unparalleled revelry.

The worship of Thubâ'i reflects a fundamental truth of Khelâthi philosophy: the pursuit of material comfort, sensual pleasure, and worldly delight is not a distraction from spirituality but an expression of it. To refuse Thubâ'i's gifts is to refuse the divine bounty itself. The followers of Thubâ'i argue that only those who understand pleasure can truly appreciate the cosmos, for they experience its richness directly. Good harvests, successful trading voyages, abundant wine, beautiful lovers—these are not obstacles to enlightenment but glimpses of the divine generosity that underlies existence.

Yet the faith of Thubâ'i is not naive; his temples and priests teach that abundance carries within it the seeds of excess, and that unchecked indulgence can lead to ruin as surely as poverty can. The god's dual nature encompasses both the blessing of prosperity and the cautionary lesson of those who have gorged themselves to destruction. A merchant who hoards wealth while his workers starve, a noble who squanders his family's fortune on endless debauchery—these are shown the wrath of Thubâ'i as readily as they enjoyed his rewards. The god demands not merely consumption but celebration, and celebration properly understood is a social act, a sharing of joy with others.

The followers of Thubâ'i include merchants and traders, whose commercial success they attribute to the god's favor; nobles who understand that their wealth creates opportunities for refinement and culture; farmers and vintners, who credit Thubâ'i with the fertility of their crops; and perhaps most significantly, the common people, who see in Thubâ'i's worship a divine sanction for the simple pleasures that make life bearable—good beer, good company, music, and laughter. The contradiction between spiritual devotion and sensual enjoyment that might perplex other faiths simply does not exist in the worship of Thubâ'i; the two are one and the same.

Beer and wine are central to both the religious and secular life of Aû'Khelâthu, and both are explicitly understood as gifts from Thubâ'i. Brewmasters maintain small shrines in their breweries, and vintners time their harvests and fermentation processes by festivals dedicated to the god. The finest wines and beers are regularly offered at his temples, where the priesthood conducts solemn ceremonies of appreciation before distributing them to the faithful for consumption. Music and dance are integral to Thubâ'i's worship; his temples employ musicians and dancers who perform not in solemn procession but in joyful celebration.

## Worship

The devotion to Thubâ'i is characterized by abundance and celebration. Worshippers bring offerings of the finest foods and drinks available to them—the wealthy offer rare wines, exotic fruits, and meat from sacrificed animals; the poor bring beer, bread, and honey. Temples hold great feasts several times monthly, occasions where the offerings are transformed into communal meals where rich and poor, slave and free, eat together. These feasts are understood as literal participation in the divine, as Thubâ'i's abundance becomes flesh and sustenance for all who partake.

The major festivals honoring Thubâ'i occur at the height of the annual inundation, when the river swells with the god's blessings and the fields are enriched with fertile silt. During this season, the normally rigid class structure of Aû'Khelâthu loosens; elaborate masquerade balls are held where social distinctions temporarily dissolve; the temples organize drinking competitions, dancing contests, and sumptuous banquets that last from sunset to sunrise. Merchants close their shops for these celebrations, farmers set aside their tools, and even the Gar-Aû participates in public festivities honoring the god. The revelry is not frivolous but sacred—a form of worship through the genuine enjoyment of life's pleasures.

Private worship typically involves the maintenance of personal shrines where devotees leave offerings of food, drink, flowers, or incense. Many homes contain images of Thubâ'i positioned prominently in dining areas or bedchambers, reminding inhabitants to appreciate the comforts they enjoy. Individuals contemplating important business transactions, artistic endeavors, or romantic pursuits often make special offerings to Thubâ'i, requesting his favor and blessing. Evening meals often conclude with a ritual toast to Thubâ'i, a moment of gratitude for the food provided and an expression of hope for continued prosperity.

## Organization

The priesthood of Thubâ'i is organized around the temples, which function as centers of feasting and celebration as much as places of worship. The Thâz'Lekhau, or High Priest, is typically chosen not only for spiritual knowledge but for a demonstrated ability to organize festivals and maintain the temple's reputation for generosity and excellent food. Beneath the Thâz'Lekhau serve Lem'Nelgir, ordained priests who oversee different aspects of the temple's operations—the keeper of the wine vaults, the keeper of the granaries, the keeper of the festival calendar.

The acolytes of Thubâ'i, known as Wazu, receive training that differs substantially from those of other temples. They learn not merely theology but practical skills: viticulture and brewing, cuisine and provisioning, music and dance. Many Wazu never advance to full priesthood but instead pursue careers as chefs, brewmasters, musicians, or event planners, maintaining informal ties to the temple throughout their lives. This has made the Thubâ'i priesthood less formally organized than some others, yet no less influential—the ability to command the finest food and entertainment in a region gives considerable social power.

The wealth and resources flowing to Thubâ'i's temples are substantial. The god's patronage makes successful merchants generous to his sanctuaries; the hospitality of the temples ensures they receive constant donations of food and wine. However, the priesthood's control over these resources is loose compared to other faiths; wealth is meant to flow through Thubâ'i's temples like water through a riverbed, never stagnating, always moving toward new celebrations and new generosity. A high priest who accumulates wealth without adequate spending on festivals and feasts quickly loses donations and political influence.

## Relations

Thubâ'i maintains a complex relationship with [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]], god of knowledge and restraint. The worship of both exists comfortably in Aû'Khelâthu, despite their apparent opposition, with educated individuals often maintaining shrines to both deities. Where Reth'Sa'âr counsels careful record-keeping and intellectual discipline, Thubâ'i urges the enjoyment of the rewards such discipline produces. Together they are seen as representing the complete life—Reth'Sa'âr guides the accumulation of resources and knowledge, while Thubâ'i sanctifies their use and enjoyment.

The relationship with [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]], god of creation and craftsmanship, is generally positive. Craftspeople and artisans, while primarily devoted to Psaq'âru, often make offerings to Thubâ'i in the hope that their work will be appreciated and well-rewarded. Thubâ'i is understood as the god who ensures that human effort receives its due compensation, that the rewards of labor are substantial enough to justify the work.

Thubâ'i maintains an uneasy distance from [[lore-azuathisdty|Azu'âthis]], god of chaos and destruction. Where Thubâ'i represents the ordered abundance that flows from cosmic stability, Azu'âthis embodies the chaos that would destroy all prosperity. The priesthood of Thubâ'i does not speak openly of Azu'âthis, considering him inauspicious, but the awareness is present—prosperity is fragile, abundance can be destroyed in an instant, and the pleasures of life exist under the constant threat of catastrophe. This awareness adds a subtle poignancy to Thubâ'i's worship, a recognition that life's joys must be seized and celebrated precisely because they are not guaranteed.
