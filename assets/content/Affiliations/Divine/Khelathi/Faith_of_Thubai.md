A brewer in a flower-wreathed doorway at the height of the inundation presses a cup of dark beer into a foreign visitor's hand. "Drink it before you say anything polite," she says. "The temple has opened its vaults, the shops are shut, and the man dancing on that table is the steward of a Halzi'a. Tonight no one asks what you are. Tomorrow, mind, they will ask what you owe, and [[lore-thubaidty|Thubâ'i]] will not be on your side if you have been a miser all year."

Thubâ'i, the **Golden Reveler**, is the abundance that flows through [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Aû'Khelâthu]] like the river itself, bringing fertility to the fields, prosperity to traders and joy to those who know how to celebrate life. He is a god of exuberant plenty rather than restraint or ascetic virtue: the laughter at a feast, the swell of passion, the glint of gold in lamplight. He is shown as a corpulent figure crowned with cornucopiae and hung with precious metals, or sometimes as the golden waters of the river running with treasure. His temples overflow with the scents of flowers, incense and roasted meat, and his festivals are famous across the civilized lands for their revelry.

His worship carries a real philosophical claim: _pursuing comfort, sensual pleasure and worldly delight is an expression of spirituality, not a distraction from it_. To refuse his gifts is to refuse the divine bounty itself. His followers argue that only people who understand pleasure can appreciate the cosmos, because they taste its richness directly. Good harvests, successful trading voyages, abundant wine and beautiful lovers are glimpses of the generosity that underlies existence.

## The Warning Inside the Feast

His priests are not naive about it, and a newcomer should hear this part early. Abundance carries the seeds of excess, and unchecked indulgence ruins a person as surely as poverty does. The god's nature has two faces: the blessing of prosperity, and the lesson of those who gorged themselves to destruction. A merchant who hoards wealth while his workers starve and a noble who squanders his family's fortune on endless debauchery meet the wrath of Thubâ'i as readily as they enjoyed his rewards. He asks for celebration, and celebration properly understood is a social act, the sharing of joy with others.

## Who Follows Him

His followers include merchants and traders, who credit their commercial success to his favor; nobles, who know their wealth buys refinement and culture; farmers and vintners, who thank him for the fertility of their crops; and, most of all, the common people, who find in his worship divine sanction for the simple pleasures that make life bearable: good beer, good company, music and laughter. The contradiction between spiritual devotion and sensual enjoyment that perplexes other faiths does not exist for them, because the two are the same thing.

Beer and wine are central to religious and secular life alike in Aû'Khelâthu, and both are understood as his gifts. Brewmasters keep small shrines in their breweries, and vintners time harvest and fermentation by his festivals. The finest wines and beers go to his temples, where the priesthood conducts solemn ceremonies of appreciation before sharing them with the faithful. Music and dance are integral: his temples employ musicians and dancers who perform in joyful celebration, never in solemn procession.

## Worship

Worship is abundance and celebration. The wealthy bring rare wines, exotic fruits and meat from sacrificed animals; the poor bring beer, bread and honey. The temples hold great feasts several times a month, turning the offerings into communal meals where rich and poor, slave and free, eat together, and the feasts count as literal participation in the divine, since his abundance becomes flesh and sustenance for everyone who partakes.

The major festivals fall at the height of the annual inundation, when the river swells with the god's blessings and the fields take on fertile silt. The rigid class structure of Aû'Khelâthu loosens. Elaborate masquerade balls dissolve social distinctions for a night, and the temples run drinking competitions, dancing contests and sumptuous banquets that last from sunset to sunrise. Merchants close their shops, farmers lay down their tools and even the [[lore-garauu|Gar-Aû]] joins the public festivities. The revelry is sacred, a form of worship through real enjoyment of life's pleasures.

At home a devotee keeps a shrine with offerings of food, drink, flowers or incense. Many homes put an image of Thubâ'i in a dining area or bedchamber to remind the household to appreciate its comforts. People weighing an important business deal, an artistic project or a romance make a special offering to ask his favor, and evening meals often end with a ritual toast to him, a moment of gratitude for the food and a hope for continued prosperity.

## Organization

The priesthood is organized around the temples, which are centers of feasting and celebration as much as places of worship. The Thâz'Lekhau (High Priest) is typically chosen for spiritual knowledge and also for a demonstrated ability to organize festivals and keep the temple's reputation for generosity and excellent food. Beneath serve the Lem'Nelgir (ordained priests), who oversee different parts of the temple's operation: the keeper of the wine vaults, the keeper of the granaries and the keeper of the festival calendar.

The Wazu (acolytes) are trained differently from those of other temples. They learn theology and also practical skills: viticulture and brewing, cuisine and provisioning, music and dance. Many never reach the full priesthood and become chefs, brewmasters, musicians or organizers of great occasions, keeping informal ties to the temple all their lives. That makes the priesthood less formally organized than some others and no less influential, because the power to command the finest food and entertainment in a region carries considerable social weight.

The wealth flowing to his temples is substantial. His patronage makes successful merchants generous to his sanctuaries, and the temples' hospitality brings steady donations of food and wine. The priesthood holds those resources loosely compared with other faiths: wealth is meant to run through his temples like water through a riverbed, never stagnating, always moving toward new celebrations and new generosity. A high priest who hoarded wealth without spending enough on festivals and feasts would find donations shrinking and political influence draining away.

## Relations

His tie to [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]], god of knowledge and restraint, is complex. Their worship sits easily together in Aû'Khelâthu despite the apparent opposition, and educated people often keep shrines to both. Reth'Sa'âr counsels careful record-keeping and intellectual discipline; Thubâ'i urges the enjoyment of what that discipline earns. Together they represent the complete life: Reth'Sa'âr guides the accumulation of resources and knowledge, and Thubâ'i sanctifies their use and enjoyment.

His relation with [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]], god of creation and craftsmanship, is generally warm. Craftspeople, devoted first to Psaq'âru, often make offerings to Thubâ'i in the hope that their work will be appreciated and well rewarded. He is the god who ensures that human effort receives its due and that the reward of labor is enough to justify the work.

He keeps an uneasy distance from [[lore-azuathisdty|Azu'âthis]], god of chaos and destruction. Thubâ'i stands for the ordered abundance that flows from cosmic stability and Azu'âthis for the chaos that would destroy all prosperity. His priests do not speak openly of Azu'âthis and consider him inauspicious, but they know that prosperity is fragile, that abundance can be destroyed in an instant and that the pleasures of life exist under constant threat of catastrophe. The awareness gives his worship a quiet poignancy: joys must be seized and celebrated precisely because they are not guaranteed.
