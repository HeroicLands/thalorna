Uznêra is the great mother goddess of Aû'Khelâthu, the principle of fertility, renewal, and healing that sustains all life. She is the great river that floods each year, laying rich black silt across the valley and coaxing the dry-season fields back into lush abundance. She is the womb from which all creation springs, the nurturing force that sustains children from birth to maturity. But Uznêra is also the healer, the restorer of health, the one who mends what is broken and returns the wounded to wholeness. Her theology teaches that all life depends on balance between masculine and feminine principles, that creation itself requires the cooperation of male and female divine forces.

The symbol of Uznêra—the lotus intertwined with a serpent—contains profound theological meaning. The lotus represents renewal and beauty, emerging pristine from the mud of the igelu each dawn, reborn each morning. The serpent represents transformation and the shedding of old forms, the healing power that comes through renewal. Together, they express Uznêra's essential truth: that healing and fertility require the willingness to shed the old, to transform, and to grow.

Uznêra's teachings have profound implications for Khelâthi society. The faith asserts that women possess intrinsic spiritual authority and authority within the home and family. While Khelâthi law and society are patriarchal in structure, the Theology of Uznêra provides theological justification for women's significant legal rights—particularly regarding property, inheritance, and the ability to initiate divorce. A woman who bears children has fulfilled the will of Uznêra and thereby possesses spiritual standing in her community. A woman skilled in healing has received a direct calling from the goddess. The priesthood of Uznêra, uniquely among the Khelâthi faiths, is reserved primarily for women, though some male priests serve as healers and scholars of the goddess's mysteries.

The temples of Uznêra are places of life and renewal. Unlike the austere stone temples of Qe'âret or the blazing sanctums of Uqa'â, the temples of Uznêra are gardens of green growing things. The priestesses maintain vast gardens where healing herbs are cultivated, where fruit trees bear abundant crops, and where the sacred lotus pools harbor the flowers that symbolize the goddess's eternal renewal. These temples serve not only as places of worship but as centers of practical healing, where the sick and injured come to be treated by priestesses trained in the arts of herbalism and medicine.

## Worship

The greatest festival of Uznêra's faith occurs during the flood season of the igelu, when the river reaches its height and inundates the fields with rich silt. The Flood Festival celebrates the goddess's generosity and the promise of abundant harvest. During this festival, women lead elaborate processions to the temple, bearing wreaths of flowers and offerings of grain. The Thâz'Lekhau of Uznêra blesses the flood waters and offers prayers for protection of mothers and the safe delivery of children during the coming year. Men and women together celebrate the renewal of the land, often in festivals of sensuality and joy that are unusual in the primarily solemn Khelâthi religious landscape.

When a woman becomes pregnant, she may visit a temple of Uznêra to receive the goddess's blessing and protection. During the pregnancy, she may return to the temple for healing preparations—special herbs and tinctures believed to support the pregnancy and ease the eventual birth. When the time of labor arrives, the pregnant woman is brought to a temple chamber where priestesses attend her, invoking Uznêra's aid to ensure safe delivery. The priestesses are trained in the practical arts of childbirth assistance, and their knowledge—combined with their spiritual authority—makes temple births far more likely to result in successful outcomes than births attended by untrained midwives.

Those suffering from illness or injury may visit a temple of Uznêra to receive healing. The priestesses employ both physical remedies (herbal medicines, poultices, and surgical techniques) and spiritual interventions (prayer, ritual blessing, and communion with the goddess's power). The efficacy of temple healing is such that Khelâthi nobility will often summon priestesses from the nearest temple of Uznêra when faced with serious illness, trusting their knowledge more than they trust the practices of other healers. This has made the priestesses of Uznêra some of the most respected and honored figures in Khelâthi society.

The faith maintains smaller ceremonies marking the major transitions of life. When a child is born, the priestesses conduct a naming ceremony and invoke Uznêra's protection over the child. When a girl reaches adolescence, the priestesses conduct a ritual acknowledging her transition from child to woman and her new capacity to bear life. When a woman passes beyond her childbearing years, the priestesses conduct a ceremony honoring the wisdom she has gained and her new role as teacher and spiritual guide within her family.

## Organization

Unlike other Khelâthi priesthoods, the hierarchy of Uznêra's faith is matriarchal in structure. At the apex stands the Thâz'Lekhau, the High Priestess, who is chosen from among the most respected and accomplished priestesses. She typically serves until her death or voluntary retirement, after which the senior priestesses gather to elect her successor. The Thâz'Lekhau serves as the chief advisor to the Gar-Aû on matters relating to fertility, childbirth, health, and women's concerns.

Below the Thâz'Lekhau stand the Lem'Nelgir, ordained priestesses who direct the temple complexes and oversee the spiritual and practical work of healing. These women have undergone years of training in herbalism, anatomy, childbirth assistance, and the sacred mysteries of Uznêra. Many are also midwives and healers of significant renown. The most senior Lem'Nelgir often serve as counselors to the Thâz'Lekhau and help guide the priesthood's strategic decisions.

The Wazu acolytes, young women training for priesthood, undergo apprenticeships lasting five to ten years. They learn the names and properties of hundreds of healing herbs, study the body's systems and functions, and train in the practical arts of assisting birth and caring for the sick. They also study the sacred texts and learn the ritual mysteries that connect their practical healing work to Uznêra's spiritual power. Some Wazu never advance to full priesthood but instead become accomplished healers, known as Zethu, who serve the temples and communities throughout their lives.

The priesthood also employs male priests, though in smaller numbers and typically not in positions of top authority. These men, called Lem'Nelgir Djem (male priests), often specialize in botanical knowledge, the cultivation of the sacred gardens, or the study of heredity and bloodlines. A few serve as healers, particularly in cases where male patients might be uncomfortable receiving treatment from priestesses.

## Relations

The Faith of Uznêra maintains warm alliance with [[affiliation-qearet|Faith of Qe'âret]]. Both goddesses emphasize the necessity of balance and the fundamental importance of partnership. Qe'âret's insistence that the cosmos depends on balance finds theological expression in Uznêra's teaching that creation itself requires the cooperation of masculine and feminine principles. The two priesthoods work together to support women's legal and spiritual rights, ensuring that the Theology of Uznêra provides the framework for justice regarding women's concerns and that the sacred authority of the goddess is reflected in law.

Uznêra stands in harmonious relationship with [[affiliation-uqaa|Faith of Uqa'â]]. The sun's heat enables the fertility that Uznêra nurtures. Uqa'â's purifying power prevents disease and corruption, while Uznêra's healing power restores those afflicted despite Uqa'â's protection. Many temples dedicate specific hours to celebrating the alliance between the two deities, with priestesses of Uznêra gathering in sunlit courtyards to pray for health and blessing, consciously bringing together the illuminating power of Uqa'â with the nurturing power of Uznêra.

The priesthood of Uznêra maintains complicated relationships with the other Khelâthi faiths. They work closely with the priests of Wethûr in managing the transition from life to death, ensuring that women who die in childbirth are honored and their souls properly guided. They maintain distance from the chaos cults, seeing disease and corruption not as signs of Azu'âthis's power but as perversions of the natural order that Uznêra's healing is meant to restore. Some tension exists between the priesthood of Uznêra and more ascetic faiths that view bodily pleasure and sensuality as corrupting; the priestesses of Uznêra argue that the body itself is sacred, that sexuality is holy when properly honored, and that the denial of the body's legitimate needs is a denial of Uznêra's gift.

The faith of Uznêra is thus a counterbalance to the austerity of some Khelâthi religious traditions. Where other priesthoods demand sacrifice and renunciation, Uznêra teaches that life itself—in all its sensual, fertile, pleasure-filled reality—is sacred. This does not mean Uznêra's followers advocate for chaos or excess, but rather that they insist life's generative and healing forces are as divine and as worthy of celebration as life's abstract principles of order and truth.
