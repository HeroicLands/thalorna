A steward of a great household arrives at a temple gate with a litter and a message that his lady is ill, and a Lem'Nelgir of Uznêra meets him among the lotus pools with her sleeves already rolled. "Tell me what she eats, what she drinks and when the fever comes," she says, walking toward the herb beds. "I will send two priestesses with you tonight. You may thank the goddess when you are home, not before. And you need not look for a physician from the city. Your master's father sent for us, and so did his father."

**Uznêra** is the great mother goddess of Aû'Khelâthu, the principle of fertility, renewal and healing that sustains all life. She is the great river that floods each year, laying rich black silt across the valley and coaxing the fields back into lush abundance. She is the womb from which creation springs and the nurturing force that carries children from birth to maturity. She is also the healer, who mends what is broken and returns the wounded to wholeness. Her theology teaches that all life depends on balance between masculine and feminine principles, and that creation itself requires the cooperation of male and female divine forces.

Her symbol is a lotus intertwined with a serpent. The lotus is renewal and beauty, rising pristine from the mud of the igelu each dawn and reborn each morning. The serpent is transformation and the shedding of old forms, the healing that comes through renewal. Together they state her essential truth: healing and fertility both require the willingness to shed the old, to transform and to grow.

## What She Teaches About Women

Her teaching reaches into Khelâthi law. Her faith asserts that women have intrinsic spiritual authority and authority within the home and family, and it supplies the doctrine behind women's wide legal standing, particularly over property and inheritance and in the right to initiate divorce. A woman who bears children has fulfilled Uznêra's will and has spiritual standing in her community. A woman skilled in healing has received a direct calling from the goddess. Her priesthood, alone among the Khelâthi faiths, is reserved primarily for women, though some male priests serve as healers and scholars of her mysteries.

## The Temples

Her temples are places of life and renewal. The temples of Qe'âret are austere stone and those of Uqa'â blazing sanctums, but hers are gardens of green growing things. The priestesses keep vast gardens where healing herbs are cultivated and fruit trees bear abundantly, and the sacred lotus pools hold the flowers that stand for the goddess's eternal renewal. The temples are also centers of practical healing, where the sick and injured are treated by priestesses trained in herbalism and medicine.

## Worship

The greatest festival is the **Flood Festival**, held when the igelu reaches its height and drowns the fields in silt. It celebrates the goddess's generosity and the promise of abundant harvest. Women lead elaborate processions to the temple with wreaths of flowers and offerings of grain. The Thâz'Lekhau of Uznêra blesses the flood waters and prays for the protection of mothers and the safe delivery of children in the coming year. Men and women together celebrate the renewal of the land, often in festivals of sensuality and joy that are unusual in the otherwise solemn Khelâthi religious landscape.

A pregnant woman may visit a temple of Uznêra for the goddess's blessing and protection, and return during the pregnancy for healing preparations, special herbs and tinctures believed to support the pregnancy and ease the birth. When labor comes she is brought to a temple chamber where priestesses attend her and invoke the goddess's aid. They are trained in the practical arts of assisting childbirth, and their knowledge together with their spiritual authority makes temple births far more likely to succeed than births attended by untrained midwives.

People who are ill or injured may come for healing. The priestesses use physical remedies, herbal medicines, poultices and surgical techniques, and spiritual ones, prayer, ritual blessing and communion with the goddess's power. Temple healing works well enough that Khelâthi nobility often summon priestesses from the nearest temple of Uznêra in serious illness, trusting their knowledge above other healers', and that has made the priestesses some of the most respected and honored figures in Khelâthi society.

Smaller ceremonies mark the great transitions of life. When a child is born, the priestesses hold a naming ceremony and invoke the goddess's protection over the child. When a girl reaches adolescence, they hold a ritual acknowledging her passage from child to woman and her new capacity to bear life. When a woman passes beyond her childbearing years, they hold a ceremony honoring the wisdom she has gained and her new role as teacher and spiritual guide in her family.

## Organization

The hierarchy of her faith is matriarchal, unlike the other Khelâthi priesthoods. At the apex stands the Thâz'Lekhau, the High Priestess, chosen from among the most respected and accomplished priestesses. She serves until her death or voluntary retirement, and then the senior priestesses gather to elect her successor. She is the Gar-Aû's chief advisor on fertility, childbirth, health and women's concerns.

Below her stand the Lem'Nelgir, ordained priestesses who direct the temple complexes and oversee the spiritual and practical work of healing. They have trained for years in herbalism, anatomy, childbirth assistance and the sacred mysteries of Uznêra, and many are midwives and healers of significant renown. The most senior often counsel the Thâz'Lekhau and help guide the priesthood's strategic decisions.

The Wazu (acolytes) are young women training for the priesthood in apprenticeships of five to ten years. They learn the names and properties of hundreds of healing herbs, study the body's systems and functions and train in assisting birth and caring for the sick, and they study the sacred texts and the ritual mysteries that connect their healing work to the goddess's spiritual power. Some Wazu never reach full priesthood and become accomplished healers called **Zethu**, who serve the temples and communities for life.

The priesthood also employs male priests, in smaller numbers and typically not in the top positions. These men, called **Lem'Nelgir Djem** (male priests), often specialize in botanical knowledge, the cultivation of the sacred gardens or the study of heredity and bloodlines. A few serve as healers, particularly where a male patient would be uncomfortable receiving treatment from priestesses.

## Relations

The Faith of Uznêra is warmly allied with the [[affiliation-qearet|Faith of Qe'âret]]. Both goddesses stress the necessity of balance and the importance of partnership, and Qe'âret's insistence that the cosmos depends on balance finds its theological expression in Uznêra's teaching that creation requires the cooperation of masculine and feminine principles. The two priesthoods work together to support women's legal and spiritual rights, so that Uznêra's theology frames justice in women's concerns and the goddess's sacred authority is reflected in law.

She stands in harmony with the [[affiliation-uqaa|Faith of Uqa'â]]. The sun's heat enables the fertility she nurtures, and Uqa'â's purifying power prevents disease and corruption while her healing power restores those afflicted despite his protection. Many temples set aside specific hours to celebrate the alliance, when priestesses of Uznêra gather in sunlit courtyards to pray for health and blessing and join Uqa'â's illuminating power to her nurturing one.

Her priesthood keeps complicated relations with the other faiths. It works closely with the priests of Wethûr in managing the passage from life to death, so that women who die in childbirth are honored and their souls properly guided. It keeps its distance from the chaos cults, seeing disease and corruption as perversions of the natural order that her healing exists to restore, and not as signs of Azu'âthis's power. Some tension exists between it and the more ascetic faiths that treat bodily pleasure and sensuality as corrupting. The priestesses answer that the body is sacred, that sexuality is holy when properly honored and that denying the body's legitimate needs denies Uznêra's gift.

Her faith is thus a counterweight to the austerity of some Khelâthi religious traditions. Where other priesthoods demand sacrifice and renunciation, Uznêra teaches that life in all its sensual, fertile and pleasure-filled reality is sacred. Her followers do not advocate chaos or excess; they insist that life's generative and healing forces are as divine and as worthy of celebration as its abstract principles of order and truth.
