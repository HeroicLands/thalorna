A merchant has brought his son of seven to the school gate of a temple of Reth'Sa'âr and is explaining, a little too fast, what the boy already knows. The Lem'Nelgir who keeps the gate lets him finish. "Years," he says. "Reading and writing in more than one script, and then mathematics, and then geometry, and then the part you cannot pay for, which is learning to write down only what is so. A third of them go home to the counting-house. Some of the rest will keep the tables of the stars. He may be one of them. Leave him."

**Reth'Sa'âr**, Scribe of the Eternal Library, is the custodian of all knowledge and the divine scribe, who records the deeds of mortals and the workings of the cosmos alike. In the vaults of the Eternal Library, a celestial archive that exists both within and beyond the world, he inscribes every moment of creation with his ibis quill, so that nothing of importance is ever lost. He is shown with the head of an ibis or crowned with the lunar crescent, because he governs the moon's cycles and the patterns by which mortals organize knowledge and time.

His faith teaches that **knowledge is the greatest treasure and the surest path to virtue**. Where other gods give wealth or martial prowess, he offers understanding: the ability to see truth beneath illusion and to move through both the temporal and the divine realms. His worshipers believe that study, writing and contemplation bring them near the divine mind. The scribal class of Aû'Khelâthu owes its existence to him, and scribes count as priests of the written word who preserve civilization through meticulous records.

His priesthood also supplies the empire's reckoning. Tax rolls and contracts carry the regnal year of the reigning Gar-Aû, and temple chronicles count from the [[lore-qettelgu|Qet Telgu]]; both figures come from Reth'Sa'âr's priests.

## Libraries, Schools and Bards

In the cities his sanctuaries are known for vast libraries, walls lined with thousands of papyrus scrolls and clay tablets inscribed with mathematical treatises, astronomical observations and sacred rituals. Young scribes spend years as apprentices in these temples, learning the mechanics of writing and the philosophy that knowledge rightly understood leads to harmony with the divine order. The priesthood is known for intellectual rigor. Its schools take the children of merchants, administrators and even nobles, and they offer a path of advancement by merit that is probably unique in Aû'Khelâthu.

In the herding country of the west and the borderland hinterlands he takes a different aspect, the divine memory, keeper of oral histories and sacred songs. The herders keep bards devoted to his worship who carry their people's stories forward by recitation and memorization, and who believe that Reth'Sa'âr himself speaks through their voices. These oral historians are treated with deep reverence, since they ensure that the deeds of the ancestors are not forgotten, preserved in sacred speech that carries across the open land.

One learned follower stands out in the capital: **Amqelet-Zelemu Heka'amun**, an astrologer who takes her study of the heavens as an act of worship, an attempt to read the divine script written across the stars. She keeps a small shrine in her home with her astronomical tables and notes, and believes that Reth'Sa'âr guides her observations toward truths that benefit her community.

## Worship

His worship is contemplation, study and the careful preservation of knowledge. Regular offerings at his temples include fine papyrus, rare inks made from precious minerals and carefully transcribed copies of sacred texts. His most devoted followers practice scribal work daily as a form of meditation, copying passages from sacred literature or recording their own observations, since the act of writing is itself a prayer.

His festivals fall at the start of each new lunar cycle, when his dominion over time and record-keeping is most evident. Scribes from across Aû'Khelâthu gather to share new knowledge, mathematical discoveries, medical observations and astronomical findings, as an offering of their intellectual labor to the god. The temples hold competitions in calligraphy and rhetoric, believing that excellence in expressing knowledge honors him, and people bring their children to watch so that the next generation of scholars and wisdom-keepers is inspired.

At home a devotee keeps a journal or record-book of important events, spiritual insights and practical knowledge gained from experience. Many homes hold a small shrine with an image of Reth'Sa'âr and a few carefully chosen scrolls representing the owner's most valued knowledge, and scholars of means often commission copies of important texts to donate to temples, leaving a lasting legacy of their intellectual work.

## Organization

The priesthood is hierarchical in a way distinct from that of the martial temples. At the apex stands the Thâz'Lekhau, the High Priest or High Priestess, who typically holds spiritual authority and recognized learning in mathematics, astronomy or sacred language. Below serve the Lem'Nelgir, ordained priests of knowledge, each over a specific domain: the keeper of the astronomical tables, the keeper of medical texts, the keeper of architectural plans and so on. Each Lem'Nelgir holds authority over a particular archive and guides the training of younger scholars.

The acolytes, the Wazu, are often carefully chosen youths who show promise in intellectual work. Their training is rigorous and lasts many years, covering reading and writing in multiple languages and scripts as well as mathematics, geometry and the principles of sacred knowledge. Many never reach the full priesthood and take secular careers as scribes, administrators or scholars, staying connected to the temple through continued study and pilgrimage. The system has produced a vast network of educated people across Aû'Khelâthu who all keep some allegiance to Reth'Sa'âr's priesthood.

His temples often serve as centers of learning and record-keeping for whole regions. They house the official archives where administrative records, tax receipts and legal documents are stored and preserved, and the priesthood takes that duty with absolute seriousness as stewards of civilization's memory. Corruption is virtually unknown among them, because the priests hold that betraying the duty to preserve knowledge accurately is a betrayal of the divine, and not because the god enforces it.

## Relations

His tie to [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]], the creator god, is complex. Psaq'âru shapes the physical world and gives mortals creativity; Reth'Sa'âr records and organizes what Psaq'âru has made and gives it structure and meaning. The two are complements, since creation without knowledge is chaotic and knowledge without creation is sterile, and temple legends show them in constant, productive dialogue, with Reth'Sa'âr explaining the principles behind Psaq'âru's work.

With [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]], the sun god, his relation is reverence tinged with subordination. Uqa'â is the ultimate authority and the divine will moving through creation, and Reth'Sa'âr serves as his counselor and scribe, recording his decrees and helping mortals understand the divine law Uqa'â embodies. In temple theology it is Reth'Sa'âr who helps Uqa'â prepare for the nightly battle against [[lore-azuathisdty|Azu'âthis]], supplying the magical knowledge and strategic insight that sustain the cosmic order.

He keeps a distant relation with [[lore-thubaidty|Thubâ'i]], god of pleasure and prosperity. His priesthood does not disapprove of Thubâ'i's worship but ranks pleasure below the pursuit of truth. Prosperous merchants often keep smaller shrines to both gods, knowing that accounting and record-keeping, Reth'Sa'âr's domain, made their wealth possible and that the fruits of that wealth are meant to be enjoyed, Thubâ'i's message. The balance is pragmatic, and it reflects much of Aû'Khelâthu's working philosophy.
