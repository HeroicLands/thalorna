A river pilot comes up the stair of a [[lore-tjaqurdty|Tjaq'ûr]] temple on a still, brassy afternoon in late summer, and the Lem'Nelgir on the roof does not ask him why he has come. "You want to know whether to sail at dawn," she says, and points to the east, where a gray wall is standing over the hills. "I will tell you what the sky says, and the price is an offering thrown into the wind from this parapet. But do not ask me to turn the storm. Nobody turns him. Ask for the nerve to be where you need to be when it breaks."

Tjaq'ûr, the **Bringer of Tempests**, is the god of storms, wind and the ferocious power of nature at its most violent and transformative. The [[lore-khelathiclt|Khelâthi]] keep him distinct from the [[affiliation-azuathis|Faith of Azu'âthis]], who stands for the primordial chaos and cosmic entropy that threaten all existence. Tjaq'ûr is the destructive and purifying power of natural phenomena: the thunderhead that shatters ancient trees, the gale that drives ships onto the rocks, the lightning that burns away corruption and the wind that carries necessary change. To worship him is to accept that not all divine power flows through gentle channels. Some gods speak in thunder and fury, and what they say, though terrifying, carries wisdom for anyone brave enough to listen.

## The Sky Over the Valley

His domain is the wild weather of [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Aû'Khelâthu]]'s wider landscape: the thunderstorms that break over the river valley in late summer, the gales that churn the [[lore-vylarianclt|Vylarian]] coast into deadly swells and the squalls that sweep down from the eastern hills and flatten standing grain in an hour. The Khelâthi understand him as the breath and fury of the sky itself, at home over the cultivated countryside, the mountains, the sea and the far grazing country. His tempests remind people that nature runs on its own logic and not on human convenience. A thunderstorm that arrives without warning can wreck a caravan, and the same storm brings the torrential rains that swell the river's tributaries and freshen the upland soils. That paradox of destructive renewal is the heart of his theology.

His symbol is a serpent coiled around a lightning bolt. The serpent is an ancient emblem of elemental danger, graceful and adaptable and always ready to strike. The lightning is raw, uncontainable power, the force that splits sky and earth and leaves only scorched change behind. Together they show a god who is neither wholly malevolent nor wholly benevolent but amoral as a natural force is amoral. Tjaq'ûr does not choose to destroy; destruction is what he does, and sometimes it purifies and renews.

In the western grazing country, where the sky opens wide and thunderstorms race across the pasture with nothing to break their fury, his place in religious practice is ambiguous. The herders and traders there read weather as a matter of survival, and their respect for his power is tinged with fear but free of hatred. They call his name to turn aside threatening storms, believing that acknowledging his power might persuade him to send the fury elsewhere. Their stories tell of herding clans who survived impossible storms through prayer and offerings, and of those whose arrogance or irreverence brought a sudden bolt from a clear sky. The herders' code is never to insult Tjaq'ûr or claim to be stronger than the storm god, lest pride invite catastrophic punishment.

In the cities and settled lands his worship is more philosophical. Urban theologians debate whether his destructive power serves a cosmic function beyond destruction: whether each tempest clears away spiritual stagnation as well as physical, whether he is a cosmic purifier and whether the chaos he represents is a necessary counterweight to the order of [[lore-qearetdty|Qe'âret]]. Some priestly schools argue that without his periodic fury the universe would harden into fixed patterns, death would become eternal and change itself would cease, and so he is as essential as any other god, doing a cosmic work that mortals are slow to appreciate.

His priests study lightning strikes closely for signs and omens. A bolt that damages a corrupt official's house is read as divine judgment, wickedness punished with immediate and unmistakable force. A storm that brings life-giving rain at the moment of drought is his grudging mercy, his terrifying power turned toward nourishment. Readings like these let the Khelâthi keep a complex faith in a god who is feared and honored together, whose temple doors stand open even as people pray to be spared his worst attention.

## Worship

His worship is formal and solemn, with constant awareness that the worshiper is invoking a dangerous power. His temples stand on high points of land where lightning is likeliest to strike, because the priests hold that such places are naturally thin barriers between the mortal realm and his divine presence. The roofs are not tiled but painted with conductive metals, designed to attract lightning and channel it into the earth without damage to the structure. Over time the channels become scorched and scarred, and the patterns they form are read as the god's own handwriting, messages written in burn-marks.

The chief observance falls in the season of violent storms, usually the change between seasons when temperature swings make the air unstable. For those weeks the priests make daily offerings of incense and flowers, hurling them into the wind from the temple's highest points and trusting the storm winds to carry them to the god. The gestures are dramatic and forceful, respect for a wild and untamed power. The hymns do not beg for mercy; they acknowledge his supremacy and the inevitability of change.

People facing particular danger from storms visit his temples to make personal offerings: sailors preparing for the [[place-vylarianse|Vylarian Sea]] run, merchants crossing the exposed western grazing country, soldiers marching into highland terrain where thunderstorms break without warning, and rivermen out in the season when sudden squalls churn the valley. Their prayers do not ask him to stop the storm. They ask for the courage and wisdom to survive it. One such prayer runs:

> Lord of Tempests, your power shakes heaven and earth. I do not ask that you spare me, for I am dust before your fury. Grant me instead the fortitude to endure, and if I must fall, grant that my death not be in vain but serve some greater purpose in your cosmic design.

## Organization

The priesthood is smaller and more specialized than those of order-minded gods like [[lore-wethurdty|Wethûr]] or Qe'âret. The Thâz'Lekhau (High Priest) typically holds both religious and advisory roles at the [[lore-garauu|Gar-Aû]]'s court, as a specialist consultant on war, frontier campaigns and crisis management, because his domain reaches past storms into strategic conflict and the chaos of warfare, both understood as the god's disruptive, change-bringing power.

The Lem'Nelgir (ordained priests) train in theology and in practical knowledge. They study meteorology and can forecast the weather, which makes their predictions valuable to traders and the military. They learn to read lightning scars and storm patterns, developing an almost mystical skill at finding meaning in natural events. Some also serve as military strategists, advising commanders on the best timing for a campaign by astrological and storm-pattern alignments that suggest the god's favor.

The Wazu (acolytes) do the dangerous work of temple maintenance. They climb the high roofs to tend the lightning-conducting channels and record each new scorch-pattern in detailed drawings. A strike during maintenance could kill an acolyte instantly, yet the work is a great honor, a chance to serve the god most directly. Those who survive years of it gain enormous prestige in the priesthood, and many rise to significant authority.

His priesthood is on tense terms with that of Qe'âret, the goddess of order. Both teach in theory that order and chaos serve complementary cosmic functions. In practice Qe'âret's priests often argue for cautious approaches that preserve stability, and Tjaq'ûr's argue for embracing necessary change and renewal through destruction. The friction keeps Khelâthi society from stagnating entirely or sliding into chaotic violence.

## Relations

Tjaq'ûr stands directly against the [[affiliation-azuathis|Faith of Azu'âthis]], god of primordial chaos. Both are destructive, and they differ at the root: [[lore-azuathisdty|Azu'âthis]] is mindless entropy, the unmaking of all things, while Tjaq'ûr is creative destruction, the clearing of the old to make room for the new. His priests teach explicitly that he wars on Azu'âthis and that every storm he brings is a small victory in an eternal struggle against total dissolution.

With the [[affiliation-uqaa|Faith of Uqa'â]], the solar god, the relation is constant negotiation. [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]] brings heat and light and Tjaq'ûr brings wind and storm, and together they make weather. Many Khelâthi hold that the greatest storms arrive when Uqa'â's heat builds to unbearable intensity and divine power overflows the boundaries of order, and they read Tjaq'ûr's tempests then as a necessary release of cosmic pressure, a balancing mechanism. Some theologians argue that the two are not enemies but complementary expressions of cosmic power, order and chaos, heat and wind.

He also keeps a complex relation with the [[affiliation-qearet|Faith of Qe'âret]]. Both understand that universal law and harmony need opposing forces to take part. Qe'âret's domain is order and justice and Tjaq'ûr's is change and transformation. Without him her order would turn tyrannical and stagnant, and without her his power would dissolve into meaningless entropy. The highest teaching in Khelâthi temples is that the two are locked in an eternal cosmic dance, neither wholly winning nor wholly losing, and that the tension between them holds the universe in balance.
