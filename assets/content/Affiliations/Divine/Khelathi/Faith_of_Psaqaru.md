"Put your hand on the corner," a Lem'Nelgir of [[lore-psaqarudty|Psaq'âru]] tells the newest mason on a temple site, and holds a plumb line against the wall beside it. "Does it sit true? Then the god's hand is on it. If it does not, the error is not in the engineering. It is in you, and the god sees it, and so will the next thousand years."

Psaq'âru is the supreme architect of creation, the divine craftsman whose hands and mind shaped the cosmos from formless void. Temple theology reads the universe as his masterwork: every mountain, every star and every creature exists because he conceived its form and brought it into being through perfect artistry. He is shown as a figure of serene focus, often with hammer and chisel in hand, and sometimes as something more abstract, the invisible force of creative intention that works in the world through human hands. His symbol, a chisel carving a star, joins human craft to divine inspiration at the moment the transcendent becomes material.

His faith teaches that _creation did not stop at the world's beginning_. Every builder who raises a wall, every artisan who coaxes beauty from raw material and every architect who plans a city not yet built is taking part in his ongoing work. He does not simply grant success to craftspeople. He grants inspiration: the ability to see potential in stone and wood, to understand proportion and harmony, and to perceive the hidden forms waiting inside materials. Those devoted to him hold that excellence in craft is worship, and that the pursuit of perfection in one's work is love of the divine made practical.

## What the Builders Believe

The great pyramids that rise like mountains from the uplands, the temples whose columns seem to pierce the heavens and the obelisks cut from single stones and raised with a precision that astounds later ages all count as his presence made concrete. The builders and architects regard themselves as high priests of Psaq'âru, not servants of the [[lore-garauu|Gar-Aû]], and their works as prayers in stone. The precision is spiritual discipline as much as technical necessity: a corner that is not perfectly square or a column that is not precisely vertical is a failure of devotion, not merely of engineering.

The miners of the eastern hills, who dig out the stone and metals that feed the civilization, pray to him in particular. They take their work, dangerous, exhausting and essential, as a share in his creative labor. Every stone taken out is raw material awaiting transformation, and every vein of copper or gold is Psaq'âru revealing treasure. The mines are sacred ground, and miners keep small shrines deep underground where they leave oil and incense before they start work.

The visual arts follow the same belief. [[lore-khelathiclt|Khelâthi]] tradition is conservative in its forms and conventions, yet it reaches a highly developed aesthetic that honors Psaq'âru through discipline and perfection. Sculptors, painters and decorators follow strict rules of proportion, perspective and subject, and within them achieve real sophistication, because true freedom lies in mastering form and the artist serves the god best by reaching perfection within the established channels.

## Worship

His worship is about excellence, dedication and the sacred nature of craft. Artisans of every kind make offerings at his temples: sculptors give their finest work, made as a religious donation; metalworkers bring pieces of perfect craftsmanship; architects present detailed plans for buildings still to be raised. The gifts hang in the temple galleries, where they inspire contemplation and teach aspiring craftspeople.

His major festivals mark the start and the finish of a significant building. When a major temple, palace or public structure is to be raised, the priesthood holds elaborate ceremonies at which the Gar-Aû and the master builders together invoke the god's blessing, asking that the work go forward without accident, that the architects' vision be fully realized and that all who labor have safe passage and satisfaction in their part. At completion the celebrations are larger still; a whole region may gather to see a new temple dedicated or an obelisk raised, and each person there understands themselves as part of the god's continuing work.

At home a craftsperson keeps a personal shrine where the tools of the trade sit beside a religious image. A mason sets a perfectly proportioned stone beside an image of Psaq'âru, a metalworker displays his finest piece as an offering, and an architect keeps models of the best designs in a shrine room. Many professionals clean and arrange their tools every morning before they start, a meditative act that centers them and turns the mind toward the day's creative problem.

## Organization

The priesthood is relatively small beside other temples, because the god is worshiped in workshops, building sites and quarries throughout [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Aû'Khelâthu]] as much as in sanctuaries. His major temples are still centers of architectural knowledge and training. The Thâz'Lekhau (High Priest) is typically chosen from among the greatest architects of the generation and often stays involved in major construction after taking religious office, so the priesthood keeps current with every innovation in the building arts.

The Lem'Nelgir (ordained priests) specialize in aspects of the craft tradition: one oversees a school for stonemasons, another the training of sculptors, another the preservation of architectural knowledge and the keeping of plans and records. The Wazu (acolytes) are usually gifted young craftspeople whom master artisans identify and bring to the temple for spiritual training and advanced technical education. Many leave the priesthood later to become master craftspeople, spreading the god's influence as they set up their own workshops and train the next generation.

The temples hold technical knowledge as a sacred duty, with libraries of architectural plans, building records and treatises on mathematics, geometry and engineering. When a master builder dies, his plans and techniques are brought to a temple to be copied and kept for the future. That institutional memory is how Aû'Khelâthu has kept its building practice consistent and excellent across centuries.

## Relations

Psaq'âru and [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]], god of knowledge and writing, complement each other. Psaq'âru grants the creative vision and the ability to shape material reality; Reth'Sa'âr preserves how it is done through records and writing. Between them they complete the cycle of human civilization: one creates the monuments and structures, the other records that they exist and the principles of their construction so the knowledge passes safely to the next generation. Their temples exchange information and collaborate on major projects.

His tie to [[lore-thubaidty|Thubâ'i]], god of prosperity, is practical interdependence. Thubâ'i sees that craftspeople are well rewarded, that building projects get adequate resources and funding and that successful artisans enjoy what they earn. Psaq'âru's craftspeople in turn make the luxury goods, fine furnishings and beautiful objects that let Thubâ'i's devotees celebrate life in style. A wealthy merchant who commissions a sculptor to make a masterwork honors both gods: Psaq'âru through the making of something beautiful, Thubâ'i through the spending of wealth on pleasure and beauty.

Within the wider Pantheon Psaq'âru holds a respected place and is understood as serving [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]], the supreme sun god, by providing the temples and monuments through which Uqa'â's will becomes visible to mortals. One strand of theology holds that Psaq'âru actually made Uqa'â: the universe came into being through Psaq'âru's creative will, and Uqa'â is the animating principle moving through his creation. The position has never caused conflict, since the priesthoods treat the aspects of creation as complementary, each divine in its own way and each necessary to the whole.
