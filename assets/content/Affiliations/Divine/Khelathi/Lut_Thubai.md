## Overview

Lut-Thubâ'i is the temple of [[lore-thubaidty|Thubâ'i]], god of the flood and of prosperity, whose priests read the river's rise each year and set the prosperity rites that follow it. The temple's own granaries stand behind the year's account, a reserve the state draws on when the ordinary harvest falls short. A bad reading from this temple is felt everywhere downstream of it, in the price of grain as much as in the rites performed to avert worse.

## Character

"Eat first," says the Lem'Nelgir at the temple door, "and we'll talk about the god afterward." She is the temple's keeper of the festival calendar, and she means it as theology. The temple of Thubâ'i holds great feasts several times a month. Offerings of the finest food and drink the givers can manage are turned into communal meals where rich and poor, slave and free, eat together, and the poor bring beer, bread and honey while the wealthy bring rare wine and fruit. The feast is understood as taking part in the god's abundance.

The great festival comes at the height of the inundation, when the river swells and the temples organize banquets that run from sunset to sunrise. Masquerades loosen the rank of the valley for a night; merchants close their shops; farmers put down their tools; even the Gar-Aû takes part. The temple is not frivolous. Its priests read the river's rise each year, and its granaries stand behind the year's account. The music, the beer and the dancing are a form of gratitude, and the people who run them, the Wazu (acolytes) trained as brewmasters, cooks and musicians, have learned the practical craft along with the doctrine.

Hold one warning in mind. The god's anger falls on the merchant who hoards while his workers starve, and a high priest who piles up wealth without spending it on feasts finds the donations thinning. Wealth is meant to move through the temple like water through a riverbed.

## Relations

The temple's weight reaches into the state's accounts and the city's amusements.

- The state—Draws on the temple's granaries when the ordinary harvest falls short. The granary is a standing reserve, and every ministry that plans the winter's food plans on this one.
- [[affiliation-lemthubai|Lem'Thubâ'i]]—The priesthood of the same god, which keeps the flood-records the granaries and the tax rolls are set against.
- [[affiliation-linshelunthb|Lin'Shelun elu Thubâ'i]]—The performers and artists the temple funds. They sing, play and paint at its feasts and carry its name into the city.

Reth'Sa'âr's temple and this one keep a courteous distance. Educated households often keep a shrine to both gods and see no contradiction in the pair.

## Commerce and Currency

The Keeper of the Granaries holds the temple's grain, and the grain is the reserve the state draws on when the harvest falls short. Say a selat's harvest comes in thin. The Halzi'a's steward goes to the temple, and the temple opens its stores against the year's account. The High Priest's reading of the flood is the figure that the granaries and the tax rolls are set against; a bad reading is felt downstream in the price of grain as much as in the rites performed to avert worse.

The other flow is in the opposite direction, and it runs through the feasts. A merchant planning a large deal makes an offering to Thubâ'i before he signs. Brewers keep small shrines in their breweries and send the finest of their beer; vintners time their harvests by the god's festivals. The temple turns what it receives into food, wine and music for the city, and its priesthood is judged by how well the money moves.
