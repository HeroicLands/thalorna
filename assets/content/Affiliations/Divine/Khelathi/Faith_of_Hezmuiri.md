A family stands at the gate of an embalming temple with their father's body on a litter, and the Lem'Nelgir who comes out to meet them does not begin with comfort. "He will be in our hands for seventy days," she says. "Some of what you see us do will look like harm. Stay for the first prayer and you will see why it is not. Then go home and keep the house as he would have kept it; the rest is ours."

That is the work of [[lore-hezmuiridty|Hezmuîri]], the **Keeper of Transitions**, god of decay, mummification and transformation, and the compassionate guide of the dead through the passage from life into whatever follows. Most people take decay for something to deny and overcome. [[lore-khelathiclt|Khelâthi]] theology holds that _decay is a necessary and sacred transformation_, and that the body which served its owner faithfully must be changed before the spirit can go on into eternity. Hezmuîri sees that the change is done with reverence, precision and respect.

## The Jackal and the Moon

His symbol is a jackal's head cradling a crescent moon. The jackal lives in the scrublands and wild margins that flank the Khelâthi countryside, a scavenger and survivor that moves through the night with quiet dignity. The Khelâthi do not see it as a defiler; they see a creature that understands why bodies must return to the earth. The crescent is night and also renewal, the cycle by which the old becomes the new. Together they show a god nobody reviles or fears, as people fear [[lore-azuathisdty|Azu'âthis]], and everybody honors for doing a necessary and beautiful thing.

Few gods are honored so universally, for the plain reason that nearly everyone will need him. Birth belongs to [[lore-uzneradty|Uznêra]] and death to [[lore-wethurdty|Wethûr]], and Hezmuîri has the stretch between. Around that stretch the Khelâthi have built an entire industry: the mummification, the preparation of tombs, the making of burial goods, the composition of funerary texts. All of it works under his auspices, and his priesthood is correspondingly vast, wealthy and influential.

Do not confuse him with Wethûr. Wethûr presides over the spirit's journey through the trials and passages of the underworld. Hezmuîri has the body: removing the organs, drying and preserving the flesh, wrapping it in linen and precious oils, setting amulets and protective spells at the joints. A master embalmer is a devoted servant of Hezmuîri who performs priesthood through skilled hands, and the knowledge to keep a body from decay for millennia is divine service, not mere craft.

The Khelâthi call his transformation loving. He does not torture or punish the dead. He sees that each corpse is treated with dignity, that nothing of value is wasted and that the dead are prepared for their rest with meticulous care. To trust Hezmuîri is to trust that your last days will be handled with respect, and that your body will be changed into imperishable form instead of abandoned to rot in the wilderness or desecrated.

The Khelâthi dedicate enormous resources to tomb-building and burial because they believe his transformation is the transition into eternal continuation. The vast necropoli around the great cities are monuments to that belief: that the god's art will keep them whole and incorruptible, and that they will dwell in those preserved bodies forever.

## Worship

His worship is most intense around a death. The family brings the body at once to an embalming temple, where priests and skilled craftspeople of Hezmuîri take custody. The family joins rituals and prayers that commend the dead to his care and ask him to guide the transformation and the spirit's passage.

Every step of the embalming is a sacred ritual with prayers beside it. When the embalmer removes the internal organs, the most visibly destructive step, the attending priest chants to Hezmuîri and explains the act:

> Hezmuîri, Keeper of Transitions, we remove these organs not to harm but to preserve. We dry this flesh to prevent corruption. We anoint with these oils to honor the body that served this soul faithfully in life.

The words turn what could look like desecration into an act of respect.

The great annual observance is the **Festival of the Preserved Dead**, held at the height of the inundation, when agricultural work stops and the land goes into a kind of hibernation. Priests walk among the tombs in the great necropoli making offerings to the dead, and families visit their ancestors' tombs with food and drink, speak aloud to the deceased and ask Hezmuîri to keep protecting and preserving them.

His worship also reaches into daily life through amulets against decay and corruption. Nearly every household keeps small mummified objects, mice or birds preserved by techniques the priests of Hezmuîri developed, as talismans that the owner's own body will be preserved perfectly at death. They pass down through families for generations, and the fact that they never rot is taken as reassuring evidence of his power and kindness.

## Organization

Essentially every person needs these priests one day, and the preparation of tombs and burial goods creates enormous economic activity, so the priesthood of Hezmuîri is among the largest and most powerful in [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Aû'Khelâthu]]. The Thâz'Lekhau (High Priest) of Hezmuîri typically holds influence second only to the [[lore-garauu|Gar-Aû]]'s and can at times rival it, depending on the political alignments of the day and the strength of the individual priesthoods.

Beneath the High Priest an extensive hierarchy of Lem'Nelgir (ordained priests) manages the specialties of the domain. Some are scholars and ritualists who chant the required prayers and supervise the complex ceremonies. Some are master embalmers who have spent decades perfecting the technical knowledge to preserve bodies perfectly. Some run the vast facilities themselves: the drying beds where bodies lie in natron, the workshops where linen is prepared, the chambers where aromatic oils and ointments are made.

Wazu (acolytes) begin young and spend years on the technical and spiritual sides of mummification. One may apprentice under a master embalmer and learn the anatomy and manual skill; another may train as a ritualist and scholar and study the funerary texts and prayers. The most capable rise to Lem'Nelgir and to leadership.

One feature sets this priesthood apart: family inheritance plays a large part in succession. Master embalmers often teach their children and grandchildren, and so family dynasties of religious specialists arise. The skills are understood as sacred knowledge, and a family's accumulated expertise is held to be divinely bestowed. Some of the greatest embalming families in Aû'Khelâthu have kept their practice for generations, each adding refinements to what the ancestors passed down.

## Relations

Hezmuîri and Wethûr, the [[affiliation-wethur|Lord of Silent Passage]], are complementary, and temple theology teaches that their work cannot be separated. Hezmuîri transforms and preserves the body; Wethûr guides the spirit through its trials and its rest. Without the careful preservation the spirit would have no anchor for eternal existence, and without Wethûr's guidance even a perfect body would mean nothing.

His bond with the [[affiliation-uznera|Faith of Uznêra]], goddess of healing and renewal, brackets the body's whole existence: Uznêra protects and heals in life, and Hezmuîri preserves and transforms after death. Many temples keep shrines to both, and priests teach that trust in Uznêra in life and trust in Hezmuîri at death gives continuity across the boundary between existence and eternity.

[[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]], god of wisdom and knowledge, keeps what embalmers learn. His sacred ibis-quill has recorded countless refinements to the craft, and new embalming techniques are understood as discoveries made possible by his blessing. Priests of the two gods work closely, with Reth'Sa'âr's scholars documenting and analyzing what Hezmuîri's practitioners do.

[[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]], the solar god, is protective of the work, since his heat helps dry the body before natron is applied. Priests make offerings to both gods as part of the embalming ritual, thanking Uqa'â for the preserving heat of his light and Hezmuîri for the skill to use that heat well.
