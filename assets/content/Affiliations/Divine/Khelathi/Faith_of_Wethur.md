On the yearly day for offerings a household priest of [[lore-wethurdty|Wethûr]] stands at the door of a family tomb while a grandmother sets out bread, beer and flowers and her grandson fidgets behind her. "Speak to your great-grandfather," the priest tells the boy. "Tell him what the house has done this year. He is listening, and he is not frightened of you, and you should not be frightened of the door. Everyone who has ever lived has gone through it. It is a well-kept road, and there is a guide on it."

The guide is Wethûr, the **Lord of Silent Passage**, who leads souls through the underworld, keeps the tombs and guards the boundary between the living world and the realm of the dead. He is impartial and inevitable, neither cruel nor kind. As the sun sets each day into the western horizon, every mortal passes westward in the end into his realm, and nobody escapes him, bargains with him or cheats his dominion. Yet he is a god of order and not of terror. He sees that the dead pass in order, that souls are properly guided and that the tombs of the righteous stay inviolate.

## What Death Means Here

In the theology of [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Aû'Khelâthu]] death is a transition, perhaps the most important one in existence. The [[lore-khelathiclt|Khelâthi]] understand that a person's true life continues after death, in blessing or damnation, depending on how they lived and how they were prepared for the passage. Wethûr's work is to ease that transition. He keeps the pathways of the underworld safe, guards against anyone who would disturb the rest of the dead and ensures that the souls of the righteous find their way to eternal peace in the blessed [[place-zulaten|Zulaten]]. For the unrighteous his role is more complicated. He does not condemn anyone himself; he ensures that the soul faces proper judgment under the laws of [[lore-qearetdty|Qe'âret]], and a soul short on either of its two accounts is taken by **Álgit**, the **Devourer of the Dead**, rather than passing on.

Outsiders find the Khelâthi preoccupation with death morbid, and the Khelâthi call it their deepest wisdom. All earthly life is temporary, they say: the monuments one builds crumble, the kingdoms one conquers are conquered in turn and the wealth one gathers is inherited by others, while the afterlife is eternal. How one lives and the truth of one's heart matter infinitely more than any earthly achievement. His faith therefore teaches a reorientation of values: work righteously in life, prepare constantly for death, and meet the god with composure and acceptance when the time comes.

His temples are vast mortuary complexes, usually at the edge of a city or in the great necropoli where the tombs of the Gar-Aûu and nobles cluster. They are places of worship and prayer and also centers of the mortuary industry, with embalming workshops, tomb workshops and storehouses of burial goods. The priesthood employs hundreds of craftspeople, artisans and laborers who see that the dead are properly prepared for their journey.

## Worship

His worship is preparation for death and honor to the dead, and it is solemn and reflective where [[lore-uzneradty|Uznêra]]'s festivals are joyful and [[lore-uqaadty|Uqa'â]]'s ceremonies brilliant. At set points in the lunar cycle worshipers may visit a temple of Wethûr to meditate on their own mortality and rehearse their passing in the mind. Priests guide the meditation and help the faithful think about what they hope the state of their heart will be at the **Weighing**: truthful, humble and free of the burden of unresolved guilt or unfulfilled duty.

When death approaches, suddenly or slowly, the family may call a Lem'Nelgir of Wethûr to the bedside. The priest offers prayers and helps the dying person reach acceptance and spiritual preparation, and his presence is meant to ease the transition, help the soul understand what is happening and begin the guidance toward the afterlife while life is still ending.

The center of his worship is the elaborate funeral rite and the later care of the dead. The body goes to the mortuary temples of Wethûr, and the embalming begins as a sacred ritual and not a mere practical preservation. Over days or weeks, by the deceased's status and the family's wealth, the body is cleansed, dried, anointed and wrapped. At every stage priests perform rituals to protect the body and guide the soul: sacred texts are recited, protective amulets are placed within the wrappings and prayers ask Wethûr to receive the soul favorably.

When the embalming is done, the body is placed in a coffin, often decorated with images of the gods and texts to aid the soul's journey, and the family brings it to the tomb. There a priest performs the **Opening of the Mouth**, touching the mummy's mouth with a sacred implement while reciting prayers that restore the deceased's faculties in the afterlife, so that the soul can eat, drink, see and hear in the world beyond. Only then is the tomb sealed.

The relation between the living and the dead continues past the sealing. On set dates through the year family members return to the tomb with offerings of food, drink and flowers. They speak to their dead, ask for intercession with the gods and assure them of continuing love and respect. The dead stay active in the lives of the living and can bless or curse their descendants according to how well they are honored and remembered.

## Organization

A Thâz'Lekhau (High Priest) heads the priesthood and oversees the mortuary temples and the vast mortuary industry. The Thâz'Lekhau keeps the standards of sacred practice and sees that all the dead, whatever their status, receive fitting preparation and burial. Below stand the Lem'Nelgir (ordained priests), who oversee individual mortuary temples and perform the rites of embalming and burial.

These priests work closer to the realities of death and decay than most. A Lem'Nelgir must be trained in theology and ritual and in the practical techniques of mummification: removing the organs, treating the body with natron salt and special oils, wrapping and positioning the mummy. The priesthood therefore joins spiritual authority to technical knowledge.

The Wazu (acolytes) train for that double charge. They study the sacred texts and learn the prayers that guide souls, and they apprentice under master embalmers to learn the technical processes that preserve the body. Some never reach full priesthood and become master embalmers, called Wer'Khat, who gain significant status and wealth from their skill.

The mortuary temples also employ vast numbers of workers: stonemasons and sculptors who make coffins and tomb decorations, artists who paint the sacred texts and images that aid the soul, laborers who maintain the tombs and guard them from grave robbers, and scribes who record burials and keep the genealogies of the dead. The complexes are massive economic enterprises as well as centers of spiritual practice.

## Relations

Wethûr's priesthood works closely with the [[affiliation-qearet|Faith of Qe'âret]]. Wethûr does not judge the dead; that belongs to Qe'âret. He ensures that the soul appears before her in proper condition and with proper preparation, and that after judgment it is guided to its destiny. The two Thâz'Lekhau consult regularly on the afterlife and the proper preparation of souls for judgment.

His tie to the [[affiliation-uznera|Faith of Uznêra]] is the tie of life's two ends. Uznêra stands for life, fertility and renewal, and Wethûr for the necessary completion of life's cycle; in Khelâthi theology death is the culmination and transition of life and no opposite to it. Her priestesses attend the living through birth and the trials of earthly life, and his priests guide them through the final transition. In mass death or epidemic the two priesthoods work together, her priestesses healing and his priests preparing those who cannot be healed for their final passage.

He works in close alliance with the [[affiliation-hezmuiri|Faith of Hezmuîri]], god of mummification and decay. [[lore-hezmuiridty|Hezmuîri]] oversees the body's physical transformation, the decay and preservation that must occur, and Wethûr the soul's spiritual transformation and guidance. Hezmuîri is the god of the technical processes of mummification, Wethûr the god of the soul's journey and the tomb's sanctity. The two priesthoods work closely together in the mortuary temples, with Hezmuîri's priests doing the practical embalming and Wethûr's performing the spiritual rites.

His priesthood stands against grave robbers, desecrators and anyone who disturbs the peace of the dead. Grave robbery is cosmic blasphemy as much as theft, since it breaks the order he keeps and threatens the soul's peaceful rest. The priesthood actively pursues robbers, and those caught face severe punishment under Khelâthi law, often death or mutilation.

His faith gives Aû'Khelâthu much of its cultural identity. The care of the dead, the preservation of bodies, the building of magnificent tombs and the continual upkeep of ties between living and dead all flow from his theology. Other civilizations may call it an unhealthy obsession with death, and the Khelâthi call it the wisdom of knowing where true value lies: in the eternal life to come, not in temporary earthly achievement.
