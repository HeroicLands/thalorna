Psaq'âru stands as the supreme architect of creation, the divine craftsman whose hands and mind shaped the cosmos from formless void. In temple theology, the universe itself is understood as Psaq'âru's masterwork—every mountain, every star, every creature exists because Psaq'âru conceived its form and brought it into being through perfect artistry. He is depicted as a figure of serene focus, often shown with hammer and chisel in hand, or sometimes as a more abstract presence—the invisible force of creative intention that manifests in the world through human hands. His symbol, a chisel carving a star, represents the marriage of human craft with divine inspiration, the moment when the transcendent becomes material and perfect.

The faith of Psaq'âru teaches that creation did not cease at the world's beginning but continues eternally. Every builder who raises a structure, every artisan who shapes raw material into beauty, every architect who envisions cities yet unbuilt participates in Psaq'âru's ongoing work. The god does not merely grant success to craftspeople; he grants inspiration itself, the ability to see potential in stone and wood, to understand proportion and harmony, to perceive the hidden forms waiting within materials. Those devoted to Psaq'âru believe that excellence in craft is an act of worship, that the pursuit of perfection in one's work is a direct expression of love for the divine.

The monumental architecture for which Aû'Khelâthu is legendary throughout the known world—the great pyramids that rise like mountains from the uplands, the temples whose columns seem to pierce the heavens, the obelisks carved from single stones and erected with precision that astounds later ages—all are understood as manifestations of Psaq'âru's presence. The builders and architects of these works view themselves not as servants of the Gar-Aû but as high priests of Psaq'âru, their works prayers made concrete. The precision required in Khelâthi architecture is not mere technical necessity but spiritual discipline; a corner that is not perfectly square, a column that is not precisely vertical, represents a failure not merely of engineering but of devotion.

The miners of the eastern hills, who extract the stone and metals that feed civilization, make their prayers specifically to Psaq'âru. They understand their work in the mines—dangerous, exhausting, and essential—as participating in the god's creative work. Every stone extracted is a raw material waiting for transformation, every vein of copper or gold discovered is Psaq'âru revealing treasure. The mines themselves are considered sacred spaces, and the miners maintain small shrines deep underground where they leave offerings of oil and incense before beginning their work.

The visual arts tradition of Aû'Khelâthu, though conservative in its adherence to established forms and conventions, achieves a highly refined and developed aesthetic that is understood as honoring Psaq'âru through discipline and perfection. Sculptors, painters, and decorators follow strict rules governing proportion, perspective, and subject matter, but within these constraints achieve remarkable sophistication. This approach reflects the belief that true freedom lies in mastering form, not in abandoning it—the artist serves Psaq'âru best not by indulging every impulse but by achieving perfection within established channels.

## Worship

The worship of Psaq'âru emphasizes excellence, dedication, and the sacred nature of craft. Artisans and craftspeople of all kinds make offerings to his temples—sculptors offer examples of their finest work, commissioned specifically as religious donations; metalworkers bring items of perfect craftsmanship; architects present detailed plans for buildings to be constructed. These offerings are displayed in temple galleries, creating spaces of remarkable beauty that inspire both spiritual contemplation and technical education for aspiring craftspeople.

The major festivals honoring Psaq'âru occur at the beginning of significant building projects and at the moment of completion. When a major temple, palace, or public structure is to be constructed, the priesthood of Psaq'âru conducts elaborate ceremonies where the Gar-Aû and master builders together invoke the god's blessings, requesting that the work proceed without accident, that the vision of the architects be perfectly realized, and that all who labor be granted safe passage and satisfaction in their contributions. Upon completion, even larger celebrations occur; the entire region may gather to witness the dedication of a new temple or the raising of an obelisk, understanding themselves as part of Psaq'âru's continuing creative work.

Private worship typically involves the maintenance of personal shrines where craftspeople keep tools of their trade alongside religious images. A mason places a perfectly proportioned stone beside an image of Psaq'âru; a metalworker displays an exceptional piece of work as an offering; an architect keeps models of their finest designs in a shrine room. Many professionals engage in a daily ritual of cleaning and arranging their tools before beginning work, treating this preparation as a meditative act that centers them spiritually and focuses their mind on the day's creative challenges.

## Organization

The priesthood of Psaq'âru is relatively small compared to some other temples, as the god's worship is conducted not merely in sanctuaries but in workshops, construction sites, and quarries throughout Aû'Khelâthu. However, the major temples of Psaq'âru serve as centers of architectural knowledge and training. The Thâz'Lekhau, the High Priest, is typically chosen from among the greatest architects of the generation, and often continues to be involved in major construction projects even after assuming religious office. This ensures that Psaq'âru's priesthood maintains current knowledge of all innovations and developments in the building arts.

The Lem'Nelgir, ordained priests of Psaq'âru, typically specialize in different aspects of the craft tradition. One priest might oversee a school for stonemasons, another the training of sculptors, another the preservation of architectural knowledge and the maintenance of plans and records. The acolytes, or Wazu, are usually gifted young craftspeople identified by master artisans and brought into the temple to receive both spiritual training and advanced technical education. Many Wazu eventually leave the priesthood to become master craftspeople themselves, spreading Psaq'âru's influence throughout the civilization as they establish their own workshops and train the next generation.

The temples of Psaq'âru function as repositories of technical knowledge, maintaining libraries of architectural plans, building records, and treatises on mathematics, geometry, and engineering. The priesthood sees preservation of this knowledge as part of their sacred duty; when a master builder dies, his plans and techniques are brought to the temples to be copied and preserved for future generations. This institutional memory has allowed Aû'Khelâthu to maintain remarkable consistency and quality in its building practices across centuries.

## Relations

Psaq'âru maintains a relationship of mutual complementarity with [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Reth'Sa'âr]], god of knowledge and writing. While Psaq'âru grants the creative vision and the ability to shape material reality, Reth'Sa'âr preserves the knowledge of how this is done through records and writings. Together they represent the complete cycle of human civilization—Psaq'âru creates the monuments and structures, Reth'Sa'âr records their existence and the principles of their construction, ensuring that knowledge passes safely to future generations. Temples of both deities often exchange information and collaborate on major projects.

The relationship with [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Thubâ'i]], god of prosperity, is one of practical interdependence. Thubâ'i ensures that craftspeople are well-rewarded for their work, that building projects receive adequate resources and funding, and that successful artisans enjoy the fruits of their labor. In turn, Psaq'âru's craftspeople produce the luxury goods, fine furnishings, and beautiful objects that enable Thubâ'i's devotees to celebrate life in style. A wealthy merchant might commission a sculptor to create a masterwork, thereby honoring both gods—Psaq'âru through the act of creating something beautiful, Thubâ'i through the expenditure of wealth on pleasure and beauty.

Psaq'âru maintains a position of respect within the greater pantheon, understood as serving [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Uqa'â]], the supreme sun god, by providing the temples and monuments through which Uqa'â's will is manifest to mortals. Some theology suggests that Psaq'âru was actually the creator of Uqa'â himself, that the universe came into being through Psaq'âru's creative will, and that Uqa'â represents the animating principle that moves through Psaq'âru's creation. This subtle theological position has never caused conflict; instead, it emphasizes that the different aspects of creation are complementary, each divine in their own way, each necessary for the whole.
