Uqa'â is the primordial fire from which all creation springs. He is the sun that rises each morning, burning away darkness and shadow, bringing warmth, light, and life to all the world. Yet Uqa'â is more than merely the sun in the sky—he is the principle of illumination itself, the divine force that makes truth visible, that purifies the corrupted, and that drives back the primordial chaos that eternally threatens creation. Each day, Uqa'â undertakes his sacred journey across the heavens. Each night, he descends into the underworld to wage war against Azu'âthis, the chaos serpent, ensuring that the morning will come and that creation will continue to exist.

The worship of Uqa'â is the worship of life itself, of the perpetual triumph of order over chaos, of light over darkness. Those who follow Uqa'â are called to live with clarity, purpose, and righteous intensity. His followers believe that just as Uqa'â illuminates the physical world, a righteous person should illuminate the moral and spiritual world around them. To worship Uqa'â is to commit oneself to burning away falsehood, to cleansing oneself and one's society of corruption, and to maintaining the eternal vigilance that prevents chaos from returning.

Uqa'â's temples are oriented toward the east, and their central sanctums feature vast openings that allow the sun to flood the inner chambers during the hours before noon. The temple architects position these sanctums so that the sun's rays fall directly upon the altar precisely at solar noon, creating a moment of perfect illumination. Worshippers gather at these moments to feel the warmth of the god's presence directly upon them. The heat of the sun is understood not as hostile but as purifying—it burns away weakness, indulgence, and spiritual corruption.

The priesthood of Uqa'â has been described by foreign observers as the second most powerful institution in Aû'Khelâthu, rivaled only by the priesthood of Qe'âret in authority and influence. The Thâz'Lekhau of Uqa'â is among the most politically influential figures in the entire empire. More than one Gar-Aû has owed his throne to the support of Uqa'â's priesthood. When the solar priesthood withdraws its blessing, kingdoms shake. When it grants its endorsement to a new Gar-Aû, even the most ambitious rival commanders rarely resist. The reason is theological: the Gar-Aû is understood to be Uqa'â's earthly manifestation, the god's representative in the physical world. A Gar-Aû lacking the support of Uqa'â's priesthood is a Gar-Aû lacking legitimacy itself.

## Worship

The greatest festival of Uqa'â's faith occurs on the summer solstice, when the sun reaches its highest point in the sky and its rays are most intense. On this day, all work ceases. The people gather at temples and public spaces to greet the rising sun and to celebrate the triumph of light. Priests lead elaborate ceremonies in which the Gar-Aû himself (or in some cases, the Thâz'Lekhau serving as his proxy) offers the first grains of the new harvest to Uqa'â. The ceremony emphasizes gratitude for Uqa'â's protection during the past year and petitions for his continued blessing during the year to come.

Daily worship of Uqa'â involves saluting the sun as it rises and as it sets. Many Kemetians begin their day by facing east and offering a simple prayer of thanks and commitment: "Uqa'â, illuminator of all things, as you bring light to the world, so shall I bring light to my duties. As you burn away shadow, so shall I burn away falsehood from my words and actions." In temples, the priests perform more elaborate rituals, including the lighting of sacred fires at dawn and the pouring of libations at sunset.

One of the most important rituals in Uqa'â's faith is the purification ceremony. Those who have committed sins or been exposed to spiritually corrupting influences may come to a temple of Uqa'â to undergo ritual cleansing. The ceremony involves standing in direct sunlight while a priest anoints the supplicant with sacred oils and recites prayers of purification. The belief is that Uqa'â's light will burn away the corruption, leaving the person spiritually renewed. These ceremonies are particularly important for warriors who have killed in combat; though the killing may have been justified, it still leaves a spiritual stain that must be cleansed before the warrior can participate fully in temple life.

## Organization

The priesthood of Uqa'â is hierarchically organized with the Thâz'Lekhau at its apex. This High Priest holds extraordinary political and spiritual authority. Historically, the choice of a new Thâz'Lekhau for Uqa'â has been decided by the sitting Gar-Aû in consultation with the outgoing Thâz'Lekhau, though occasionally the priesthood itself has asserted the right to choose. These contests for succession have, on rare occasions, led to open conflict between the throne and the temple.

Below the Thâz'Lekhau stand multiple Lem'Nelgir, the ordained priests who serve as temple administrators, teachers, and ritual leaders. The priesthood of Uqa'â requires extensive training in theology, astronomy, and natural philosophy. Young priests study the patterns of the sun's movement through the heavens, learning to predict its paths with precision. This knowledge is both practical (for maintaining the temple's solar observatories) and spiritual (for understanding Uqa'â's divine will as expressed through the heavens).

The Wazu acolytes serve apprenticeships that may last a decade or more. They learn to maintain the sacred fires, to perform basic rituals, and to understand the theological foundations of Uqa'â's faith. The most promising Wazu are trained in the secret mysteries of the faith—hidden knowledge about the nature of Uqa'â's nightly battle against chaos and the hidden names of power through which the priesthood communes with the god.

The priesthood also employs countless minor functionaries: temple servants, builders, astronomers, and record-keepers. The temples of Uqa'â serve not only as places of worship but as centers of astronomical learning. The priests maintain observatories from which they track the movements of the sun, moon, and stars, using this knowledge both to refine their understanding of the cosmos and to make practical predictions about seasonal changes that affect agriculture and trade.

## Relations

The Faith of Uqa'â maintains its closest alliance with [[affiliation-maat|Faith of Qe'âret]]. Truth and light are complementary principles; where Uqa'â burns away shadow and deception, Qe'âret ensures that truth is immutable and that justice is upheld. The Thâz'Lekhau of Uqa'â and the Thâz'Lekhau of Qe'âret confer regularly on matters of theological significance and imperial importance. When conflicts arise between the priesthoods of other deities, Uqa'â and Qe'âret often work together to maintain order and ensure that divine law is respected.

Uqa'â stands in protective alliance with [[affiliation-iset|Faith of Uznêra]]. Uznêra's principle of renewal through balance finds its complement in Uqa'â's power to cleanse and purify. Uqa'â's heat ripens the crops that Uznêra's followers nurture; Uznêra's regenerative power ensures that the world can survive the sometimes harsh intensity of Uqa'â's light. The two faiths celebrate their alliance during the spring renewal festivals, when life returns to the land and Uqa'â's warmth becomes a blessing rather than a trial.

The priesthood of Uqa'â wages an unrelenting battle against the forces of chaos, particularly against the worship of Azu'âthis. This is not a mere theological disagreement but an existential struggle. Each night, Uqa'â descends into the underworld to combat the chaos serpent directly. The priesthood of Uqa'â understands itself as Uqa'â's earthly army in this cosmic war. They work actively to suppress chaos cults, to purify those who have been spiritually contaminated by exposure to chaotic forces, and to maintain the vigilance necessary to prevent chaos from returning to the world.

Unlike the Aurèldían pantheon, the Faith of Uqa'â contains no schism, no division into light and dark sects. The solar priesthood is unified in its theology and purpose. The god's nature is singular: he is fire as purification and illumination, never as destruction or domination. Some foreign observers find this unity suspicious, suggesting that the priesthood maintains hidden mysteries or that a latent heresy may emerge. But the Kemetians themselves insist that this unity is itself one of Uqa'â's greatest gifts to Aû'Khelâthu—a priesthood undivided, a faith unambiguous, a purpose clear and absolute.
