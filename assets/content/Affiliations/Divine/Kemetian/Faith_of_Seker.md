Sēker is the god who guides souls through the underworld, the keeper of tombs, and the guardian of the boundary between the living world and the realm of the dead. He is neither cruel nor kind, but rather impartial and inevitable. As the sun sets each day, disappearing into the western horizon, so too does every mortal eventually pass westward into Sēker's realm. There is no escaping him, no bargaining with him, no cheating his dominion. Yet Sēker is not a god of terror but of order. He ensures that the passage of the dead is orderly, that souls are properly guided, and that the tombs of the righteous remain inviolate.

In the theology of Ta'Kheperu, death is not an ending but a transition—perhaps the most important transition in existence. The Kemetians understand that the true life of a human being continues after death, either in blessing or in damnation, depending on how one lived in the mortal world and how one was prepared for the passage. Sēker's role is to facilitate this transition. He keeps the pathways of the underworld safe, he guards against those who might disturb the rest of the dead, and he ensures that the souls of the righteous find their way to eternal peace in the blessed Duat. For the unrighteous, his role is more complicated; he does not personally condemn, but rather ensures that the soul faces proper judgment according to the laws of Má'át.

The Kemetian obsession with death might appear morbid to outsiders, but the people of Ta'Kheperu understand it as the deepest wisdom. All earthly life is temporary, they say. The monuments one builds will eventually crumble. The kingdoms one conquers will eventually be conquered. The wealth one accumulates will eventually be inherited by others. But the afterlife is eternal. The way one lives, the truth of one's heart—these things matter infinitely more than any temporary earthly achievement. Sēker's faith thus teaches a profound reorientation of values: work righteously in life, prepare yourself constantly for death, and when the time comes, meet the god with composure and acceptance.

The temples of Sēker are vast mortuary complexes, often located at the edges of cities or within the great necropoli where the tombs of Per-Aás and nobles are clustered. These temples serve multiple functions: they are places of worship and prayer, but also centers of the mortuary industry—embalming workshops, tomb workshops, and repositories of burial goods. The priesthood of Sēker employs hundreds of craftspeople, artisans, and laborers who work to ensure that the dead are properly prepared for their journey to the afterlife.

## Worship

The worship of Sēker is centered on preparation for death and the honoring of the dead. Unlike the joyful festivals of Íšét or the brilliant ceremonies of Rā, Sēker's worship is marked by solemnity and reflection. At specific times of the lunar cycle, worshippers may visit a temple of Sēker to meditate on their own mortality and to mentally rehearse their eventual passing. Priests guide these meditations, helping the faithful contemplate what they hope will be the state of their heart at the weighing—truthful, humble, and free from the burdens of unresolved guilt or unfulfilled duty.

When death approaches, whether suddenly or slowly anticipated, the family of the dying person may call for a Hem'Netjer of Sēker to attend them. The priest will come to the deathbed to offer prayers and to help the dying person achieve a state of acceptance and spiritual preparation. The priest's presence is meant to ease the transition, to help the soul understand what is happening, and to begin the process of guidance toward the afterlife even as life is ending.

Most importantly, Sēker's worship centers on the elaborate funeral rites and subsequent care of the dead. When a person dies, the body is brought to the mortuary temples of Sēker, where the embalming process begins. This is not merely a practical preservation of the body but a sacred ritual. Over the course of days or weeks, depending on the deceased's status and the family's wealth, the body is subjected to elaborate processes of cleansing, drying, anointing, and wrapping. At each stage, priests perform rituals meant to protect the body and guide the soul. Sacred texts are recited, protective amulets are placed within the wrappings, and prayers are offered to Sēker asking him to receive the soul favorably.

After mummification is complete, the body is placed within a coffin (often elaborately decorated with images of the gods and with texts meant to aid the soul's journey). The family then brings the mummified body to the tomb, where a final ritual, the "Opening of the Mouth," is performed. A priest touches the mummy's mouth with a sacred implement while reciting prayers meant to restore the deceased's faculties in the afterlife—so that the soul can eat, drink, see, and hear in the world beyond. Only after this ritual is the body sealed within the tomb.

But the relationship between the living and the dead does not end at the tomb's sealing. On specific dates throughout the year, family members return to the tomb to leave offerings of food, drink, and flowers. They speak to their deceased relatives, asking for their intercession with the gods and assuring them of continuing love and respect. The dead are understood to remain active in the lives of the living, capable of blessing or cursing their descendants depending on how well they are honored and remembered.

## Organization

The priesthood of Sēker is organized hierarchically under a Wer'Hekau who oversees the mortuary temples and the vast mortuary industry. The Wer'Hekau is responsible for maintaining standards of sacred practice and ensuring that all the dead, regardless of status, receive appropriate preparation and burial. Below the Wer'Hekau stand the Hem'Netjer, the ordained priests who oversee specific mortuary temples and who perform the sacred rituals of embalming and funeral rites.

Unlike some other priesthoods, the priests of Sēker work in close contact with the practical realities of death and decay. The Hem'Netjer must be trained not only in theology and ritual but in the practical techniques of mummification—the removal of organs, the treatment of the body with natron salt and special oils, the wrapping and positioning of the mummy. This makes the priesthood of Sēker unique; its members combine spiritual authority with practical technical knowledge.

The Wab acolytes undergo training that prepares them for this unusual combination of sacred and practical responsibility. They study the sacred texts and learn the prayers meant to guide souls, but they also apprentice under master embalmers, learning the technical processes that preserve the body. Some Wab never advance to full priesthood but instead become master embalmers, known as Wer'Khat, who gain significant status and wealth through their skills and knowledge.

The mortuary temples also employ vast numbers of workers: stonemasons and sculptors who create coffins and tomb decorations, artists who paint sacred texts and images meant to aid the soul's journey, laborers who maintain the tombs and protect them from grave robbers, and scribes who record the details of burials and maintain the genealogies of the dead. The temple complexes are thus massive economic enterprises as well as centers of spiritual practice.

## Relations

The Faith of Sēker maintains close working relationship with [[affiliation-maat|Faith of Má'át]]. The god Sēker does not judge the dead; that responsibility belongs to Má'át. Rather, Sēker ensures that the soul appears before Má'át in proper condition and with proper preparation, and that after judgment, the soul is properly guided to its destiny. The Wer'Hekau of Sēker and the Wer'Hekau of Má'át consult regularly on matters relating to the afterlife and the proper preparation of souls for judgment.

Sēker also maintains important relationship with the faith of [[affiliation-iset|Faith of Íšét]]. While Íšét represents life, fertility, and renewal, Sēker represents the necessary completion of life's cycle. In Kemetian theology, death is not opposed to life but is rather its culmination and transition. Priestesses of Íšét attend to the living, guiding them through birth and the challenges of earthly life. Priests of Sēker guide them through their final transition. The two priesthoods work together during times of mass death or epidemic, with Íšét's priestesses offering healing and Sēker's priests preparing those who cannot be healed for their final passage.

Sēker works in close alliance with the priesthood of [[affiliation-anubis|Faith of Ánubís]], the god of mummification and decay. The relationship between Sēker and Ánubís is complementary: Ánubís oversees the physical transformation of the body—the decay and preservation that must occur—while Sēker oversees the spiritual transformation and guidance of the soul. Ánubís is the god of the specific technical processes of mummification, while Sēker is the god of the soul's journey and the tomb's sanctity. The two priesthoods work closely together in the mortuary temples, with priests of Ánubís handling the practical embalming work while priests of Sēker perform the spiritual rituals.

Sēker's priesthood stands in direct opposition to grave robbers, desecrators, and those who disturb the peace of the dead. Grave robbing is not merely theft but a form of cosmic blasphemy—it disrupts the order that Sēker maintains and threatens the soul's peaceful rest. The priesthood of Sēker actively pursues grave robbers, and when caught, such criminals face severe punishment under Kemetian law, often including death or mutilation.

The faith of Sēker thus provides Ta'Kheperu with its unique cultural identity. The elaborate care of the dead, the preservation of bodies, the construction of magnificent tombs, the continual maintenance of relationships between living and dead—all of these practices flow from Sēker's theology. Other civilizations might view this as unhealthy obsession with death. The Kemetians understand it as the necessary wisdom that comes from understanding where true value truly lies—not in temporary earthly achievements but in the eternal life to come.
