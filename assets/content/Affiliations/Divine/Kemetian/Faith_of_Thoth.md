Reth'Sa'âr stands as the custodian of all knowledge, the divine scribe who records not merely the deeds of mortals but the very workings of the cosmos itself. In the vaults of the Eternal Library—a celestial archive that exists simultaneously within and beyond the world—Reth'Sa'âr inscribes every moment of creation with his ibis quill, ensuring that nothing of importance is ever truly lost to time. He is depicted as a figure of profound wisdom, often shown with the head of an ibis or crowned with the lunar crescent, for he governs not only the moon's cycles but the rhythmic patterns by which mortals organize knowledge and time.

The faith of Reth'Sa'âr teaches that knowledge is the greatest treasure and the surest path to virtue. Where other gods grant material wealth or martial prowess, Reth'Sa'âr offers understanding—the ability to perceive truth beneath illusion, to navigate the complexities of both the temporal and divine realms. Those who devote themselves to his worship believe that through study, writing, and contemplation, they approach the divine mind. The scribal class of Aû'Khelâthu owes its very existence to Reth'Sa'âr's grace; scribes are understood not merely as administrative functionaries but as priests of the written word, preserving civilization itself through their meticulous records.

Among the urban temples, Reth'Sa'âr's sanctuaries are distinguished by their vast libraries, their walls lined with thousands of papyrus scrolls and clay tablets inscribed with mathematical treatises, astronomical observations, and sacred rituals. Young scribes spend years in apprenticeship within these temples, learning not only the mechanics of writing but the deeper philosophy that knowledge, rightly understood, leads to harmony with the divine order. The priesthood of Reth'Sa'âr is known for its intellectual rigor; they maintain schools where the children of merchants, administrators, and even nobles may study, creating a meritocratic path to advancement that is perhaps unique in Aû'Khelâthu.

Among the hill-nomads of the western steppe and those who dwell in the borderland hinterlands, Reth'Sa'âr manifests in a different aspect—the divine memory, the keeper of oral histories and sacred songs. The nomadic tribes maintain bards who are specifically devoted to his worship, carrying forward the stories of their people through recitation and memorization, believing that through their voices Reth'Sa'âr himself speaks. These oral historians are treated with remarkable reverence, for they ensure that the deeds of ancestors are not forgotten, preserved in the sacred speech that echoes across the open land.

The character of Meret-Sebek Heka'amun, the learned astrologer, follows Reth'Sa'âr with particular devotion. Her study of the heavens is understood as an act of worship, a attempt to read the divine script written across the stars. She maintains a small shrine in her home where she keeps her astronomical tables and notes, believing that Reth'Sa'âr guides her observations toward truths that benefit her community.

## Worship

The worship of Reth'Sa'âr emphasizes contemplation, study, and the careful preservation of knowledge. Regular offerings to his temples include fine papyrus, rare inks manufactured from precious minerals, and carefully transcribed copies of sacred texts. The most devoted followers engage in daily scribal practice as a form of meditation, copying passages from sacred literature or creating detailed records of their own observations, believing that the act of writing itself is a form of prayer.

Festivals honoring Reth'Sa'âr occur at the beginning of each new lunar cycle, when his dominion over time and record-keeping is most evident. During these celebrations, scribes throughout Aû'Khelâthu gather to share new knowledge—mathematical discoveries, medical observations, astronomical findings—as an offering of their intellectual labors to the god. Temples hold competitions in calligraphy and rhetoric, believing that excellence in the expression of knowledge honors Reth'Sa'âr. The people bring their children to witness these festivals, understanding them as opportunities to inspire the next generation of scholars and wisdom-keepers.

Private worship typically involves the maintenance of personal journals or record-books, where devotees document important events, spiritual insights, and practical knowledge gained through experience. Many homes contain small shrines with an image of Reth'Sa'âr and a few carefully selected scrolls representing the individual's most valued knowledge. Scholarly individuals of means often commission copies of important texts specifically to donate to temples, creating a lasting legacy of their intellectual contributions.

## Organization

The priesthood of Reth'Sa'âr follows a hierarchical structure distinct from that of martial temples. At the apex stands the Thâz'Lekhau, the High Priest or High Priestess, who typically possesses not only spiritual authority but recognized learning in mathematics, astronomy, or sacred language. Beneath them serve the Lem'Nelgir, ordained priests of knowledge who oversee specific domains—the keeper of the astronomical tables, the keeper of medical texts, the keeper of architectural plans, and so forth. Each Lem'Nelgir maintains authority over their particular archive and guides the training of younger scholars.

The acolytes in Reth'Sa'âr's temples, known as Wazu, are often carefully selected youths showing promise in intellectual pursuits. Their training is rigorous and extends for many years, encompassing not only reading and writing in multiple languages and scripts but also mathematics, geometry, and the principles of sacred knowledge. Many Wazu never advance to full priesthood but instead pursue secular careers as scribes, administrators, or scholars, maintaining their connection to the temple through continued study and pilgrimage. This system has created a vast network of educated individuals throughout Aû'Khelâthu, all maintaining some allegiance to Reth'Sa'âr's priesthood.

Temples of Reth'Sa'âr often function as centers of learning and record-keeping for their entire regions. They house the official archives where administrative records, tax receipts, and legal documents are stored and preserved. The priesthood maintains this responsibility with absolute seriousness, understanding themselves as stewards of civilization's memory. Corruption is virtually unknown within Reth'Sa'âr's priesthood, not from divine enforcement but from the internalized belief that betraying one's duty to preserve knowledge accurately is a betrayal of divinity itself.

## Relations

Reth'Sa'âr maintains a complex relationship with [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Psaq'âru]], the creator god. While Psaq'âru shapes the physical world and grants creativity to mortals, Reth'Sa'âr records and organizes what Psaq'âru has created, providing it with structure and meaning. The two deities are understood as complements—creation without knowledge is chaotic, but knowledge without creation is sterile. Temple legends depict them in constant, productive dialogue, with Reth'Sa'âr explaining the principles underlying Psaq'âru's work.

The relationship with [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Uqa'â]], the sun god, is one of reverence tinged with subordination. Uqa'â represents the ultimate authority and the divine will that moves through creation. Reth'Sa'âr serves as Uqa'â's counselor and scribe, recording his decrees and helping mortals understand the divine law that Uqa'â embodies. In temple theology, it is Reth'Sa'âr who helps Uqa'â prepare for his nightly battle against [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Azu'âthis]], providing magical knowledge and strategic insight that sustains the cosmic order.

Reth'Sa'âr maintains a distant relationship with [[affiliation-kemtnpnthn|Thubâ'i]], god of pleasure and prosperity. While Reth'Sa'âr's priesthood does not disapprove of Thubâ'i's worship, they see pleasure as ultimately of lesser importance than the pursuit of truth. However, prosperous merchants often maintain smaller shrines to both gods, recognizing that accounting and record-keeping (Reth'Sa'âr's domain) have made their wealth possible, while understanding that the fruits of that wealth are meant to be enjoyed (Thubâ'i's message). This pragmatic balance reflects much of Aû'Khelâthu's actual philosophy.
