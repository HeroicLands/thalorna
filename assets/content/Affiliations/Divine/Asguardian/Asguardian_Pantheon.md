"They are going to lose," a gudi who keeps a farm hov in the [[place-norgaad|Norgaad]] passes tells every newcomer, before he gives them a single god's name. "At the close of this age the gods who stand against the fire gho down with the world, and their heirs inherit whatever comes after. We worship them because they know it and gho out to meet it anyway. If you want gods who promise to win, gho south."

The Asguardian Pantheon is the dominant faith of the northern and coastal peoples of the [[place-ankrscntnnt|Ankaris Continent]], worshipped in various forms across the [[place-nrdlndsrgn|Nordlands]], [[place-vrystwald|Vristwald]], [[place-aelwyth|Aelwyth]] and other northern territories. In its teaching, its ten gods each govern a part of existence, and its people expect the present age to end at [[lore-aldarlok|Aldarlok]] in a battle the defending gods do not survive. An outsider remembers the faith for that expectation, and for the weight it gives a sworn word.

## What the Faith Holds

The faith begins from a threat. The traditions of the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]] in western Ankaris take the gods for the guarantors of a well-ordered world; the Asguardian tradition holds that the gods are defending the world against a catastrophe they may not prevent. The certainty of Aldarlok, the contest that closes the present age, colors every devotion. A worshipper of [[lore-thrunvalddty|Thrunvald]] honors the courage that will be needed when the fire comes, and a follower of [[lore-eidgardty|Eidgar]] practices the habits of honor that decide, on the last day, who stands against the flame and who is consumed by it.

The sagas say the Ten are ten because of a war they won, and they were not the first gods. The north counts time in world-ages that are born, live and die like people—the [[lore-aldarhringur|Aldarhringur]], the ring of ages—and the Ten were kindled from the remnant of the age before this one. Before the world stood there were gods and there were giants—the [[lore-thursaett|Thursaett]]—and the two fought until the giants broke. The beaten kin were driven off the field and shut into [[place-thursguard|Thursguard]], a plane of their own that no telling describes from the inside. One giant was killed instead of banished: [[lore-hrimthurspr|Hrimthur]], out of whose body the ten cut [[place-worldthlrn|the World of Thalorna]] (Mannguard) in the [[lore-thursblot|Thursblet]], and on the cut world [[lore-motefnirdty|Mótefnir]] pressed the first people out of clay—the [[lore-gnumborinn|Gnúmborinn]]. That is the founding act of the pantheon, and it is the only thing all ten of them have ever done together. After the victory the ten dispersed: each went his own way, and every quarrel, alliance and betrayal the sagas are made of dates from after the war, never before it.

The defending kin within the Ten are called [[lore-asvinir|Ásvinir]] in archaic liturgies, and what holds them together is common cause, not harmony: they stand against [[lore-nahilddty|Nahild]], against [[lore-svartbrandurdty|Svartbrandur]], and against the slow corruption both of them spread. The gods quarrel, keep favorites, and are neither all-knowing nor infallible. What they are is committed, and the faith measures any being's worth the same way, by what it is prepared to stand against.

The faith’s underlying cosmology is two worlds and one tree, and that much every Asguardian child learns before they can read. Mannguard is the world underfoot, the one a man is born into and works and dies in. [[place-asguard|Asguard]] is the gods' world, a plane rather than a planet and reached by no road, where the Ten keep their halls and the dead are gathered. Thursguard is the third and nothing is taught about its inside; the rune-priests say only that the giant-kin are in it and that nothing has come back out. The world-ash [[lore-heimsask|Heimsask]] stands through the two that the Ten and the living share and holds them in one growth, rooted in Asguard and crowned over Mannguard; when it burns, the story goes, both worlds burn with it. Asguard divides into four regions: [[place-eldheim|Eldheim]], the fire-land where Svartbrandur musters his host; [[place-valsal|Valsal]] and [[place-solvangur|Solvangur]], which take the honored dead between them and hold the halls [[place-valsalhall|Ódvarsholl]] and [[place-solvangurhall|Sólrunsholl]]; and the great island [[place-nulthey|Nulthey]], where the unworthy pass. What lies past that structure—how many roots the ash puts down, what the water around Nulthey is—belongs to the rune-priests and the skalds, and they do not agree.

The faithful dead divide into two broad destinies. Those who have died honorably—warriors fallen in battle, oath-keepers who died keeping their word, mothers who died in childbirth, craftsmen who fell in the defense of their work—are gathered into the halls of the defending gods, principally Ódvarsholl in Valsal and Sólrunsholl on the field of Solvangur, where they wait as the [[lore-valdrengir|Valdrengir]] for the last war. Those who died ignobly, broken their oaths, turned from their duty, or simply died of sickness without distinction pass to Nulthey, Nahild's island, where they diminish slowly until nothing remains. Nahild does not torture them; she is indifferent to them. The rune-priests teach that unworthiness is its own slow death, and that the only answer to it is a life lived with honor.

## Worship and Priesthood

"Nobody above me can tell me how to keep this hov," the Norgaad gudi says, "and nobody below me has to ask my leave to pour mead on his own horgur." Asguardian practice has no center, and the grand civic cults of the Aureldian south have nothing like its looseness. There is no pontiff over the pantheon, no continental synod and no single canon. Each faith keeps its own clergy, its own liturgy and its own ranks, and the details change from kingdom to kingdom and sometimes from jarldom to jarldom. What binds the pantheon together is a shared mythology, a shared calendar and a shared ritual grammar: the runes, the mead poured in offering, the ancestral tongue spoken at the high ceremonies, and the binding weight of the spoken oath.

Most faiths keep their clergy to a single conferring rite rather than a ladder of circles. Eight of the Ten admit, raise, and cast out through the blet—the offering and shared meal a hov's gudi grants or withholds—rising from the laity through an ordained priesthood to a high priest who consecrates hovs and performs the great blet at a season's turning. Nahild's suppressed cult confers by teaching its observances in secret rather than at any public rite, and the human clergy of the [[affiliation-bjartr|Faith of Bjart]] follow the Sinalean structure of the iltorin and the Ansorun. Outside the Ten, the scattered factions of the [[affiliation-hrimthur|Faith of Hrimthur]], each tied to its own hov and wound, confer nothing at all.

Temples are called _hovs_, and their form varies. A major hov in a royal seat may be a substantial stone hall with carved pillars, smoke-darkened rafters, and interior chambers for divination and the keeping of relics; a rural hov may be little more than a wooden shrine-house in a sacred grove. Size does not make a hov. A hov is a place where a _blet_ (sacrifice or offering) may be properly made, where a _ting_ (assembly) may gather under the god's attention, and where oaths may be sworn with binding force. A family's household may contain a smaller _horgur_—a stone altar or cairn—where the everyday offerings are made: the first pour of the day's mead, a portion of meat from the slaughter, a lock of hair before a journey.

Beside the clergy gho the _velvur_, itinerant seeresses bound to no hov, who travel between settlements offering divination, dream-reading and contact with the dead. A velva is received with great courtesy wherever she goes: she eats at the jarl's table, sleeps by the hearth, takes whatever hospitality is offered, and in return will sit upon the high seat at the heart of the hall and answer the questions the household needs answered. The velvur are revered across the whole pantheon and belong to no single god, though most regard themselves as particular servants of [[lore-solrundty|Solrún]] and [[lore-odvardty|Odvar]], who both hold the craft of seid in their keeping.

## Sacrifice, Oath, and Blet

The central ritual act in Asguardian religion is the _blet_—the shared offering and meal by which the community renews its relationship with the gods. A blet may be small, the pouring of mead at a household horgur; or it may be great, the slaughter of horses and oxen at a royal hov on the turning of the seasons, with the entire assembled warband eating the consecrated meat and drinking the consecrated ale. The distinguishing feature of blet is that what is offered is not destroyed but shared: in the faith’s understanding, the gods partake, the people partake, the dead partake, and the offering becomes the means by which the three are momentarily bound together. A blet not attended is a blet not accomplished.

The second great ritual act is the _oath_. Where the Aureldian tradition treats an oath as a private promise, an Asguardian oath is a public binding, spoken before witnesses, ideally before a hov-priest and under the god's attention, and sometimes sealed by a cut or a ring or an exchange of weapons. The faithful hold that oaths bind both parties and the gods who witness them. Breaking an oath is the single gravest offense in Asguardian religious ethics—graver than murder, graver than theft—because it corrodes the currency by which the entire society maintains its trust. A broken oath must be answered, either in restitution or in blood, and an oath-breaker unwilling to face the answer is regarded as already on the road to Nahild's gate.

The third is the _rune-casting_—the reading of the carved rune-staves to discern the currents of fate. This is properly the province of the [[affiliation-odvar|Odvar clergy]], but the practice is widespread across the pantheon in diluted forms, and many households keep a set of domestic runes for small decisions. The theological underpinning is that fate (_nelth_) is real, is layered, and is partially knowable; the runes do not predict the future but describe the shape of the currents in which a given choice will be made.

## Ceremonies and the Asguardian Year

The Asguardian calendar is organized around four great seasonal blets, supplemented by dozens of faith-specific festivals.

[[lore-jol|Jel]] (mid-winter) is the longest and most universally observed. For the twelve nights around the solstice, the hovs are kept ablaze with light, the jarls' halls hold open feast, the dead are invited to the table, and the living hold vigil against the long dark. Odvar is prominent—the [[lore-jol|Jel-Ride]] is abroad on the worst of the nights—but every faith has its place in the season. [[affiliation-thrunvald|Thrunvald's priests]] bless the hearth; [[affiliation-solrun|Solrún's priestesses]] conduct the rites of the coming year's loves and marriages; the skalds of [[affiliation-skaldscrcl|the Circle]] sing the year-long cycle of hero-sagas from first night to last.

[[lore-sumarmal|Somarmál]] (the beginning of summer, roughly the first month of the Ankarian calendar) is the blet of return—the return of warriors from winter quarters, of ships to the water, of cattle to the high pastures. The offerings are for the coming year: [[lore-frodvindty|Frodvin]] is prominent, as are Thrunvald for seafaring and [[miscgear-hornnjordur#hafvald|Hafvald]] among those coastal peoples who keep the older names. It is the traditional season for the great assemblies, the tings at which disputes are heard and oaths are taken.

[[lore-midsumar|Midsomar]] (the summer solstice) is the brightest festival, the long bonfire-night when the sun barely sets and the gods of light are at their strongest. [[lore-bjartrdty|Bjart]] is central here, and the feast is a festival of community and joy; fires are kindled on every promontory along the northern coasts, and the faithful leap the low flames for luck.

[[lore-veturnetur|Veturnetur]] (the Winter Nights, at the turn toward winter in late autumn) is the most somber of the four. The livestock that will not be kept through the winter are slaughtered in a great blet, the meat preserved, the bones read, the dead honored. Nahild's name is spoken with caution; Solrún receives the greatest portion, for she is the keeper of half the honored dead; Odvar is invoked as the wanderer of the coming long dark.

Between these four, each faith maintains its own calendar of high rites—the [Feast of Valsal]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} for Odvar, the [Reaver's Thanksgiving]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} for Thrunvald, the [Rite of Minnir's Well]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} among the rune-priests, the [[lore-fireweeks|Fire-Weeks]] of Svartbrandur's extreme ascetics, and the secret observances of Nahild's clergy which are known only to their own initiates.

## The Cosmic Struggle: Aldarlok

The defining eschatological claim of the Asguardian faith is that the present age is not eternal. At some appointed time—a time the skalds describe in terms so specific that it cannot be a myth and so elusive that it cannot be a date—the world will come to a contest from which it cannot be saved by half-measures. The chained will slip their chains, the burning realm will open, [[lore-vetrulfur|Vetrúlfur]] the Winter-Wolf will come loose, and Svartbrandur will ride out of Eldheim with his fire-kin at his heel, and the final battle will be joined.

In the standard telling, the defending gods and the Valdrengir will muster on the plain of [[place-vigvoll|Vigvoll]]. Odvar will fall to the wolf, and the wolf will fall to his son. Thrunvald will strike [[lore-heimsormur|Heimsormur]] the World-Wyrm and take nine steps and die of its venom. Eidgar will meet his long-chained adversary, the hound [[lore-dulmvargur|Dulmvargur]], and both will be destroyed together. Frodvin will fight Svartbrandur himself and be cut down, and Svartbrandur will then burn the world and everything in it, and the tree itself will fall. Out of the ashes a remnant will rise and a remade world will be kindled from the coals, but the pantheon that now stands will not stand to see it—what stands in the new age will be their heirs, the [[lore-tvelgaett|Tvelgaett]], not themselves.

This is not held to be certain in every detail. The rune-priests speak of _branchings_ in the great nelth, of choices not yet made, of the possibility—however slim—that some part of the outcome may be shifted by the conduct of those alive in the final age. But the general shape is fixed. The gods know they will die; the Valdrengir are being trained for a war they will lose; the honorable dead are being gathered for a battle that will end in fire. The theological response is not despair but defiance: to fight well in a losing cause is, in the Asguardian understanding, the highest form of virtue. It is what makes the gods worthy of worship, and it is what makes a mortal worthy of the gods' hall.

The expected end gives the faith its moral weight. A people who expect the world to end are not cynical; they are grave. The feast is sweeter for being temporary; the oath is heavier for being sworn under the shadow of the last fire; the hero is admired for going out to meet a fate he cannot escape. The wickedness of Nahild, the corruption of Svartbrandur's extreme adherents, and the slow rot of broken oaths are understood as sins and as accelerations—things that hasten the coming of Aldarlok, things that cut away at the time the defending gods still have.

## Key Rivalries and Bonds

In the sagas, Svartbrandur and Eidgar stand in permanent opposition, destruction against order and justice, and the priests of both read the quarrel as Aldarlok in small. Eidgar and Frodvin share a bond of mutual respect: Frodvin keeps the earth and the peace, and Eidgar keeps the justice that lets a peace hold.

Odvar and Solrún share the rulership of the honored dead: half of the slain gho to Valsal to serve under the All-Father, half to Solvangur to sit at Solrún's tables. This division is treated as amicable in the old tellings, but it conceals a theological tension the skalds do not always name—the old god of rune and war does not entirely trust the seid-craft that his sister-wife has made her own, and Solrún does not entirely forgive him for the ways he has misused it. They work together; they do not love each other.

Thrunvald and [[lore-velgrimurdty|Velgrímur]] are the pantheon's strangest pairing. The Trickster is Thrunvald's traveling companion in a dozen sagas, his foil, his occasional rescuer, his frequent embarrassment. The faithful of Thrunvald treat Velgrímur with suspicion bordering on hostility, but Thrunvald himself—in the mythic record—keeps him close. The rune-priests teach that Velgrímur is necessary: the world is held together as much by the cunning that bends as by the strength that breaks, and the pantheon would be poorer for his absence. At Aldarlok, however, the cunning will finally run out, and Velgrímur will stand with Svartbrandur against his kin. The faithful know this and honor Velgrímur anyway.

Nahild stands apart from the other gods, her hatred of life itself placing her at odds with the entire pantheon. In these accounts, she draws her power from [[lore-ginnauga|Ginnauga]], the Eye of the Void, a primordial artefact of raw corruption and a source of [[lore-theshadow|the Shadow]], and commands legions of undead—[[lore-nagengir|Nágengir]] and [[lore-haugverdir|Hrúmverdir]]—in her ceaseless war against the living. Her clergy are suppressed in every Asguardian kingdom; her hovs are hidden; her offerings are refused by the community. She is the only member of the Ten whose worship the pantheon itself regards as heretical, and her faith survives only because the appetites she serves—rage, grief unadmitted, envy unresolved—survive in every generation of mortals.

Mótefnir and Svartbrandur are set against each other by their own clergy and by nobody else's doctrine. The Maker puts living things into the world and leaves most of them unfinished; the Destroyer unmakes on purpose and finishes everything he touches. Neither hall claims the other is an enemy at Aldarlok, and Mótefnir's priests are quick to refuse the symmetry when it is offered to them: the god who stops working is not the god who burns the work, and treating the two as a pair flatters Svartbrandur.

In the northern creation account, the world's own substance stands outside the pantheon, and the north keeps a faith for it. Hrimthur is not one of the Ten and is not spoken of as one. He is the giant they killed at the close of the war, and the ground every hall is raised on is his body; the Faith of Hrimthur keeps that cutting open on purpose at the turn of every winter. The Ten's own clergy treat those rites as accurate rather than awkward. The founding violence is the pantheon's oldest claim and its proudest, and a hov that renews it is renewing a victory.

## Relations With Other Faiths

Across Ankaris, the Asguardian pantheon coexists uneasily with the Aureldian traditions of the south. The two pantheons share certain deities in different guises—Bjart and [[lore-zunelasdty|Zunelas]] are, explicitly, the same being under different names; Thrunvald and [[lore-brazemasdty|Brazemas]] are often identified in the syncretic theology of the border kingdoms; the Faith of Odvar and the [[affiliation-marendas|Faith of Marendas]] share a mutual respect among their priesthoods that occasionally crosses into active cooperation. On other points they differ sharply. The Aureldian faiths have no Aldarlok; their theology of death is concerned with judgment rather than defense; their pantheon is balanced where the Asguardian is embattled. Northern pilgrims to Aureldian temples often come away feeling they have been in a beautiful place where nothing is at stake.

With the peoples of the [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara Region]] and the [[affiliation-vrnkpnthn|Varnuka tradition]], Asguardian contact has been limited—geography keeps the faiths apart—but the rune-priests who have studied Varnukan texts have noted with interest the parallels between their pantheon's tension between preservation ([[lore-mahajayadty|Mahajaya]]) and destruction ([[lore-rasikaradty|Rasikara]]) and the Asguardian eschatology. No formal theological exchange has come of them.

With Sinalean worship of [[lore-goddreams|the God of Dreams]], Asguardian relations are close and old. The human worshippers of Bjart have adopted the Sinalean clergy structure outright—the only Asguardian faith to do so—and the cooperation between the elder-race priesthoods and the northern human faithful is the single most visible link between human religion and the pre-human religious traditions of Thalorna. Where the [[lore-flksinale|Sinale]] have withdrawn from most of the human world, the Bjart clergy remain a living bridge, and the north's rune-priests count that bridge as one reason their faith has not grown as provincial as, they say, the Aureldian traditions have in their southern heartland.

With [[affiliation-tharivaan|Thári'vaan]], the non-theistic tradition of [[affiliation-kalihara|Kalihara]], there is no relationship at all—Asguardian vessels do not reach that far, and Kalihara's interior does not welcome foreign priests. The rare Asguardian merchant who has seen the port city temples of [[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]] reports only that the Kaliharans "have no gods, and do not seem to miss them," which the Asguardian theologians find incomprehensible.
