In the beginning, according to Mitharan theology, there was no distinction between order and chaos, between Āsha and Druj. All was potential, all was undifferentiated darkness—not the darkness of night but the darkness of non-existence itself. Then came Míthárä, the First Light, and from her divine luminescence, the world took shape. But Míthárä did not merely create the world once and withdraw; rather, her act of creation is eternal and ongoing. The moment a seed splits and sends forth a sprout, that is Míthárä's hand. The instant a child draws its first breath, Míthárä's light enters it. When a cracked piece of clay is molded by a potter into a new vessel, that transformation is Míthárä's work. The priests of Míthárä teach that creation did not happen in some distant mythological past but continues now, at every moment, in every place. The world is not a finished thing but a perpetual act of becoming, and Āsha itself—truth and order—is only maintained through this constant renewal.

This theology places Míthárä at a unique position in the Āsháian pantheon. While other gods represent specific domains or respond to specific human needs, Míthárä is understood as the foundational power underlying all existence. The other deities are expressions or emanations of her power; Zúráthrä's water-flows are Míthárä's creativity channeled through nurturing; Araveshta's prosperity is Míthárä's generative abundance made manifest in material goods; Sirvadar's safe passages are Míthárä's renewing light guiding the way. But Míthárä herself is the source, the eternal act of becoming, the refusal to permit calcification or stagnation. In mythology, it is said that when Druj grows powerful and threatens to freeze the world into eternal sameness—the ultimate expression of chaos through crystallization rather than destruction—Míthárä moves within creation itself, and change ripples through all things. A drought becomes a flood; a peaceful kingdom finds itself in revolution; a beloved king suddenly grows old and must yield power to a younger generation. These transformations are not punishments but mercies, renewals, the cosmic necessity of preventing any single state of being from becoming permanent enough to transform into its opposite.

The theology of Míthárä is particularly influential among the nomadic peoples of Dunhara and Khazryn, where the constant movement from pasture to pasture and the endless adaptation to changing seasons have embedded the concept of perpetual renewal into the very structure of life. For these peoples, Míthárä is not an abstraction but an immediate reality—the grass that feeds the herds must be continuously renewed, water sources appear and disappear with the seasons, and entire populations must migrate to follow the herds and the weather. Yet her influence is equally powerful in the settled cities of Amradad, where her priesthood maintains an unusual focus on the role of craftsmanship and skilled labor in the work of creation. A master craftsperson is understood as a channel for Míthárä's creative power; the architect who designs a building, the engineer who builds an aqueduct, the metalsmith who forges a sword—each is participating in the divine act of bringing new forms into being. The weariness of a field that has been over-farmed, the decay of a city that no longer changes and renews itself, the spiritual death that comes from stagnation—these are understood as examples of what happens when Míthárä's renewal is disrupted or blocked by ignorance or willful denial.

## Worship

The temples and shrines of Míthárä are always constructed in proximity to places of visible growth and transformation. In the cities, they are located near gardens, markets where goods are constantly being created and exchanged, workshops where artisans labor. In the desert and steppe, Mitharan shrines mark the places where the most dramatic renewals occur—where seasonal torrents flow and transform the landscape, where oases appear and disappear, where the migration routes shift with changing climate. The most sacred Mitharan sites are those where natural and human creation intersect visibly: a city built around a garden, a farm constructed with masterful engineering to redirect a seasonal stream, a marketplace bustling with craftspeople creating new goods daily. Pilgrims travel to these places not to pray for a single specific blessing but to meditate on the nature of transformation itself.

The central ritual of Míthárä's worship is the Ceremony of Renewal, performed on the new moon of each month and more elaborately during the seasonal changes (at the equinoxes and solstices). The ritual begins, as all Āsháian worship does, with the kindling of fire, but in Mitharan ceremony, the fire is tended in a very specific way. The priest kindles the flame, then feeds it throughout the ceremony with different materials—at first dry wood, then green branches that hiss and pop, then incense that transforms into fragrant smoke. The changing nature of the flame, the way it adapts and responds to different fuel, is the visible language of Míthárä's principle. As the fire transforms each new material, the priest speaks: "What was is no more. What is shall soon be no more. From the ashes of the old comes the new. From the cooling of the flame comes the heat of life renewed. Míthárä, eternal creator, accept this offering of change and transformation."

Worshippers bring offerings related to their own work and creativity. An artisan might bring a new tool they have crafted; a farmer might bring seeds or the first harvest from a field; a child just named for the first time might be brought to the temple for the Ceremony of First Being, where water is poured over them while fire burns nearby, symbolizing their entry into the ongoing creation. The priest then asks the supplicant a question that varies depending on circumstances but always centers on the theme of renewal and change: "What have you created this month?" or "What have you transformed?" or "How have you permitted yourself to change and grow?" The answers are spoken aloud before the fire and the community of worshippers, which serves both as confession and celebration of life's ongoing transformation.

The great festivals honoring Míthárä fall at times of natural renewal. In the spring (the month of Zarathustra, corresponding roughly to the fourth month of the common calendar), the Festival of First Growth celebrates the breaking of seeds, the blooming of flowers, the renewal of life after winter. In autumn, the Feast of Harvest celebrates the culmination of the growing season but also includes contemplation of decay and return to the earth—for the harvest is also a kind of death, the cutting down of the standing grain to feed humanity. Both festivals include elaborate displays of the work of creation: master craftspeople demonstrate their skills, new buildings are officially dedicated, new government officials are inducted into office in ceremonial recognition that even the structures of human governance must be renewed and refreshed. Markets overflow with newly created goods. Artists display new works. In some cities, old buildings are deliberately demolished or radically reconstructed during these festival periods, serving as visible symbols of renewal.

## Organization

The priesthood of Míthárä is organized according to specialization rather than rank, though a hierarchical structure does technically exist. At the apex is the Mōbad, traditionally chosen from among the most accomplished Hērbads—often a master craftsperson of considerable renown or a scholar of great learning who has demonstrated creative thinking about theological matters. Below the Mōbad are the Hērbads, who typically fall into distinct categories: the Temple Keepers who maintain the shrines and perform the regular ceremonies; the Craftmaster-Priests who work within the major workshops and guilds, supervising the training of apprentices and ensuring that craftsmanship remains grounded in spiritual understanding; and the Renewal-Walkers who travel the lands as teachers and mediators, bringing the message of perpetual renewal and reconciling theological disputes.

What makes the Mitharan priesthood unique is its deep integration with the craftspeople and artists of the realm. Every major guild—the masons, the metalworkers, the weavers, the potters—has a Hērbad embedded within it, responsible for maintaining the spiritual discipline of the craft, for training apprentices in both technique and theology, and for ensuring that the work done in that guild serves the principle of Āsha (good, honest creation) rather than Druj (shoddy work, deception, the creation of ugliness). These priest-craftspeople must be highly skilled in their trades; a Hērbad of the metalworking guild must be capable of forging a sword or crafting jewelry with genuine mastery. They are paid by the guilds for this service, creating a complex relationship between the priesthood and the commercial structures of the cities.

The order maintains the most extensive educational institutions of any Āsháian cult. The Mitharan temples run academies where promising young people are trained in craft, art, engineering, mathematics, and philosophy. These academies have produced many of the greatest architects, engineers, and craftspeople of the age. The curriculum emphasizes not merely technical skill but the philosophical understanding that all craft is a form of prayer, that beauty created with intention is a direct expression of Āsha, and that the worker who creates with mindfulness and respect for the materials is participating in the divine act of creation. The order maintains detailed written records of architectural knowledge, mathematical principles, artistic techniques, and engineering innovations, accumulated over centuries and carefully guarded. Access to these libraries is a privilege jealously protected by the priesthood.

The structure of the priesthood reflects its belief in perpetual renewal: unlike other Āsháian orders, the Mitharan priesthood deliberately rotates its personnel through different temples and roles every seven years. A Hērbad might spend seven years serving as a Temple Keeper, then move to work with a guild as a Craftmaster-Priest, then become a Renewal-Walker, then return to the temples in a different capacity. This constant circulation prevents the development of rigid hierarchies and ensures that the priesthood never becomes calcified or stale in its practices. Similarly, the position of Mōbad is not held for life but for a term of twelve years, after which the priest must step down (though they often continue to serve in other capacities). This practice, while unusual and occasionally contentious when a particularly beloved Mōbad must surrender power, is justified theologically as a necessary expression of Míthárä's principle: that all things must change, that permanence in power leads to stagnation and corruption, and that renewal requires the relinquishment of authority as well as its acquisition.

## Relations

Míthárä occupies a unique position in the Āsháian pantheon as the supreme creator-deity to whom all other gods are subordinate. The priesthoods of Zúráthrä, Araveshta, and Sirvadar all acknowledge Míthárä's primacy, and theological texts commonly describe her as the "First Mother from whom all other divine powers flow." Yet this supreme position does not translate into simple dominion; rather, Míthárä's theology emphasizes complementarity and interdependence. The priesthood teaches that the other deities represent specialized expressions of Míthárä's creative power, focused in their particular domains. Without Zúráthrä's nurturing waters, [[affiliation-zurathra|Faith of Zúráthrä]], Míthárä's creative power would manifest as sterile potential rather than living actuality. Without Araveshta's capacity to celebrate and enjoy creation, [[affiliation-aravesta|Faith of Árávēštä]], the act of creating would be hollow and purposeless. Without Sirvadar's power to guide and connect, [[affiliation-sirvadar|Faith of Šírvádár]], creation would be scattered and disconnected rather than forming a coherent cosmos.

The relationship with [[affiliation-zurathra|Faith of Zúráthrä]] is particularly close, as both deities share an emphasis on sustaining and nurturing life. The two priesthoods cooperate extensively in settled agricultural communities and at oases, with Mitharan craftspeople often working to build and maintain the irrigation systems and water-works that Zúráthrä's priesthood oversees. Some theological schools argue that Míthárä and Zúráthrä are two aspects of a single divine principle—the eternal act of creation-and-sustenance—distinguished only by their specific focuses. The priesthoods of both orders work together in the cities to maintain libraries and schools, and it is common to find temples dedicated to both deities in close proximity.

With [[affiliation-aravesta|Faith of Árávēštä]], the relationship is more one of friendly rivalry. Both deities represent generosity and abundance, but Míthárä's abundance is portrayed as the abundance of potential and transformation, while Araveshta's is the abundance of material prosperity and sensory pleasure. Conservative Mitharan theologians view Araveshta with some suspicion, arguing that the pursuit of pleasure without the deeper understanding of the ongoing necessity of renewal and transformation can lead to a kind of spiritual blindness. Araveshatian priests counter that joy and celebration are themselves forms of renewal, that the feasting and festivity that honor Araveshta are necessary to sustain human spirit and therefore serve Míthárä's principle as well. The debate is conducted in learned and amicable tones, but it reflects a genuine theological disagreement about the proper balance between ascetic discipline and joyful celebration.

The priesthood of Míthárä has long served as mediator and reconciler in theological disputes within the broader Āsháian pantheon, particularly when conflicts arise between the more ascetic orders and the more indulgent ones. The Mitharan emphasis on balance, on the necessity of both creation and dissolution, both celebration and discipline, gives their priesthood a certain authority in these disputes. The Mōbad of Míthárä is traditionally consulted by the Sultanate's court when important theological questions must be settled or when conflicts between priestly orders threaten civil harmony. This role as mediator has sometimes resulted in Mitharan priests being perceived as less committed to the interests of their own order, a criticism that the priesthood meets with characteristic philosophical equanimity: that true service to Míthárä requires the capacity to see all perspectives and to understand how each represents a partial truth within a larger cosmic pattern.
