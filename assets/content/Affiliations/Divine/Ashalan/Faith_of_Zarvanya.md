Zárvanya is the goddess of dreams, visions, omens, and divine insight. She is the weaver of the threads that connect the waking world to the realm of eternal truth—that timeless space where past and future exist simultaneously, where the gods speak directly to mortal minds, and where the deepest patterns of Asha become visible to those gifted with the sight to perceive them. She is known across every major pantheon by different names—the Aureldian call her [[lore-goddreams|the God of Dreams]], the Khelathi honor her as a form of Reth'Sa'ar, the northern peoples know her by a dozen titles—but the Ashaian name alone captures her essence: she is not merely the goddess of sleep and dreams, but the Weaver, the one who spins the threads of vision into patterns of meaning.

In the desert lands where the Ashaian faith took root, dreams carry weight that civilized peoples of greener lands can scarcely comprehend. A nomadic tribe lost in the Khazryn steppe, their water running low and the stars obscured by sandstorm, will place absolute faith in the vision of their fire-keeper if Zárvanya has spoken clearly through it. A sheikh will make decisions of war and peace based on a dream vouchsafed to him in the night. A merchant will commit vast resources to a trading venture because Zárvanya showed him the path in sleep. In desert cultures, where the margin between life and death is often the width of a single choice, the ability to perceive divine guidance through dreams is not a luxury or an eccentricity—it is survival. Zárvanya does not offer comfort or escape through dreams; she offers truth, guidance, and the whispered counsel of the eternal gods.

The Ashaian understanding is that Zárvanya does not create dreams from nothing. Rather, she is a weaver who takes the threads of past and future, of cosmic pattern and individual destiny, and arranges them in such a way that the dreamer can perceive the truth encoded within them. A dream from Zárvanya is not random neurological noise—it is a message, cryptic perhaps, symbolic certainly, but absolutely meaningful. This is why dream-interpretation is elevated to the status of sacred science in Ashaian cultures. To ignore a dream vouchsafed by Zárvanya is to turn away from divine counsel; to misinterpret it is to act on falsehood and invite catastrophe.

Unlike the priests of many other deities, who are appointed or trained through formal institutional structures, the priests of Zárvanya are often called directly through dreams. A child in [[affiliation-kngdmmrdd|Amradad]] might be seized by a vivid, recurring vision—always the same sequence, the same symbols, the same impossible clarity—and when the family seeks interpretation from a Delkoshekh of Zárvanya, the priest will often declare that the goddess herself has chosen this child. The visions will intensify, becoming more frequent and more insistent, until the child's family brings them to a Dream House for formal training. Alternatively, a nomadic tribesperson might wander alone into the desert—as all young adults of both genders are expected to do—and experience a vision-quest in which Zárvanya speaks directly, showing the person's true calling and purpose. Such individuals, if their visions prove authentic, are brought into the priesthood with reverence and some awe.

## Worship

The central ritual of Zárvanya's worship is the sacred sleep. In the great Dream Houses of [[affiliation-kngdmmrdd|Amradad]] and the other settled cities of the Ashaian realms, the faithful enter specially prepared chambers at dusk. These rooms are painted with intricate iconography—stars and geometric patterns that echo the architecture of the cosmos, symbols of truth woven into the very walls. A Delkoshekh of Zárvanya kindles a fire using incense specifically prepared to induce clarity of vision: dried herbs that open the mind, aromatic oils that sharpen perception, rare unguents whose origins are closely guarded secrets of the priesthood. The faithful lie on cushions of wool, their minds cleared through meditation and prayer, and wait for Zárvanya to speak.

The goddess does not always speak clearly. Some nights yield visions of clarity—the dreamer awakens knowing exactly what must be done, seeing the pattern that had been hidden. Other nights produce only fragmentary images, symbols that must be parsed and interpreted. The next morning, the Delkoshekhs of the Dream House gather with the supplicants, and together they perform dream-interpretation—a practice that combines philosophical insight, knowledge of symbolism, astronomical lore, and pure intuition. A master dream-interpreter (known as a Rushnaveh, or "vision-reader") might spend hours unpacking the layers of meaning in a single dream, tracing the symbolism back to its roots in cosmic principle and forward to its practical application in waking life.

Not all Ashaian dreamers seek Zárvanya's counsel in the formal Dream Houses. In the nomadic camps and among the common folk of settled cities, dreaming is a natural part of spiritual life. When a significant dream comes unbidden—vivid, memorable, impossible to forget—it is understood to be Zárvanya's calling. The dreamer will perform a small ritual: they will kindle a fire or light a lamp, and as it burns, they will speak aloud the dream, recounting it in detail. This recitation is understood to anchor the vision in memory and to honor the goddess for her counsel. If the dream requires interpretation, the dreamer will seek out a trusted Delkoshekh; if it is clear, they will simply act upon the guidance received.

The Festival of the Woven Night, celebrated under the dark moon in the month when Zárvanya's influence is believed to be strongest, is an occasion of collective dreaming and shared vision. Throughout the night, the faithful gather in fire-temples and public squares, lying together under the stars. A Shekhul or Master Delkoshekh of Zárvanya leads a meditation that lasts from sunset until the first light of dawn, guiding the assembled people into a deep, receptive state of consciousness. The hope is that Zárvanya will send a collective vision—a dream shared by many people simultaneously—that conveys a message of great importance. These communal visions are understood as the most powerful guidance the goddess can offer. Some of the greatest decisions in Ashaian history are said to have been made on the basis of a collective vision vouchsafed during the Festival of the Woven Night.

Daily worship of Zárvanya is more subtle. Before sleep, the pious will often kindle a small lamp—the dream-light—and offer a brief prayer: "Zárvanya, Weaver of truth, open the gates of vision. Let me see clearly in sleep what is hidden in waking. Speak to me in the language of symbols and thread. Guide my steps tomorrow with the wisdom perceived tonight." Upon waking, before rising or speaking to anyone, a devoted worshipper will reflect on any dreams they remember, seeking for Zárvanya's message.

## Organization

The priesthood of Zárvanya is distinctive among the Ashaian cults because it is simultaneously highly formalized and deeply mystical. The formal structure reflects the institutional side: the priesthood maintains the Dream Houses, trains interpreters, and preserves the accumulated knowledge of dream-symbolism across generations. Yet within that structure, every priest is understood to be a mystic—directly touched by the goddess, capable of receiving visions, responsible for interpreting cosmic truths from the raw material of symbol and image.

At the apex stands the Shekhul of Visions, a figure rarely encountered in public life. The Shekhul of Visions maintains a small retreat, often hidden away in the highest tower of a great fire-temple or in a remote mountain sanctuary. This individual spends most of their time in meditation and sleep, allowing Zárvanya to speak through them. When major questions of religious doctrine or cosmic significance arise, the other priesthood members will petition the Shekhul of Visions for guidance. The Shekhul will enter deep sleep, sometimes for days or even weeks, and emerge with a pronouncement that carries the weight of divine revelation.

The Rushnaveh (vision-readers) form the backbone of Zárvanya's working priesthood. These are individuals—some identified in childhood, others called later in life—who possess natural gifts of interpretation and intuition. A Rushnaveh undergoes years of training in the symbolic languages that Zárvanya uses: the meanings encoded in different animals and plants, the messages conveyed by numbers and geometric patterns, the cosmic correspondences between celestial bodies and human destinies. But training alone does not make a true Rushnaveh. The goddess must confirm the calling through direct revelation. A trainee might spend an entire night in a dream-chamber and receive nothing—silence from Zárvanya—and be dismissed from training as unsuitable. Alternatively, a trainee might receive such a clear vision that their readiness is immediately apparent. Those who receive Zárvanya's direct confirmation become Delkoshekhs of Vision and are authorized to counsel the faithful.

The Roqeshekhs (initiates) of Zárvanya's order undergo a training that is both intellectual and mystical. They study the formal texts—manuals of dream-symbolism, cosmological texts that explain the structure of the realm of vision, philosophical works on the nature of truth and its revelation. But they also spend hours in meditation, learning to still the mind, to enter deep sleep voluntarily, to maintain awareness even while dreaming. The most advanced practices involve controlled dreaming—the ability to remain conscious within a dream-state, to question the visions as they unfold, to demand clarity from Zárvanya rather than passively accepting fragmentary images.

Notably, the priesthood of Zárvanya includes many women, and in some regions, women form the majority of the formal priesthood. This reflects the belief that women, who are biologically closer to the cycles of the moon and thus aligned with Zárvanya's celestial nature, are often more naturally gifted at dream-interpretation and vision-receiving. Male priests are not excluded and some are renowned for their prowess, but the priesthood as a whole is less male-dominated than the temple hierarchies of other Ashaian deities.

## Relations

Zárvanya occupies a unique position within the Ashaian pantheon as the connection between the divine realm and mortal consciousness. In this regard, she works in concert with [[affiliation-ahurdaen|Ahurdaen]], the supreme deity. Ahurdaen creates and sustains the cosmic order; Zárvanya is the means by which mortals perceive and understand that order. Without Zárvanya's visions, the commands of Ahurdaen would remain eternally hidden, unknowable. With her guidance, even the simplest person can glimpse the face of truth.

Her relationship with [[affiliation-korsadra|Khórsadra]] (Bearer of Eternal Light) is also important, though more complex. Both deities are concerned with revealing truth, but they work through different mediums. Khórsadra reveals truth through the light of reason and knowledge—the accumulated learning that can be taught and transmitted from one person to another. Zárvanya reveals truth through direct mystical experience—the vision that comes unbidden and speaks in the language of symbol. Both forms of revelation are necessary. A person who relies only on reason without mystical insight may understand the world intellectually but miss the deeper patterns of cosmic truth. Conversely, a mystic who rejects reason can be led astray by false visions and deceptive symbols. The two priesthoods work in some tension but also in complementarity, each checking the other's excesses.

Zárvanya is related to [[affiliation-ataros|Ataros]], the Sacred Flame, in that both deities facilitate revelation. But where Ataros reveals through the purification of fire—burning away falsehood and corruption—Zárvanya reveals through the gentler medium of vision. A Delkoshekh of Ataros might read the will of the gods in the pattern of flame and ash; a Delkoshekh of Zárvanya reads it in dreams. Both are paths to truth, but they appeal to different temperaments and different circumstances.

Finally, Zárvanya stands in eternal opposition to [[affiliation-angaros|Angaros]], the Shadowed Serpent. Angaros is the god of deception, confusion, and the corruption of truth into lies. He sends false visions and misleading dreams to those who would trap themselves in illusion and self-deception. The battle between Zárvanya and Angaros is fought in the realm of dream and vision: Zárvanya sends clarity and truth through dreams, while Angaros attempts to flood the dreaming-mind with lies and delusion. A person who receives a vision must always ask themselves: is this truth or deception? Did Zárvanya weave this thread, or did Angaros lay a trap? This is why dream-interpretation is so carefully studied and why masters of the craft are so highly valued—they are the defenders of truth in the realm where Angaros's deceptions run deepest.
