Zárványä is the goddess of dreams, visions, omens, and divine insight. She is the weaver of the threads that connect the waking world to the realm of eternal truth—that timeless space where past and future exist simultaneously, where the gods speak directly to mortal minds, and where the deepest patterns of Āsha become visible to those gifted with the sight to perceive them. She is known across every major pantheon by different names—the Aurèldían call her [[lore-goddreams|the God of Dreams]], the Kemetíans honor her as a form of Thōth, the northern peoples know her by a dozen titles—but the Āsháian name alone captures her essence: she is not merely the goddess of sleep and dreams, but the Weaver, the one who spins the threads of vision into patterns of meaning.

In the desert lands where the Āsháian faith took root, dreams carry weight that civilized peoples of greener lands can scarcely comprehend. A nomadic tribe lost in the Khazryn steppe, their water running low and the stars obscured by sandstorm, will place absolute faith in the vision of their fire-keeper if Zárványä has spoken clearly through it. A sheikh will make decisions of war and peace based on a dream vouchsafed to him in the night. A merchant will commit vast resources to a trading venture because Zárványä showed him the path in sleep. In desert cultures, where the margin between life and death is often the width of a single choice, the ability to perceive divine guidance through dreams is not a luxury or an eccentricity—it is survival. Zárványä does not offer comfort or escape through dreams; she offers truth, guidance, and the whispered counsel of the eternal gods.

The Āsháian understanding is that Zárványä does not create dreams from nothing. Rather, she is a weaver who takes the threads of past and future, of cosmic pattern and individual destiny, and arranges them in such a way that the dreamer can perceive the truth encoded within them. A dream from Zárványä is not random neurological noise—it is a message, cryptic perhaps, symbolic certainly, but absolutely meaningful. This is why dream-interpretation is elevated to the status of sacred science in Āsháian cultures. To ignore a dream vouchsafed by Zárványä is to turn away from divine counsel; to misinterpret it is to act on falsehood and invite catastrophe.

Unlike the priests of many other deities, who are appointed or trained through formal institutional structures, the priests of Zárványä are often called directly through dreams. A child in [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Amradad]] might be seized by a vivid, recurring vision—always the same sequence, the same symbols, the same impossible clarity—and when the family seeks interpretation from a Hērbad of Zárványä, the priest will often declare that the goddess herself has chosen this child. The visions will intensify, becoming more frequent and more insistent, until the child's family brings them to a Dream House for formal training. Alternatively, a nomadic tribesperson might wander alone into the desert—as all young adults of both genders are expected to do—and experience a vision-quest in which Zárványä speaks directly, showing the person's true calling and purpose. Such individuals, if their visions prove authentic, are brought into the priesthood with reverence and some awe.

## Worship

The central ritual of Zárványä's worship is the sacred sleep. In the great Dream Houses of [[affiliation-sultntmrdd|Amradad]] and the other settled cities of the Āsháian realms, the faithful enter specially prepared chambers at dusk. These rooms are painted with intricate iconography—stars and geometric patterns that echo the architecture of the cosmos, symbols of truth woven into the very walls. A Hērbad of Zárványä kindles a fire using incense specifically prepared to induce clarity of vision: dried herbs that open the mind, aromatic oils that sharpen perception, rare unguents whose origins are closely guarded secrets of the priesthood. The faithful lie on cushions of wool, their minds cleared through meditation and prayer, and wait for Zárványä to speak.

The goddess does not always speak clearly. Some nights yield visions of profound clarity—the dreamer awakens knowing exactly what must be done, seeing the pattern that had been hidden. Other nights produce only fragmentary images, symbols that must be parsed and interpreted. The next morning, the Hērbads of the Dream House gather with the supplicants, and together they perform dream-interpretation—a practice that combines philosophical insight, knowledge of symbolism, astronomical lore, and pure intuition. A master dream-interpreter (known as a Rūshnaveh, or "vision-reader") might spend hours unpacking the layers of meaning in a single dream, tracing the symbolism back to its roots in cosmic principle and forward to its practical application in waking life.

Not all Āsháian dreamers seek Zárványä's counsel in the formal Dream Houses. In the nomadic camps and among the common folk of settled cities, dreaming is a natural part of spiritual life. When a significant dream comes unbidden—vivid, memorable, impossible to forget—it is understood to be Zárványä's calling. The dreamer will perform a small ritual: they will kindle a fire or light a lamp, and as it burns, they will speak aloud the dream, recounting it in detail. This recitation is understood to anchor the vision in memory and to honor the goddess for her counsel. If the dream requires interpretation, the dreamer will seek out a trusted Hērbad; if it is clear, they will simply act upon the guidance received.

The Festival of the Woven Night, celebrated under the dark moon in the month when Zárványä's influence is believed to be strongest, is an occasion of collective dreaming and shared vision. Throughout the night, the faithful gather in fire-temples and public squares, lying together under the stars. A Mōbad or Master Hērbad of Zárványä leads a meditation that lasts from sunset until the first light of dawn, guiding the assembled people into a deep, receptive state of consciousness. The hope is that Zárványä will send a collective vision—a dream shared by many people simultaneously—that conveys a message of profound importance. These communal visions are understood as the most powerful guidance the goddess can offer. Some of the greatest decisions in Āsháian history are said to have been made on the basis of a collective vision vouchsafed during the Festival of the Woven Night.

Daily worship of Zárványä is more subtle. Before sleep, the pious will often kindle a small lamp—the dream-light—and offer a brief prayer: "Zárványä, Weaver of truth, open the gates of vision. Let me see clearly in sleep what is hidden in waking. Speak to me in the language of symbols and thread. Guide my steps tomorrow with the wisdom perceived tonight." Upon waking, before rising or speaking to anyone, a devoted worshipper will reflect on any dreams they remember, seeking for Zárványä's message.

## Organization

The priesthood of Zárványä is distinctive among the Āsháian cults because it is simultaneously highly formalized and deeply mystical. The formal structure reflects the institutional side: the priesthood maintains the Dream Houses, trains interpreters, and preserves the accumulated knowledge of dream-symbolism across generations. Yet within that structure, every priest is understood to be a mystic—directly touched by the goddess, capable of receiving visions, responsible for interpreting cosmic truths from the raw material of symbol and image.

At the apex stands the Mōbad of Visions, a figure rarely encountered in public life. The Mōbad of Visions maintains a small retreat, often hidden away in the highest tower of a great fire-temple or in a remote mountain sanctuary. This individual spends most of their time in meditation and sleep, allowing Zárványä to speak through them. When major questions of religious doctrine or cosmic significance arise, the other priesthood members will petition the Mōbad of Visions for guidance. The Mōbad will enter deep sleep, sometimes for days or even weeks, and emerge with a pronouncement that carries the weight of divine revelation.

The Rūshnaveh (vision-readers) form the backbone of Zárványä's working priesthood. These are individuals—some identified in childhood, others called later in life—who possess natural gifts of interpretation and intuition. A Rūshnaveh undergoes years of training in the symbolic languages that Zárványä uses: the meanings encoded in different animals and plants, the messages conveyed by numbers and geometric patterns, the cosmic correspondences between celestial bodies and human destinies. But training alone does not make a true Rūshnaveh. The goddess must confirm the calling through direct revelation. A trainee might spend an entire night in a dream-chamber and receive nothing—silence from Zárványä—and be dismissed from training as unsuitable. Alternatively, a trainee might receive such a profound and clear vision that their readiness is immediately apparent. Those who receive Zárványä's direct confirmation become Hērbads of Vision and are authorized to counsel the faithful.

The Āthravāns (initiates) of Zárványä's order undergo a training that is both intellectual and mystical. They study the formal texts—manuals of dream-symbolism, cosmological texts that explain the structure of the realm of vision, philosophical works on the nature of truth and its revelation. But they also spend hours in meditation, learning to still the mind, to enter deep sleep voluntarily, to maintain awareness even while dreaming. The most advanced practices involve controlled dreaming—the ability to remain conscious within a dream-state, to question the visions as they unfold, to demand clarity from Zárványä rather than passively accepting fragmentary images.

Notably, the priesthood of Zárványä includes many women, and in some regions, women form the majority of the formal priesthood. This reflects the belief that women, who are biologically closer to the cycles of the moon and thus aligned with Zárványä's celestial nature, are often more naturally gifted at dream-interpretation and vision-receiving. Male priests are not excluded and some are renowned for their prowess, but the priesthood as a whole is less male-dominated than the temple hierarchies of other Āsháian deities.

## Relations

Zárványä occupies a unique position within the Āsháian pantheon as the connection between the divine realm and mortal consciousness. In this regard, she works in concert with [[affiliation-ahurdaen|Āhúrdáén]], the supreme deity. Āhúrdáén creates and sustains the cosmic order; Zárványä is the means by which mortals perceive and understand that order. Without Zárványä's visions, the commands of Āhúrdáén would remain eternally hidden, unknowable. With her guidance, even the simplest person can glimpse the face of truth.

Her relationship with [[affiliation-korsadra|Khóršádrä]] (Bearer of Eternal Light) is also important, though more complex. Both deities are concerned with revealing truth, but they work through different mediums. Khóršádrä reveals truth through the light of reason and knowledge—the accumulated learning that can be taught and transmitted from one person to another. Zárványä reveals truth through direct mystical experience—the vision that comes unbidden and speaks in the language of symbol. Both forms of revelation are necessary. A person who relies only on reason without mystical insight may understand the world intellectually but miss the deeper patterns of cosmic truth. Conversely, a mystic who rejects reason can be led astray by false visions and deceptive symbols. The two priesthoods work in some tension but also in complementarity, each checking the other's excesses.

Zárványä is related to [[affiliation-ataros|Ātáröš]], the Sacred Flame, in that both deities facilitate revelation. But where Ātáröš reveals through the purification of fire—burning away falsehood and corruption—Zárványä reveals through the gentler medium of vision. A Hērbad of Ātáröš might read the will of the gods in the pattern of flame and ash; a Hērbad of Zárványä reads it in dreams. Both are paths to truth, but they appeal to different temperaments and different circumstances.

Finally, Zárványä stands in eternal opposition to [[affiliation-angaros|Ángáróš]], the Shadowed Serpent. Ángáróš is the god of deception, confusion, and the corruption of truth into lies. He sends false visions and misleading dreams to those who would trap themselves in illusion and self-deception. The battle between Zárványä and Ángáróš is fought in the realm of dream and vision: Zárványä sends clarity and truth through dreams, while Ángáróš attempts to flood the dreaming-mind with lies and delusion. A person who receives a vision must always ask themselves: is this truth or deception? Did Zárványä weave this thread, or did Ángáróš lay a trap? This is why dream-interpretation is so carefully studied and why masters of the craft are so highly valued—they are the defenders of truth in the realm where Ángáróš's deceptions run deepest.
