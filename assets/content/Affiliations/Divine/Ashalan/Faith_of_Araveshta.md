No deity in the Āsháian pantheon provokes more theological controversy than Árávēštä, and no priesthood more openly displays the signs of wealth and earthly pleasure. Where the priests of other gods move through the world in simple robes and maintained simplicity, the Hērbads of Araveshta wear fine silks dyed in vibrant purples and reds, their fingers heavy with rings of silver and gold, their compounds fragrant with incense and perfumed oils. They defend this ostentatious display with passionate certainty: if Āsha is truth and order, then surely the abundance that flows from righteous living is itself a form of truth. To deny pleasure, to reject prosperity, to shun beauty—these, they argue, are the creeping lies of asceticism, the subtle whispers of Druj encouraging one to believe that suffering is somehow nobler than joy. Araveshta comes to those who have ordered their affairs correctly, who conduct their commerce with honesty, who maintain their households with wisdom and generosity. He is the reward for right action, the divine confirmation that one's path is true.

Yet the controversy surrounding Araveshta runs deeper than mere aesthetics of dress and comportment. Conservative theologians—particularly among the desert nomads and the stricter sects of the Sultanate—argue that Araveshta's theology is dangerously close to Druj itself. They point to the excesses of his cult, the legendary feasts where merchants drink wine until they collapse, the gambling dens that operate under his name, the temples where sensual pleasures are openly offered for a price. They ask how one can claim to serve Āsha while giving oneself over to intoxication and excess, which cloud judgment and lead to poor decisions. But Araveshta's defenders counter with equal vehemence: true excess is that of Druj—the wasteful destruction of what is good, the squandering of life itself through despair and denial. Araveshta teaches discriminating enjoyment, the art of appreciation, the recognition that the good things of this world are to be savored, not merely endured. A glass of wine taken with friends in celebration of a successful trade is not excess; it is the proper honoring of Āsha's gifts. The problem, they insist, lies not in enjoyment but in _thoughtlessness_, in mindless consumption without appreciation or gratitude.

The cult of Araveshta has found its strongest foothold in the settled cities of the Sultanate of Amradad and the maritime trading cities of eastern Harad. Here, where wealth flows from commerce and careful negotiation rather than the capricious cycles of nomadic herding, Araveshta's promise resonates powerfully. Merchants pray to him before making contracts, knowing that his blessing brings favorable terms and profitable ventures. Craftspeople invoke his aid when creating works of exceptional beauty or quality. The wealthy commission elaborate festivals in his honor, providing free wine and feasting to the poor, which they justify as acts of divinely mandated charity and goodness. In Amradad especially, the major feast-days honoring Araveshta are occasions of civic celebration where the barriers between social classes temporarily dissolve, and master and servant sit at the same table, united in the consumption of rare foods and excellent wine. The Sultan himself presides over the most lavish of these celebrations, and by appearing thus, he lends the full weight of his authority to the claim that Araveshta's way is Āsha's way.

Yet even Araveshta's priesthood acknowledges that their path is perilous and easily corrupted. For where is the line between appreciation and gluttony? Between celebration and drunken abandonment? Between the collection of beautiful things and avarice? The theological texts of the Araveshatian order contain long passages discussing this precise problem—that the pursuit of pleasure, taken to excess, becomes a different thing entirely, a kind of spiritual poisoning. This is where Araveshta's priests deploy their most sophisticated arguments: that truly righteous pleasure requires _wisdom_, requires the capacity to enjoy without losing oneself, to accumulate wealth without being enslaved by it, to pursue beauty without becoming enslaved to vanity. They point to the rule of the three feasts: a feast of the body (food, wine, sensual comfort), a feast of the spirit (beautiful music, fine art, learned conversation), and a feast of the community (the sharing of abundance with others, the bonds of hospitality and kinship). Only when all three are present, they argue, does celebration become a true expression of Āsha. A feast without beauty or community is mere gluttony; beauty without gratitude is vanity; community without celebration is mere obligation.

## Worship

The temples of Araveshta are the most architecturally ambitious and visually spectacular of all the Āsháian sanctuaries. Built with soaring arches and decorated with intricate tile-work featuring patterns of vines, fruit, and flowing water, these temples are designed to be places of sensory delight. The courtyards contain fountains and gardens where rare plants are cultivated—fruit trees brought from distant lands, flowers that bloom only once a year and fill the air with intoxicating perfume. The prayer halls are lit by oil lamps that burn fragrant oils, and the floors are covered with the finest carpets. The priests wear vestments of silk embroidered with gold thread, and the ritual vessels are crafted from silver or carved from semi-precious stones. To enter a temple of Araveshta is to be assailed by beauty—visual beauty, olfactory beauty, the beauty of sound as trained singers perform hymns in harmonies that have been perfected over generations.

The central ritual of Araveshatian worship is the Ceremony of Gratitude, held monthly on the feast-day, and more elaborately at the two great festivals (the Harvest Feast at summer's end and the Prosperity Festival at winter's turning). A worshipper brings an offering—wine, fruit, honey, bread, fine cloth, or any object of beauty or utility. The Hērbad receives the offering and asks the supplicant what joy it brings them, what pleasure or use it represents. The supplicant speaks their answer aloud: "This wine gladdens my heart and brings fellowship with my friends" or "This cloth will keep my children warm" or "This honey sweetens my food and reminds me of the goodness of life." The offering is then incorporated into the temple's store or given to the poor—the choice depends on the nature of the offering and the Hērbad's judgment. But the crucial act is the speaking, the conscious articulation of gratitude and appreciation. Araveshta, the priests teach, does not merely give; he asks that we _know_ what we have received, that we recognize the gift and honor it with gratitude.

Fire, as in all Āsháian worship, plays a central role, but in temples of Araveshta it is softened and aestheticized. Rather than the harsh, purifying flames of more ascetic orders, Araveshatian fire is gentle—oil lamps that provide warm light without harsh shadows, incense that burns slowly and releases fragrant smoke. The prayers spoken while kindling these fires take on a lyrical, almost sensuous quality. A typical prayer might run: "Golden Giver, light-bringer, you who crown the feast with pleasure and the market with prosperity, accept this fire as a beacon of our gratitude. As this flame consumes the oil and releases its light, so may we consume the good things of this world with wisdom and joy, burning brightly but never burning out." The largest festivals feature elaborate processions where the beauty of the city is put on display—musicians playing instruments, dancers performing, floats displaying the finest goods available for trade, young people crowned with flowers. The atmosphere is one of ecstatic celebration, yet always with the underlying theological message: this beauty, this prosperity, this joy—these are the rewards of righteous living and the blessings of Āsha.

## Organization

The priesthood of Araveshta is hierarchically organized but far more fluid and individualistic than other Āsháian orders. The Mōbad of Araveshta is typically the wealthiest priest in the organization, often drawn from the merchant class, and his authority rests as much on his demonstrated ability to accumulate and manage wealth as on his spiritual authority. Below him are the Hērbads, many of whom are independently wealthy and operate their own temples or chapters as semi-autonomous institutions, provided they maintain loyalty to the central Mōbad and contribute part of their wealth to the order's coffers. The Āthravāns, the initiates, are often younger members of wealthy families or promising merchants who are gradually being groomed for higher ranks.

This structure creates both strength and weakness. On one hand, the Araveshatian priesthood is deeply integrated into the mercantile life of the Sultanate and the coastal cities; their priests are often involved in major trade ventures, serve as advisors to merchant princes and sultans, and wield considerable economic and political power. A Hērbad of Araveshta might own ships, control warehouses, finance expeditions to distant lands, and use his wealth and authority to support causes he deems worthwhile—establishing hospitals, schools, libraries, and museums. The greatest temples of Araveshta function almost as private banks, lending money at favorable rates and facilitating complex commercial transactions. Some of the finest art and architecture in the Sultanate has been commissioned by Araveshatian priests and merchants.

On the other hand, this integration into commerce creates constant tension with more austere theological viewpoints. The order has historically been riven by scandals—Hērbads caught embezzling temple funds, priests exposed as loan sharks charging usurious rates, temples found to be fronts for vice and exploitation. Each such scandal prompts waves of reform movements within the order itself, often led by younger, more conservative voices demanding a return to stricter interpretation of the principles. These reform movements occasionally succeed in temporarily purging the priesthood of its most obviously corrupt elements, but the fundamental structure of the order—its integration into wealth and commerce—inevitably permits the gradual return of more relaxed standards. Currently (in the year 692 of the Thalorna calendar), the Araveshatian priesthood is in the midst of a major reform period, with the current Mōbad implementing strict auditing procedures and removing several prominent Hērbads accused of using temple wealth for personal enrichment rather than for legitimate ceremonial purposes or charity.

The priesthood maintains strong educational institutions, but their focus is significantly different from other orders. While the priests of Zúráthrä study medicine and engineering, and those of Sirvadar study cartography and natural philosophy, the Araveshatian priests cultivate knowledge of aesthetics, rhetoric, rhetoric, economics, and the arts. The great temple in Amradad's capital maintains one of the finest libraries in the known world, filled with treatises on commerce, art, philosophy, music theory, and the techniques of negotiation. The order sponsors talented musicians, artists, poets, and playwrights, believing that the creation of beauty is itself a form of worship. They also maintain schools where promising young merchants are taught the principles of ethical commerce and the deeper theological justifications for Araveshta's way.

## Relations

Araveshta's relationship with the other deities of the pantheon is marked by a kind of polite tension. With [[affiliation-zurathra|Faith of Zúráthrä]], the Mother of the Oasis, the relationship is cordial but somewhat distant. Both orders acknowledge the other's legitimacy, and both work to establish each other's presence in prosperous cities and oases. But there is an underlying theological disagreement about whether pleasure and celebration are ends in themselves (as Araveshta's priests believe) or merely means to the end of sustaining life (as Zúráthrä's priesthood argues). Progressive theologians in both orders have worked to develop more nuanced positions, with some arguing that Araveshta represents the joy of living well, while Zúráthrä represents the foundation that makes such living possible. Yet the tension remains; Zúráthrä's priests are often deeply suspicious of what they perceive as the materialism and hedonism of the Araveshatian order.

With [[affiliation-mithara|Faith of Míthárä]], the Goddess of Creation and Renewal, Araveshta's priests maintain a relationship of strategic alliance. Both deities are understood as "life-giving" in their respective domains, and the great temples occasionally coordinate their efforts in the cities, especially around the major renewal festivals. Some theological schools argue that Mithara's power to create is inseparable from Araveshta's blessing of the fruits of creation—that creation without the ability to enjoy what is created would be hollow and purposeless. The priesthoods cooperate on various charitable enterprises and share institutional resources in several major cities.

Araveshta's most important relationship within the pantheon is with [[affiliation-sirvadar|Faith of Šírvádár]], the Wandering Guide. The two deities are understood as complementary: Sirvadar protects the journey itself, the movement along the trade routes, while Araveshta ensures the prosperity that results from successful commerce. The priests of both orders work closely together to maintain the trade routes and encourage mercantile activity. Araveshatian merchant-princes often employ Sirvadar's priests as guides and consultants, and it is not uncommon to find temples or shrines dedicated to both deities in the same location, particularly in major trading cities and port towns.

The most controversial aspect of Araveshta's theology concerns his alleged relationship to chaos and Druj. Conservative thinkers argue that Araveshta represents a dangerous theological compromise, a path that leads inexorably toward the licentiousness and moral dissolution that are Druj's true goal. They point to cases of Araveshatian temples where discipline has broken down, where priests have become indistinguishable from criminal syndicates, where the pursuit of pleasure has crossed clearly into vice. Araveshta's defenders counter that such corruptions represent a betrayal of true Araveshatian principles, not their natural conclusion. They argue that wisdom, gratitude, and the cultivation of beauty are fundamentally opposed to Druj's chaos and destruction. The debate rages on in theological schools and temple courts, unresolved and perhaps unresolvable—a permanent schism in Āsháian thought about the nature of proper living and the role of pleasure and prosperity in a righteous life.
