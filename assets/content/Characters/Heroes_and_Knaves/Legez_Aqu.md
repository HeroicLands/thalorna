# Appearance {#appearance}

![[legezaqu|Legez Aqu]]{float=top-left}

Legez stands 5'9" tall with a heavy build—broad-shouldered and thick through the chest, the frame of a man who was never slight and who has spent three years walking the empire's roads. He has dark brown skin, a shaved head (maintained by habit long after the temple's purification codes ceased to bind him), and dark eyes set deep beneath a heavy brow. His features are blunt and serious: a prominent nose, a strong jaw, full lips that settle naturally into an expression of watchful gravity. The most striking marks on him are the ritual tattoos on both forearms—the feather-of-[[lore-qearetdty|Qe'âret]] inked in ceremonial blue—scored through with deliberate cuts, the temple's mark of expulsion. He keeps his left palm turned inward when he can; the horizontal branding scar there is the mark they burned into him the day they cast him out.

He dresses in a plain cloth tunic and worn leather sandals—indistinguishable from a hundred other travelers on the river roads, which is precisely the point. The only concession to his former station is a walking staff of blackwood, unadorned but well-made, and the way he carries himself: shoulders squared, head level, moving with the deliberate economy of a man who learned to command rooms before he learned to survive roads.

# Dossier {#dossier}

Legez was born in [[place-anlaghzetun|Anlagh-Zetûn]], the second son of a minor scribe who managed grain inventories for the selat's central granary. The family was respectable but unremarkable—literate, employed, and utterly without social ambition. His father's highest aspiration for his sons was that they follow him into the scribal service. Legez's older brother did exactly that. Legez, at the age of nine, was accepted as a Wazu into the Lut-Qe'âret at Anlagh-Zetûn—not through family connections but because the temple's recruiting priests identified a boy with an unusual capacity for concentration and an instinctive discomfort with dishonesty that bordered on the pathological.

The temple schools of Qe'âret are among the most rigorous in the empire. Where the schools of [[lore-uzneradty|Uznêra]] teach diplomacy and the reading of people, and the schools of [[lore-rethsaardty|Reth'Sa'âr]] teach the preservation and interpretation of knowledge, the schools of Qe'âret teach law, procedure, the weighing of evidence, and the sacred obligation of truthful speech. A Wazu of Qe'âret learns to adjudicate disputes, administer oaths, conduct the rituals of judgment, and—above all—to recognize deception. The training is not gentle. Students are tested relentlessly: presented with false evidence, subjected to elaborate fabrications designed to test their ability to separate truth from persuasion, and punished not for being deceived but for failing to admit it. The theology is simple and unforgiving: Qe'âret is the principle that holds the cosmos together. Untruth weakens it. A priest who cannot identify falsehood—or worse, who tolerates it—is not merely incompetent but cosmically dangerous.

Legez thrived. He was never the most brilliant student—that distinction belonged to others who went on to scholarly careers in the capital—but he was the most dogged. Where quicker minds leaped to conclusions and occasionally leaped wrong, Legez worked through evidence methodically, tested his assumptions against procedure, and arrived at judgments that were slow but almost always correct. His tutors noted that he lacked charm and subtlety (observations that would follow him through his career) but that his commitment to the process of truth-finding was absolute. He was ordained as a Lem'Nelgir at twenty-two, assigned first to minor judicial duties at Anlagh-Zetûn, and over the following years he built a reputation as a careful, thorough, and incorruptible temple judge.

At twenty-eight, he was transferred to the great Lut-Qe'âret at [[place-khelunu|Khelunu]]—a significant promotion. Khelunu is the scholarly and cultural heart of the empire, and its Qe'âret temple serves as the supreme judicial authority for the surrounding selatu. The temple adjudicates boundary disputes, inheritance claims, commercial fraud, and—most importantly—administers the sacred oaths that bind the empire's legal system. When a Halzi'a swears fealty, when a merchant seals a contract under divine witness, when a condemned man appeals his sentence, it is a priest of Qe'âret who presides. The temple's judgments carry the weight of cosmic law, and the Thâz'Lekhau of Qe'âret at Khelunu—a man named Anlagherhafu—was one of the most powerful religious figures outside the capital.

Legez served at Khelunu for nine years. He rose steadily: first conducting minor arbitrations, then presiding over commercial disputes between trading houses, then serving on the judicial panels that heard appeals from provincial courts. He was respected by his peers for his thoroughness and his unwillingness to take shortcuts. He was not loved. Legez has never been easy company—he is serious where others are convivial, precise where others are approximate, and possessed of a moral certainty that can feel like judgment even when none is intended. But in a temple devoted to truth, his qualities were valued even when they were not enjoyed.

The trouble began in the seventh year of his posting, when Legez was assigned to audit the temple's oracular records—the logs of auguries, divine pronouncements, and ritual verdicts delivered by the senior Lem'Nelgir on behalf of the goddess. It was routine work, part of the temple's internal quality procedures. The records were supposed to be consistent: each augury logged with the ritual conditions, the question posed, the signs observed, and the interpretation rendered. Legez found discrepancies. Small ones, at first—dates that didn't align with the ritual calendar, interpretations that referenced signs not recorded in the observation logs. He flagged them as clerical errors. But as he dug deeper, the pattern sharpened. A series of major auguries delivered to the selat's wealthiest families over the preceding five years had observation logs that were internally inconsistent—the signs described in the records did not match the astronomical conditions on the dates listed. Either the rituals had been conducted on different dates than claimed, or the signs had been fabricated entirely.

The implications were staggering. If the auguries had been manufactured, then the judgments based on them—land grants, inheritance rulings, commercial arbitrations worth enormous sums—were fraudulent. Not merely mistaken, but deliberately corrupted. And the auguries in question had all been conducted by the temple's most senior Lem'Nelgir, priests who reported directly to Thâz'Lekhau Anlagherhafu.

Legez spent six months quietly documenting the discrepancies, cross-referencing them with astronomical records maintained independently by the temple's Wazu scholars (who tracked celestial observations for ritual planning, not judicial purposes). The evidence was damning. He compiled a formal report, following precisely the procedures his training prescribed, and presented it to Thâz'Lekhau Anlagherhafu directly—believing, with the earnest faith of a principled man, that the Thâz'Lekhau would be horrified and would act to purge the corruption.

Anlagherhafu was not horrified. He had authorized the fabrications himself.

The corrupt auguries had been part of an arrangement between the temple and Halzi'a Zabqarrê, the Halzi'a of Khelunu's selat. The Halzi'a provided the temple with land grants, construction funds, and political protection; in return, the temple's oracular pronouncements consistently favored the Halzi'a's allies, validated his territorial claims against rival Halzi'a, and blessed his commercial ventures with divine sanction. It was a partnership of mutual enrichment disguised as sacred duty—precisely the kind of corruption that the theology of Qe'âret was supposed to make impossible.

What followed was swift and brutal. Within a week of presenting his report, Legez was arrested by the temple's own judicial officers—priests he had served alongside for years. The charge was not corruption or whistleblowing; it was heresy. Specifically: blasphemous denial of the goddess's voice. In Khelâthi theological law, the Thâz'Lekhau speaks with the authority of the deity. To accuse a Thâz'Lekhau of fabricating divine pronouncements is, doctrinally, to claim that the goddess has abandoned her own temple—a statement so dangerous to the religious order that it constitutes an attack on the divine itself. The charge was designed to be unanswerable. If Legez was right about the corruption, then Qe'âret had failed to protect her own house, which meant the cosmic order was broken—a claim no sane priest would make. If he was wrong, he had slandered the goddess's representative. Either way, he was guilty.

The trial lasted one day. Legez's evidence was confiscated and sealed in the temple archives. His name was struck from the rolls of the ordained. The feather tattoos on his forearms—the marks of his ordination that he had carried for eighteen years—were ritually defaced with a blade, and a brand of expulsion was burned into his left palm. He was escorted to the city gates and told that if he was found within Khelunu's selat again, the sentence would be death.

During the years before his fall, Legez had maintained a friendship with Zabet Thalura Iqe'Lêru, the Thâz'Lekhau of Qe'âret in the capital—his own order's supreme authority. They had met when Legez traveled to the capital as part of a judicial delegation from Khelunu, and something between them clicked immediately: two sharp-minded, serious priests who recognized in each other an uncommon commitment to the principle they served. Their friendship was built on intellectual respect and a shared impatience with the performative aspects of temple politics; Thalura read motive where Legez read evidence, and their conversations sharpened both. He considered her the finest legal mind in the order. She considered him the most principled judge she had ever met. In a profession full of political operators, each found in the other someone who seemed genuinely devoted to truth.

When the heresy charge was brought, the case should have been escalated to Thalura for review—as Thâz'Lekhau of Qe'âret in the capital, she held nominal supervisory authority over every Qe'âret temple in the empire, including Khelunu's. Anlagherhafu moved too quickly, invoking emergency theological provisions to handle the matter locally before word reached the capital. By the time Thalura learned of the charges, the trial was concluded and the sentence carried out. She could still have demanded a formal review. She chose not to. Her reasons were sound by every political measure: challenging Anlagherhafu publicly would have split the Qe'âret order between the capital and the provinces, exposed the judicial system to a crisis of legitimacy, and handed her enemies—the Thâz'Lekhau of Uqa'â chief among them—the evidence they needed to argue that Qe'âret's house was not in order. One man's exile was a tragedy. A schism in the order of truth would have been a catastrophe.

Legez does not know the full extent of what she could have done. He knows she held authority over Khelunu. He knows she did nothing. Whether she chose silence out of political calculation, genuine uncertainty about his claims, or something more complicated—an awareness that investigating Khelunu might reveal the limits of her own oversight across the provincial temples—is the question that eats at him. He does not hate her. He is not certain he has the right to. But the silence of the one person who could have saved him is the wound that has healed the least.

The three years since his exile have been hard. Legez wandered south along the river, then east into the hill country, surviving on the skills he had and the ones he acquired. His metalworking—a craft he had learned years ago from temple artisans who maintained the ritual bronzework and which he had always practiced as a meditative discipline—became his livelihood. He repaired tools, sharpened blades, and mended pots in the villages he passed through, earning enough to eat and move on. His staff-work, originally trained in the temple's judicial enforcement corps (the armed priests who enforced court orders and protected the temple's judicial proceedings), kept him alive on the roads. He is not a warrior, but he is a large man with training and a staff, and the roads of the empire are not as dangerous for him as they might be for a scholar.

He met [[being-amqltzlmlglq|Amqelet-Zelemu Legulu’aqun]] in the hill country east of Khelunu, roughly a year into his exile. The astrologer was already on his sacred journey, pursuing the celestial disturbances he called the Convergence of the Nine Moons, and Legez felt the wrongness of it before Amqelet-Zelemu explained it—a deep, visceral sense that the cosmic order was under strain, the same faculty that had guided his work as a temple judge. He recognized that Amqelet-Zelemu's quest was not merely astronomical but theological: something was disrupting the balance that Qe'âret held together, and restoring it was precisely the kind of work that a priest of truth and order was born to do. He joined the company not as a follower but as a companion, offering his knowledge of ritual procedure, his understanding of temple politics, and his willingness to walk into places that frightened other men. He serves as the group's spiritual anchor, the one who insists on doing things properly even when expediency beckons—a quality that Amqelet-Zelemu, the visionary, needs more than he always admits.

What Legez does not say, and what only those who watch him closely might suspect, is that he has not entirely given up on his faith. The temple expelled him. The priests who serve Qe'âret branded him a heretic. But Legez's devotion was never to the temple as an institution—it was to the principle the temple claimed to serve. Truth does not stop being true because the people responsible for upholding it have failed. He still prays, alone and in silence, and he still carries a small set of consecrated ritual implements hidden in his pack: the bronze scales, the feather token, the chalk for inscribing the circle of judgment. He performs no public rites—he has no authority to do so, and the sentence of heresy means that any ritual he conducts is doctrinally void. But in the privacy of his own practice, between himself and the goddess he still believes in, Legez Aqu remains a priest.

## Psyche

### Personality

Legez is serious, methodical, and possessed of a moral certainty that most people find either admirable or exhausting, depending on how long they have spent in his company. He does not make small talk well. He does not laugh easily. He speaks with the deliberate precision of a man trained to weigh every word against the standard of truth, and this habit—useful in a courtroom, wearying at a campfire—gives his conversation a weight that can feel like judgment even when he intends none. He is aware of this effect and has largely stopped trying to correct it. People will think what they think; what matters is whether he has spoken truly.

Beneath the gravity, there is genuine warmth—but it is the warmth of a man who expresses care through action rather than words. He cooks for the group without being asked, takes watch without complaint, mends gear with steady hands, and will walk into danger for a companion without hesitation or fanfare. He does not say "I value you." He makes sure you eat, sleep safely, and survive.

His exile has hardened him in some ways and softened him in others. The temple trained him to be certain; the road taught him that certainty has costs, and that the people who pay those costs are not always the ones who earned them. He is less rigid than he was—more willing to consider that a situation might have dimensions he hasn't seen, more patient with people who make choices he finds difficult to respect. But the core has not shifted. He believes that truth matters more than comfort, that justice is not a negotiation, and that the cosmic order he devoted his life to serving is real even if the institution that claimed to represent it has betrayed it. He will not compromise on these convictions, and he does not expect others to share them.

### Motivation

Legez's primary drive is restoration—not of his personal reputation, though he would not refuse it, but of the truth. The corruption in the Lut-Qe'âret at Khelunu is not merely an institutional scandal; it is a wound in the cosmic order, a place where the principle of truth has been deliberately broken by the people entrusted to uphold it. As long as Anlagherhafu's fabricated auguries stand unchallenged, the judgments based on them remain in force—land was redistributed, families were dispossessed, commercial disputes were settled on the basis of lies spoken in the goddess's name. Legez cannot undo this alone, but he cannot stop believing it must be undone.

His attachment to Amqelet-Zelemu's quest is genuine but entangled with this deeper purpose. The Convergence is a cosmic disturbance, and Legez believes that cosmic disturbances do not arise in isolation—they are symptoms of accumulated disorder, fractures in the fabric that Qe'âret holds together. Whether the corruption at Khelunu is connected to the celestial instability or merely another expression of the same underlying rot, he does not know. But he knows that walking toward the disturbance is the right direction, and that is enough.

There is also a quieter motivation, one he would deny if asked directly: he wants to see Thalura again. Not for romance—their friendship was never that—but because she is the only person of genuine power who might believe him, and because her silence during his trial is a question he needs answered. Did she choose politics over principle, or did she genuinely doubt him? The answer matters to him far more than it should.

### Strengths

Legez's foundational strength is his training in the judicial procedures of Qe'âret. He can evaluate evidence, identify inconsistencies in testimony, and construct arguments with a rigor that most people find impossible to match. When the group encounters conflicting accounts, hidden motives, or deliberate deception, Legez is the one who sits quietly, asks precise questions, and assembles the truth from fragments that others overlooked. This is not intuition—it is method, drilled into him over decades, and it functions as reliably as any skill he possesses.

His command presence is considerable. Despite his exile, Legez carries the bearing of a man who spent years presiding over judicial proceedings and directing temple enforcement operations. When he speaks with authority, people tend to listen—not because of his current station (which is nothing) but because something in his voice and manner communicates that he is accustomed to being obeyed and has earned the right. His knowledge of Khelâthi religious and political systems—temple hierarchies, noble houses, legal precedent, ritual protocol—makes him an invaluable guide through the empire's institutional landscape.

Physically, he is the most capable combatant in Amqelet-Zelemu's company, though he would not describe himself that way. His staff training from the temple's judicial enforcement corps was practical and thorough—designed to subdue, restrain, and if necessary incapacitate people who resisted the temple's legal authority—and three years on the road have kept him sharp. He is not a soldier, but he is a large, strong, well-trained man who does not panic under pressure and who has learned the hard way that the road does not care about your theological principles.

His metalworking skill, learned from the temple's ritual artisans and refined during exile, provides both practical utility and a source of income. He can repair weapons, mend armor, and fabricate simple tools—capabilities that make the group significantly more self-sufficient on extended journeys.

### Weaknesses

Legez's most dangerous weakness is his rigidity. He has spent his life in service to an absolute principle—truth is truth, regardless of consequences—and this commitment, which made him a superb temple judge, makes him a difficult companion and a predictable adversary. He struggles with situations that require compromise, strategic deception, or the kind of moral flexibility that the real world constantly demands. Amqelet-Zelemu and the others have learned to work around this, but there are moments when Legez's insistence on doing things the right way has cost the group time, opportunities, and goodwill.

His empathy is genuinely limited. He is not cruel—he takes no pleasure in others' pain—but he often fails to recognize emotional dimensions of situations that are obvious to everyone else. He approaches interpersonal problems as if they were legal disputes, looking for facts and procedures when what people need is understanding. This blind spot has strained his relationships throughout his life and contributed to his isolation after exile. The temple valued his thoroughness; outside the temple, thoroughness without warmth is just coldness with better documentation.

He carries deep anger—not the hot, expressive kind, but the cold, settled kind that accumulates over years of injustice. Anger at Anlagherhafu, at the priests who knew and said nothing, at the system that punished truth-telling and rewarded complicity. He controls it well, but it surfaces in moments of stress as a biting harshness that can wound the people closest to him. He is aware of it and ashamed of it, which does not stop it from happening.

Finally, his exile has left him without institutional support of any kind. He has no wealth, no patron, no temple authority, no legal standing. In a civilization where identity is bound to institutional affiliation—where a priest without a temple is barely a person—Legez is functionally a nonentity. Any encounter with imperial authority is a risk; if his identity were discovered by the wrong people, the sentence of death that awaits him in Khelunu's selat could be extended to other jurisdictions.

## Plot Hooks

1. **The Sealed Archive**: Gulmenwati sends word that the evidence Legez compiled—the astronomical cross-references, the augury logs, the discrepancy analysis—was not destroyed as Anlagherhafu claimed. It was sealed in the restricted archives beneath the Khelunu temple, classified as heretical material too dangerous to circulate but too thoroughly documented to simply burn. Retrieving it would vindicate Legez, but it would require entering Khelunu's selat (where the death sentence applies), penetrating the temple's archive security, and escaping before Iqenakht's network closes around him. The question is whether any ally could be persuaded to help—and whether the evidence, even recovered, would matter against the combined weight of a Thâz'Lekhau and a Halzi'a.

2. **The Capital Gambit**: Legez learns through Gulmenwati that the Thâz'Lekhau of Qe'âret in the capital—Anlagherhafu's superior—is conducting a quiet review of judicial irregularities across the empire's Qe'âret temples, driven by complaints from Halzi'a whose rivals benefited from suspiciously favorable auguries. If Legez could reach the capital and present his case directly, he might find a sympathetic ear at the highest level of his order. But reaching the capital means passing through hostile territory, and approaching the supreme Thâz'Lekhau as a branded heretic is as likely to end in re-arrest as in vindication. The gambit would be considerably safer with a powerful intermediary—someone like the Thâz'Lekhau of Uznêra, who has the Gar-Aû's ear and who once called Legez a friend.

3. **The Old Friend**: The company's travels bring them within reach of the capital, and Legez faces the decision he has been postponing: whether to seek out Thalura Iqe'Lêru. She has the political power to help him, the intelligence to understand his evidence, and the institutional connections to get his case before the right people. But she also chose silence when he needed her most, and he does not know whether she would risk her position now—or whether approaching her would endanger them both. The complication deepens if Thalura is simultaneously dealing with her own crises (the spy in her circle, the rival Thâz'Lekhau, the heresy within her temple) and sees Legez's reappearance as either an opportunity for alliance or a catastrophic liability.

4. **The Second Witness**: During the company's journey, they encounter a woman who claims to have served as a Wazu in the Khelunu temple during the period of the fabricated auguries. She was transferred abruptly to a remote provincial posting after asking questions about record-keeping irregularities—questions similar to the ones Legez asked. She has her own partial evidence, independently gathered, that corroborates his claims. But she is frightened, reluctant to act, and deeply suspicious of Legez's motives: the temple told her he was a heretic and a madman, and three years of believing that story is not easily overcome. Winning her trust—and protecting her from Anlagherhafu's agents, who are watching for exactly this kind of contact—becomes both a practical challenge and a test of Legez's ability to connect with someone on an emotional level rather than a purely evidentiary one.

5. **The Scales of Judgment**: Amqelet-Zelemu's quest leads the company to a sacred site where an ancient ritual of Qe'âret must be performed—a genuine weighing of truth, a ceremony so old that its power derives from the site itself rather than from any institutional authority. Legez is the only member of the company with the ritual training to conduct it, but doing so would mean performing a sacred rite as a branded heretic—an act that his own theology condemns as void at best and blasphemous at worst. The theological question cuts to the heart of everything Legez believes: Is his priesthood a function of institutional authority (which he has lost) or of personal devotion to truth (which he has not)? If he performs the rite and it works, it would be the most powerful evidence possible that the goddess has not abandoned him. If it fails, he will have proven the temple right.
