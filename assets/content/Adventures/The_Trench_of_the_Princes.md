Six hundred dead were put in a trench with the treasure of their shrines, and nobody read their names. A raiding band has cut into the grave, and what stands up from it is not the dead alone. The party can take the gold, put the dead down with fire or give the household its names, and each costs something.

## Overview

The palace household of [[place-zargandur|Zargandûr]] lies in a dry wash east of the city with the sacked gold of its shrines. A band of grave-robbers has dug into it for a season and has lost a third of its number. The [[lore-theshadow|Shadow]] enters the opening they have made and wears the dead. The Ātárzád fire-priests say only fire puts them down, and they know it for a reason they do not tell: the grave also holds sixty of their own, thrown in three days after the fall.

The adventure runs over a day and a night. The party reaches the wash in the afternoon, works the trench until dark and faces what rises, and the morning brings the reckoning with the garrison. The scenes can be taken in a different order when the party's choices pull that way, and the bypass notes carry play on from whichever scene is skipped.

## Hooks

- **The thief.** A broker in Zargandûr's Tellumi quarter, a soft-spoken man who weighs silver with his eyes shut, buys any seal or signet out of the wash, no questions, and pays in barter-goods or silver by weight. The word in his back room is that the raiders have dug for a season and have not found the chain of office, which is the piece he wants most and will not name. He lets it be understood that the last two men he sent did not come back to be paid.
- **The mercenary or common soldier.** A Zhōqarōd captain who is not permitted to say so wants the wash cleared. His own men will not cross the lip, and he cannot order them to without telling them why, so he pays a party of outsiders in horses, half before and half after, to go where his men will not. He pours the wine himself and does not drink it.
- **The leader.** A chieftain of one of the three small tribes whose riders are in the raiding band wants his sons back. Seven are missing. He cannot go himself, since he is bound by a truce with the Zhōqarōd that one armed step into the wash would break. He sits in his tent with the gray stillness of a man who has done his grieving early and wants a body to put it on, and he offers his best mares and his word.
- **The lone wanderer.** The wanderer's night camp is visited by a figure in a gold mask who stands at the edge of the firelight until the fire is built up, and then walks east. It comes back the next night a few paces nearer and stops at the same distance from the flames. The mask is cracked from brow to jaw and the hands hang open and empty. The road it takes runs to [[place-relvanvesul|the wash]].

## Scene One: The Raiders' Fire

**Purpose.** Establish the camp, the dread and the price of staying.

**Situation.** At the lower end of the wash, in the lee of a bank, the raiders' camp is a ring of low fires with the tents pitched close and the animals picketed inside the ring. The wind brings it first as a smell: lamp-oil, burnt hair and the sweetish rot of something dug out of cold sand. The fire is never let down. Two men sit awake in turns with their backs to the flames and their faces to the dark, and the rest sleep in the middle of the camp wrapped head to foot, like men who have been told what happens to a sleeper at the edge. Beyond the bank the raw cut of the trench shows pale in the starlight. The men are thin, jumpy and armed, and half of them have not slept properly in a month. Horses shy at the wash and have to be hobbled hard.

**NPCs and threats.** **Hosikqenod**, the band's chief, a heavy Sowides rider with a brand on his cheek, runs the camp by fear and by the promise of gold. He speaks little, spits often and counts heads at every meal. He is cunning and no fool: he knows the trench is wrong, and he wants a bigger crew to dig it faster and be gone before the season turns. His men include a Tellumi digger, **Danilul**, a stooped man with sand worked into the creases of his knuckles, who reads the old hand on the seals and is the only one who knows what the band has dug. He is terrified of Hosikqenod and more terrified of the trench, and he keeps his tablet inside his shirt. The sentries are frightened of the dark and shoot at shadows. A shout in the night brings a flight of arrows into empty gravel before anyone asks what called.

**Player choices.** Force: break the camp and take the gold already dug; the band outnumbers a small party and fights with the desperation of men who have nowhere to go, but they scatter if their chief falls. Stealth: approach from the dry bank, lie in the gravel and watch the changing of the sentries; the pattern is slack at the fourth hour. Guile: pose as a buying party come for the gold, or as relief diggers sent by a backer in the city; Hosikqenod wants hands badly enough to believe the second story if it is told with a shovel in the fist. Parley: offer a share and a safe fire in return for a look at the trench, and speak to Danilul alone, since he is the one man in camp who would give a great deal to be elsewhere.

**Clues and rewards.** The camp holds a small store of the gold already taken: a pair of lamp-stands, a dozen seals and a cup, heavy and soft and stamped with the twelve gods. It also holds the digger's tablet, which lists what the trench held and what has been taken, shrine by shrine. Danilul can be turned: he will say that the gold on the first layer is stamped, that a stamped plate lifted in silence wakes its dead, and that the band has been lifting plates in silence for a season. The camp's picket-line holds seven horses with the brands of three small tribes, which is the first sign of how many sons are not coming home.

**Consequences.** If the party takes the camp by force, the dead notice; the noise of it carries down the cut and the trench goes still in a way that the party feels at the back of the neck. If the party joins the band, Hosikqenod expects their fire to be added to his, and expects them to take the night watch, which is where the band's losses have fallen. A party that kills Danilul loses the only reader of the old hand.

**If bypassed.** A party that comes to the wash by night finds the camp abandoned, the fires dead and the animals gone, with a single lamp still burning in the middle of the ring and the scuff of many feet leading into the trench. The party goes straight to the cut, and the band is somewhere below.

## Scene Two: The Fire-priest

**Purpose.** Introduce the Ātárzád knowledge of the dead and its price.

**Situation.** A Zhōqarōd patrol is camped on the bank above the wash at a distance, under a Dēlkoshekh named **Rōqeshōr**. They have been sent by the prefect to watch and to do nothing, and they do it with the stiff discomfort of soldiers ordered to keep their backs to a grave. The patrol's tents are pitched in a square, the lamps are lit before dark and a fire-pot hangs from a tripod at the center, burning low and steady with a smell of cedar and hot iron. Rōqeshōr is a thin man with a burnt hand who has stood the same watch for a season and does not like it. He tells the party that only fire puts the dead of this trench down. He has seen it, he says, and he will not say where.

**NPCs and threats.** Rōqeshōr is honest and ashamed. He keeps the burnt hand wrapped in a cloth when strangers are near, and unwraps it when he is tired. The Zhōqarōd soldiers with him are loyal and silent; they are ordered not to cross the lip of the wash, and they eye the party's boots to see whether the party will do what they cannot. A Tellumi chandler, **Nushir Narsâfî**, who sells lamp-oil to the garrison and to the quarter alike, is camped with the patrol on business and can be spoken to. He is round, watchful and amiable, counts a customer's wants while he talks and has a story for every door in the quarter that burns a lamp until dawn.

**Player choices.** Force: seize the priest's fire-pot and lamp; the patrol is small, and the Dēlkoshekh will not order it to stop the party if doing so means a fight on the lip. Stealth: follow the patrol's track back and learn where the knowledge comes from. Guile: trade favors; the Dēlkoshekh gives fire for a promise to say nothing to the prefect, and he accepts that promise the way a man in debt accepts a loan. Parley: ask Nushir what the quarter knows, and buy oil from him at a fair price before asking, because he gives straight answers only to customers.

**Clues and rewards.** A fire-pot and a lamp kindled at the temple altar. The chandler's account that the lamps burn at every door for the dead of the quarter and that some in the quarter mean these. Rōqeshōr's account that sixty of the Zhōqarōd's own lie in the trench among the household, which is how the garrison's priests learned what fire does. Nushir sells lamp-oil by the skin, enough to burn a field the size of the cut, and tells the party that cheap oil smokes and the dead do not mind smoke.

**Consequences.** A party that carries the Flame into the trench with the Dēlkoshekh's leave is let alone; a party that takes it without leave is hunted by the patrol, which rides the party down at dawn and takes the lamp back with the party's goodwill thrown in. A party that promises Rōqeshōr silence carries the promise into the last scene.

**If bypassed.** A party that finds the trench without the priest is met at dawn by the patrol, which rides down the bank in a line and asks what they carry and why they are alive. Rōqeshōr is among them, and the lamp is in his hand.

## Scene Three: The Cut

**Purpose.** Show the dig, spring the trap and give the party a taste of the work.

**Situation.** The trench is a long scar, six paces wide and three deep in places, cut by the raiders across the lower wash. They have worked down through the gravel to the first layer of the dead. The cut is cold in a way the afternoon does not explain, and the smell is of wet cloth and old copper. The bodies are not dry like those of a tower. They are black, matted, packed with sand, and covered in thin beaten gold, the plates laid over the faces as the Tellumi lay them on the eyes of their great dead. Every piece of gold is pressed on a closed eye, and every closed eye opens when the gold is lifted. The eyes that open are not white and not dark. They are only looking. Spades, baskets, a rope ladder and a cracked lamp lie scattered along the lip where the diggers dropped them and ran.

**NPCs and threats.** The dead lie still while the party works. The gold is the trap. A piece taken from a face without the name being spoken wakes the dead whose face it was, and that one rises slowly, with the sand running out of its sleeves. The Shadow stands in the trench as a stillness at its far end, a patch where the air does not move and a lamp flame leans toward it, and at dusk it takes a body. Hosikqenod's men, if they live, work the far end and watch the party's end for a sign that the party has been caught.

**Player choices.** Force: dig fast and take what can be taken before dark; the first layer yields the most gold, and the cost of every plate is a pair of open eyes. Stealth: take only what lies on top, in the first hour, and leave the deeper layers to the dark. Guile: use the digger's tablet to name each piece before lifting it, if Danilul was taken alive or the tablet was seized in the camp. Parley: speak to the dead as the Tellumi do, naming the house and asking leave, with the ritual courtesy that costs nothing but takes an hour.

**Clues and rewards.** Each plate is stamped with the name of a shrine. A plate whose name is spoken comes up clean and quiet, and the eyes under it stay shut. The party finds the chain of office and the prince's seal, lying on the chest of a body in the third layer that wears more gold than any other, with a tag of beaten metal on the chain that carries a name in the old hand. The tag is the key to the fourth scene, and a reader who looks at it finds the prince's name on the underside.

**Consequences.** Pieces taken without a name wake the dead one by one. A party that lifts a plate in silence hears a single breath drawn, and then the next body breathes. The sun goes down in the middle of the work, and the party is on the floor of the trench when the first of them stands. Any treasure in the party's hands at that hour is a weight the party must choose to keep or drop.

**If bypassed.** A party that does not enter the trench is visited in the open by the same dead at dusk, walking slowly up the cut in a line toward the party's fire. They come to the nearest light, and they do not run.

## Scene Four: The Gold Mask

**Purpose.** The confrontation with the dead and the thing that wears them.

**Situation.** At full dark the dead rise in the trench: forty, then a hundred, then more. They are slow and they do not make a sound. The only noise is the dry rasp of gold plates sliding over leather and the creak of a rope ladder under a weight that is not on it. In front of them, standing on the lip with the prince's gold chain across its chest and a cracked mask where a face should be, is the one the Shadow has taken as its own. The moon catches the mask and turns it to a cold coin. The air is cold enough that breath smokes in midsummer.

**NPCs and threats.** The **Gold Mask** is a prince's body in a prince's regalia. It does not speak and it does not tire. It commands the others with a lift of the hand, and they turn at the gesture like cattle at a drover's stick. Its touch carries the cold of the void and takes the heat out of a living man, so that a gripped arm goes white to the shoulder in a few breaths. Where fire reaches the dead they fall; where it does not, they come on. The raiders are on the lip, firing arrows that do nothing, and some of them are weeping. Hosikqenod stands among them with his sword drawn, because it is the one thing he can do.

**Player choices.** Force: burn them with the fire-pots, lamp oil and torches; this works and it is expensive, because the dead burn and the gold and the household burn with them. Stealth: hold the party in the trench shadow while the dead pass; the dead hunt by warmth, so lying still and cold in the sand lets a line of them go by. Guile: lead the dead to the raiders' camp and let the band take the weight; it costs the band, and it costs the sons the chieftain wants back if they are among the diggers. Parley: read the names. The digger's tablet, the chain and the seal give the names of the household's heads, and each name read aloud calms a part of the dead, so that the nearest rank stops and stands with its hands at its sides.

**Clues and rewards.** The Gold Mask breaks if the prince's own name is read over it: the chain of office carries it on a tag. The party that speaks it hears the thing stop, and the mask falls from the face with a flat bright sound, and under it is a child. The child is the youngest of the household, a boy of seven or eight whose body the Shadow wore as the least resisting. The party that sees the face understands what the grave holds.

**Consequences.** Fire clears the trench for a year, and what burns does not rise again. The reading quiets it for good. A party that does neither is overrun, and the dead do not kill so much as lay their cold hands on a man until he lies down in the cut. The survivors wake in the gray before dawn with the gold plates pressed to their own eyes.

**If bypassed.** A party that flees the wash runs the whole way to the city with the dead in the road behind them. Nobody in the east gate wants to hear what is coming up the road, and the prefect has them arrested for bringing it, with the fire-pot as evidence.

## Scene Five: The Sixty

**Purpose.** Reveal the Ātárzád secret and pay the reward.

**Situation.** At dawn the party sees the rest of the trench. The light comes up gray and flat over the gravel, and the cut looks smaller than it did at night. In the lowest layer, apart from the household, lie sixty bodies in Zhōqarōd leather. Their hands are folded on their chests. They are the men who died in the palace court three days after the fall, when the palace household rose against the garrison, and the fire-priests would not burn them because the court was defiled. The leather is stiff with salt and the folded hands are tied with cord, as a soldier's would be, by comrades who had no time to do better.

**NPCs and threats.** Rōqeshōr arrives at sunrise. He wants the Zhōqarōd dead burned and the Tellumi dead left; the party must decide. He stands at the lip with the burnt hand unwrapped, looking down at the folded men, and does not come closer. The prefect's riders come down the wash by midday, and their captain is a man who has been waiting a long time for an excuse to hang a foreigner. If the party kept the chain or the seal, the captain asks for it. If the party has told anyone what the trench holds, the captain knows it already.

**Player choices.** Force: burn everything and bury the evidence; the prefect never learns what the garrison's priests have known, and the household burns unreceived with the sixty. Stealth: take the seals and leave before the riders arrive. Guile: sell the Ātárzád secret to the prefect's rivals in the garrison, who want the fire-priests discredited and will pay in favor, not coin. Parley: bring Rōqeshōr and a Shēkhulsīghot to the same grave, the first time in ninety years, and let both rites be said.

**Clues and rewards.** The party leaves with some gold: a pair of lamp-stands, a cup and a handful of seals, worth a few weights of silver each. Khávar's signet, if found, is worth a place at his table in Amradad. The chain of office is a relic, and the Tellumi of the quarter will give a party that returns it a good deal more than a goldsmith would. A party that reads the names to the end hears the lamps of the quarter, on the next night, lit early.

**Consequences.** The Ātárzád secret is out. The prefect learns the garrison's fire-priests have known for ninety years and what they have kept from him, and Rōqeshōr is recalled to answer for it. The Tellumi learn the Zhōqarōd dead lie among theirs, and the quarter cannot decide whether this is grief or insult.

**If bypassed.** A party that leaves without the lowest layer never learns the truth. They hear it from Rōqeshōr within the year, in the mouth of a man who has been broken by a hearing and wants an audience.

## Aftermath

- **If the dead are burned.** The wash is quiet for a season and the gold is lost with the bodies. The Tellumi quarter is not told, and the lamps at its doors burn for a household that no one has avenged. Hosikqenod's band scatters among the tribes, and the stories they take with them are of a grave that screamed when it burned.
- **If the names are read.** The household is received. The Tellumi quarter learns it and lights its lamps for a different reason. The chieftain's sons, if any lived, come home with their eyes on the ground and their mouths shut. Khávar hears of it at Amradad and sends for the party.
- **If both rites are said.** The Zhōqarōd and the Tellumi share a grave, and the garrison cannot decide what to do about it. The prefect forbids the story and cannot forbid the lamps.
- **If the party fails.** The trench is deeper, the raiders are dead and the dead begin to walk toward the city. The east gate is shut at dusk for the first time in ninety years.
