A dust storm strips the party from its caravan in the Long Dry, and their guide brings them by the stars to the one water he knows: [[place-qelodwatri|Kelodvootri]], an owned spring of twelve palms whose well lies under a stone wrapped in chains. Its owners are two exhausted riders who forbid anyone to go near the well, and their water-warden went down the shaft three days ago and has not come up. Something lives in the cistern under the palms, and every sign the party meets says it is hunting. It is not. It is the true owner of the water, and it is collecting the debt that every tribe of the sand range collects from a trespasser: one person held until the debt is paid, a beast for each skin of water drawn, and the stranger's rope laid by the stone as proof.

## Overview

The adventure runs from one dusk to the second dawn. Its clock is thirst: the party arrives with a day's water, the owners' spare skins are nearly empty, and the next water is three days off across open sand. The only water within reach is in the cistern, and the cistern belongs to the **Bodekud**, a [[lore-dreadspawncrtr|dreadspawn]] that the owners have called a [[being-qelodud|kelodud]] for generations. Their own word for it means "the one who owns the water", and they have forgotten that the word is meant plainly.

The adventure follows the customs of [[lore-ownedoasis|the owned oasis]] throughout, twice over. The riders are the owners the party can see, and a party that asks for guest-right and keeps it is protected by them. The Bodekud is the owner the party cannot see, and it keeps the same custom more strictly than the tribe does. Every scene below gives several ways through and says how play carries on if the party skips, fails or cuts short what the scene offers.

## What Is Really Happening

The full truth of the spring—what the Bodekud is, the custom it keeps, what it has done this season and what each of the tribe's tellings is worth—is in [[place-qelodwatri#truth|the truth of Kelodvootri]]. In short: the warden drew six skins without asking the owner below, went down on his own rope and fought when he was held, and the custom answered with blood. Four skins are still owed when the party arrives. Every beast the Bodekud takes pays one of them, and it holds one of the debtors alive until the rest are paid. This section covers what that truth does at the table.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The party must reach Scene Three believing that something below is hunting it, and must be able to see by the end of that scene that it is collecting. Everything the Bodekud does is exact and countable: one beast for one skin, only beasts that drank drawn water, and the held kept alive. Keep every act consistent with that, and the pattern does the revealing.

### How the reveal lands

The reveal lands in Scene Three, and it reaches the party by whichever route it took.

- **At the palms.** When the tethered camel is reeled in and Gorenghakhiq's riding camel beside it is left standing, Gorenghakhiq counts aloud. Six skins on the warden's cord; two of the riders' camels taken before the party came; one tonight; and tonight the winding stopped at three. He says it plainly: the thing is collecting, and it keeps the custom.
- **In the cleft or below.** Budaqtoliz leads the party down the steps by moonlight to the first turning, where the lamp reaches across the water to the air pocket under the south ledge. The riders' two camels stand in it alive, tied to stones in the warden's hitch. Numaqlun, seeing them, says the word the tribe does not use in front of strangers, and says what it means.
- **Already in the cistern at midnight.** The party watches an arm bring the warden's rope down the overflow channel, coil it on the ledge beside the drawing-basin, and lay his knife beside it, its blade dark with blood. It is the proof of the drawing and the proof of the fight, set out together as an owner sets them before the eldest.

> [!TIP]
> Land the reveal on Gorenghakhiq's count, Numaqlun's word or the knife on the ledge, then stop. Let the players go back over the scenes behind them themselves: the rope by the stone, the winding that never finished, the guide who would not drink.

> [!TIP]
> Reward a party that guesses early, and never deny a right guess. A party that suspects in Scene One or Two that the thing is keeping the custom can test it at once: pour water back into the pool before drawing, or offer a beast into the water through the crack in the slab. Let the test work. The winding drops by one, nothing is taken that night, and the party has earned an easier way down.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not let Gorenghakhiq or Numaqlun explain the custom before Scene Three. Gorenghakhiq may say the saying, read the rope and decline the water, and each of those must pass as a guide's habit. If he draws the conclusion aloud in Scene One, the reversal is spent before the party can act on it.

### What the party wants afterward

Once the reveal lands, the party wants to settle a debt, not win a fight. The way to bring Budaqtoliz up is to pay for him: a beast or a weight given into the water for each skin still owed. The way to drink is to ask, by pouring water back into the pool first. The trap and the kill stay possible, and they now cost the tribe its water and the party its standing, because killing an owner at its own water is the one act the custom answers with blood, and the Kuuyoyur reckon by the same custom. Scene Five's reckoning gains a party the tribe never counted on: the owner below.

> [!WARNING]
> A party that has already set its heart on the trap may carry on with it after the reveal. That closes the courteous route for good. Let it, and let the consequences in Scene Five and the Aftermath follow from what the party knew when it chose.

## Hooks

- **The thief.** The caravan's master carries a debt-token of the Kuuyoyur, and a factor in the string has offered the party a share to learn where the tribe keeps its reserve. The storm puts the party at the reserve's door with the factor nowhere in sight.
- **The mercenary or common soldier.** The party is the caravan's hired guard, and the guard's contract holds them to the string's pack-camels. Four of those camels are with them when the dust lifts, and the contract is paid when the camels reach the hand-off well.
- **The leader.** The party's captain answers for everyone who was cut off with them: the guide, a drover boy and whatever beasts they kept. Every one of them is the captain's to bring back.
- **The lone wanderer.** The wanderer has traveled with the caravan to pay off a passage debt and has nothing on the road but the guide's good opinion, which the wanderer will need to reach anywhere at all.

## The Opening

Read or paraphrase:

> The dust took the caravan at noon. When it lifted, the string was gone, and there was only your guide, your own beasts and the skins hanging flat against their sides. Gorenghakhiq brought you north by the stars to the foot of a black mesa and a ring of twelve palms.
>
> A horse skull on a pole faces you down the approach. In the middle of the palms is a well, and across its mouth lies a slab of stone wrapped in three chains, pegged into the ground at three points with stakes of fresh wood. Beside it, in the shade of the palms, lies an older slab, cracked in two. The camels will not go nearer than the pole.
>
> Your guide gets down, tears a strip from his headcloth, ties it to the pole and squats on his heels with his hands on his knees, as the custom requires. In the quiet, from beneath the stone, comes a sound everyone here knows from every well on the road: the creak and pause of a rope wound up a shaft, hand over hand, a bucket coming up.
>
> The owners of a well carry their rope and bucket away with them. Gorenghakhiq does not look at the well. He looks at you, and keeps his hands where you can see them.

> [!TIP]
> Let the winding stop before anyone speaks, and count it out under your breath at the table: four pulls, a pause, a splash. The players should hear the same four every time it comes tonight.

## Scene One: The Cloth on the Pole

**Purpose.** Entrance and guardian. Establish the custom, the thirst, the owners and the first unease, which still has an ordinary explanation.

> The sun goes down behind the mesa and the palms go black against the sky. At last light two figures come down out of the rocks at the mesa foot on foot, leading one thin camel between them. They stop at a spear's length from the pole. The older is a woman in a dusty black wrap; the younger is a boy with a strung bow, and he is watching the palms, not you.

**Situation.** The party waits at the pole while the sun goes down. The winding comes from under the stone at intervals: always four turns, then a pause, then a soft splash, as if a bucket had been let fall back into the water and the winding begun again. The two riders have been watching from the cleft since the party came into view.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **Numaqlun** is the elder of the two, a lean woman in a dusty black wrap, the wife of the tribe's water-warden. She offers salt and guest-right at once, because the custom requires it and because she has not spoken to anyone but the boy for three days. Her voice is level and her hands shake. She forbids anyone to go within ten paces of the well, and she says, before anyone asks, that the warden is below at the cleaning.
- **Budaqtoliz** is her nephew, sixteen, with a bow he keeps strung. He says nothing. Throughout the evening he watches the palms instead of the desert, and when the winding starts he turns his head toward the palm roots, not the well.
- **Gorenghakhiq**, the party's guide, is a gray, careful man of the grass, who has crossed the Khoshiqor fourteen times and has never lost a string. He knows the custom to the letter and keeps it. He knows the [[being-qelodud|kelodud]], and he has never heard a lidded well sound like this. While the party waits at the pole he says, to nobody in particular, what the guides of the grass say at every owned water: that the tribes hold the wells of the road in trust until the owners return.

> [!TIP]
> Give Gorenghakhiq his saying as a proverb, said once while the light goes, the way a man says something he has said at every well for forty years. Do not repeat it and do not dwell on it. It comes back in Scene Three with its second meaning.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley:_ wait at the pole, accept the salt and ask for water. Numaqlun gives what she has, which is a mouthful each from her last skin, and at her word Budaqtoliz waters the party's beasts from the last of the riders' skins. She says the well cannot be opened until the tribe comes. The party is now under guest-right, and protected, and bound by it. Gorenghakhiq thanks her, declines his mouthful, and waters his riding camel from his own skin.
- _Force:_ go to the well, or push past the riders to it. Two exhausted riders cannot stop the party, but a fight at a bodek is a blood-debt the tribe collects (see Scene Five). The chains are pegged tight, and lifting the slab takes four strong backs.
- _Stealth:_ accept the salt and creep to the well after dark to draw through the crack in the slab. Every skin the party draws is a skin more owed: from that night the winding runs one pull longer for each, and the rope the party let down turns up coiled by the stone in its own knot by morning. Read Scene Three's chains from the well's edge: the party is the first to see the slab move.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ read the marks. The stakes are fresh and the old lid is cracked straight through. The tribe's sign on the oldest palm has an older carving beneath it, half grown over by the bark: a figure tipping a jar into a pool, and a long shape below the pool with its arms spread. Handprints round the well-ring have dried to a white crust that smells of standing water, and each print has fingers too long for the palm and no lines on the palm at all.
- _Giving something up:_ offer a beast or a blade for water, as the custom allows a stranger. Numaqlun cannot sell what she cannot draw, and says so, and the offer earns her trust at once.

**Clues and rewards.** The winding is not a man at work: it stops at four every time, and the bucket never arrives. The old lid is cracked. Budaqtoliz watches the palms. The handprints are not a kelodud's, which has no hands, and Gorenghakhiq says so quietly to whoever is beside him. The guide will not drink the riders' water, and asked why, he says only that a guide drinks what he carries until the owner has said yes.

> [!WARNING]
> Gorenghakhiq's refusal is a courtesy of the road, and it must read as one. If a player presses him, he says Numaqlun has been generous and he has water of his own. He does not say "owner" twice.

**Consequences.** Guest-right makes Numaqlun an ally and the party bound to her rules; she relaxes them as trust grows. Every beast and every person who drank the riders' water has drunk what was drawn without the owner's leave, and the Bodekud counts it. A party that draws without leave, or fights, has a debt to the tribe and an enemy in Budaqtoliz, who still knows what Scene Three needs.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that arrives in the dark and never sees the pole camps by the palms; the riders come down at first light, sit at the water's edge and wait to be offered tea, and Scene One plays at dawn with the party already in the wrong. A party that kills or drives off the riders finds their camp in the cleft: the warden's tally-cord, his spare sandals, and Numaqlun's three days of scratches on a stone, one for each night the winding came. A party that turns back into the sand meets the thirst: no water for three days, the camels turning their heads toward the palms, and Gorenghakhiq refusing to leave a string's worth of beasts to die. He walks them back to the pole at dusk.

## Scene Two: The Wet Rope

**Purpose.** Discovery. The ordinary explanation fails, and the party learns what happened, where the other way in lies and what the tribe has forgotten.

> After the meal the fire is low and the palms are still. By the well, on the sand beside the chained stone, lies a coil of rope that was not there at sunset. It is coiled flat and tight, and it is wet.

**Situation.** The rope lies beside the well-stone, coiled the way a water-warden coils, and its end is tied off in the warden's own hitch, which Numaqlun knows as well as her own name. It is streaked with blood along a forearm's length near the knot. Budaqtoliz says he coiled nothing. Numaqlun says one thing—that her husband went down on that rope three days ago—and sits down on the sand beside it.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **Numaqlun** answers what she is asked and offers nothing on the night. Her husband is **Voghalkenod**. She will say that the old lid cracked across its middle as he went down, with a sound like a struck pot, and that she will not talk about the rest.
- **Gorenghakhiq** looks at the rope a long while. He says, low, that among the grass tribes an owner who catches a stranger drawing lays the stranger's rope by the well-stone for the eldest to see, and that he has never seen it done to an owner's own warden. He is now frightened, which he has never been on the road.
- **A [[being-bnscrpn|bone scorpion]]** lies in the shade at the mouth of the cleft in the mesa foot, where a cold breath comes up out of the rock. The riders camp on the far side of the cleft from it and have not gone near the passage.

> [!TIP]
> Let the table take Gorenghakhiq's remark as the thing below mocking the riders with the dead man's habits. That is the first reading, and it is the frightening one. Do not correct it.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley:_ sit with Numaqlun and ask about the well, not the warden. She knows the passage from the cleft to the cistern ledge, which the wardens walk once a generation, and the shutter on the overflow, which the warden closes before he clears the sump. Asked about old customs, she remembers her grandmother pouring the first draw of every season back into the shaft, and calling it letting the well drink first.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ compare the carving under the tribe's sign on the oldest palm with that custom. The figure is tipping a jar into a pool, and the long shape below it has its arms spread. Search the palm roots, where the ground is damp: wet furrows run from a hole among the roots toward the well and back, a forearm wide, with the marks of fingers at their ends.
- _Cleverness with the camp:_ search the riders' camp in the cleft. There are picket pegs for three camels and one camel on them. The warden's tally-cord hangs from his saddle, knotted six times for the skins drawn on the first evening, as a warden counts the season's water. His spare sandals are there, and his spare water-skin, full: the riders have not touched it, because it is his. A flat stone carries three scratches, one for each night the winding came.
- _Force:_ clear the bone scorpion from the cleft. It fights until it cannot move, and pain does not turn it.
- _Stealth:_ pass the scorpion as the venom-hunters do, in the last hour before dawn when it is slowest, keeping out of its shade; or bait it out of the cleft with a goat.
- _Giving something up:_ test the well as a hand-off keeper does, by lowering a bag of sand on a rope through the crack in the slab. The rope goes slack. A while later it is wound back up, slowly, without the bag, and its end is tied in the party's own knot.

**Clues and rewards.** The passage exists and starts at the cleft. The overflow shutter exists and traps the cistern's water, and whatever is in it. The thing comes out by the palm roots. It is not a kelodud: Gorenghakhiq says a kelodud does not climb into the open where the wind is, does not return what it takes, and cannot tie a knot. The tribe once poured water back before drawing, and the carving shows the same act. The tally-cord says six skins were drawn; the pegs say two camels are gone.

> [!TIP]
> Put the tally-cord into a player's hands, not into a description. Six knots on a cord is a plain thing, and a player who counts them now has the arithmetic Gorenghakhiq does aloud in Scene Three.

**Consequences.** A party that clears the scorpion has the passage open. The sand-bag is a weight given into the water: the next time the winding comes, it stops at three. A party that notices has found the debt, and a party that does not has still paid a skin of it.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that never sees the rope at the well finds it on the cistern ledge in Scene Four, coiled beside the drawing-basin, still wet. A party that goes straight into the passage tonight meets Scene Three below ground (see that scene). A party that never hears Numaqlun learns the passage from Budaqtoliz in Scene Three, the shutter from the bar and the counterweight on the ledge in Scene Four, and the pouring-back from the carvings on the passage walls. A party that never searches the camp hears the count from Numaqlun in Scene Three, when Gorenghakhiq asks her how many skins her husband drew.

## Scene Three: The Chains

**Purpose.** The setback and the reveal. The horror shows that it is active and takes something of the party's, the way back to the road closes, and the party sees what the thing below is doing.

> Midnight, and the palms are still. Then the chains begin to move. Links grind against the slab, a stake creaks and starts out of the ground, and the stone shifts toward the well-mouth as if something below had hold of it. Behind the palms, where your beasts are tethered, one of the camels is simply gone.

**Situation.** The camp lies quiet at the custom's two hundred paces from the water, or nearer if the party has chosen otherwise. Something has got the chains through the crack and is hauling on them from below. The winding is loud now, and it stops at three. The party's tethered camel is gone without a sound, and its tether comes back across the sand a moment later, end first, moving steadily toward the palms, and goes down into the hole among the roots. Gorenghakhiq's riding camel, tethered on the same line, stands where it was.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **The Bodekud**, seen for the first time: a pale arm comes up out of the palm roots in the dark, jointed in too many places, longer than the palm is tall, and draws back down the hole like a hand into a sleeve. It passes over Gorenghakhiq's camel to reach the one beside it.
- **Budaqtoliz** breaks. He saw the arms two nights ago in the palm roots and said nothing, because Numaqlun would not hear that the warden was anything but lost. Now he admits that the thing has learned to leave by the old overflow channel.
- **Numaqlun**, if anyone suggests the thing is still holding her husband, wants to go down at once.
- **Gorenghakhiq** stands by his untouched camel with his hand on its neck, and counts.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This scene must establish that the takings follow the debt. The beast taken is one that drank drawn water, the one left standing is the guide's, and the winding is one shorter than it was at dusk. Whatever else happens tonight, describe those three things.

**Player choices.**

- _Force:_ haul back on the chains with ropes and beasts, or drive the stakes deeper. The party can hold the slab for the night. Whatever pulls from below is strong, but it lets go before dawn.
- _Stealth:_ lie still among the palms and watch the overflow mouth. The arms come up again before dawn, and a watcher sees how far they reach, that they never cross the sunlit side of the palms by day, and that they feel along the tether lines and pass over a beast as a buyer passes over stock.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ build a fire across the hole in the roots. The arm that touches the heat goes rigid and slowly withdraws, and later the fire's ashes are drawn down into the hole. Tie a wet saddle-cloth to a line as a lure and see where it is taken.
- _Parley:_ get Budaqtoliz to say what he saw, and have him take the party to the cleft by moonlight. At the first turning of the steps the lamp reaches across the water to the air pocket under the south ledge, where the riders' two camels stand alive, tied to stones.
- _Giving something up:_ give it a beast to buy the night. Numaqlun says the tribe did this once, generations ago, and stopped. The beast is taken, nothing else is, and the winding drops by one.

**The reveal, at the palms.** Gorenghakhiq asks Numaqlun how many skins her husband drew, or takes the tally-cord from the party, and counts aloud. Six skins. Two camels before the party came. One tonight, and never his, because he drank nothing drawn and watered his beast from his own skin. Four pulls at dusk, three now. Then he says it: the thing is collecting, and it keeps the custom. The wells are held in trust until the owners return, and this owner never left.

**The reveal, in the cleft.** Numaqlun, at the first turning, sees the camels alive in the air pocket and stops on the step. She says the tribe's word for the thing in the well, **Bodekud**, and what it means: the one who owns the water. Then she says that the tribe has said it as a name since her grandmother's grandmother, and that an owner keeps the beasts he takes alive.

**The reveal, below.** A party already in the cistern at midnight hears the chains clatter down through the crack in the roof above the pool, and watches the camel come down the overflow channel past its lamp, dragged by an arm, without a sound, to be tied in the air pocket beside the riders' two. Then an arm brings the warden's rope down the channel, coils it on the ledge beside the drawing-basin, and lays his knife beside it, its blade dark with blood. Budaqtoliz's voice calls the party's names down the shaft from above, then stops.

> [!TIP]
> Gorenghakhiq's count is the most reliable of the three, because it needs only the tally-cord and the tether line. If the party went below by the passage and the cistern route is not in play, bring him down the steps behind it with the count already done.

> [!WARNING]
> A party that has already killed the riders still meets the reveal: Gorenghakhiq counts from the tally-cord in their camp. Do not let the reveal wait for Scene Four because a witness is gone.

**Clues and rewards.** The overflow channel is its road. Dry heat stills it. It takes without a sound, takes only what is owed, and keeps what it takes alive. Budaqtoliz knows the cleft and the steps below it as far as the first turning.

**Consequences.** Before dawn, the Bodekud takes one more. Budaqtoliz, who drank the drawn water at dusk, goes to watch the palms with his bow if he is still unguarded, and does not come back; his bow lies in the roots, still strung. He is the one it holds until the debt is paid. If he is guarded, the thing takes another of the party's beasts that drank, with the last skin of drawn water hung on it, and the winding stops at two. It never touches Gorenghakhiq's camel. Either way, the dust rises at dawn and covers every track back toward the road, and the remaining camels will not be led away from the only water they can smell. If the party let the slab go, it drops into the shaft with a long crash. The well-mouth is then open, the winding can be heard all night from inside it, and the open shaft is a third way down.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that flees into the sand finds no water by noon, the camels turned back toward the mesa on their own, and Budaqtoliz's bow lying across the track where the dust has not covered it, pointing toward the palms; Gorenghakhiq, walking back beside them, does his count on the way. However the night goes, the party wakes with less water than it had, a companion or a beast in the cistern, no road out, and the means to know what is owed.

## Scene Four: The Water Chamber

**Purpose.** Confrontation and revelation. The source of every sign is in front of the party, and the party can use what it has learned: pay, ask, trap or fight.

> The air goes cold enough to show your breath. Below you a long hall of black water lies still under a roof of cut stone, with ledges running round it and chains hanging down through a crack above the pool. Somewhere under the far ledge, something breathes that is not you.

**Situation.** By the cleft, the passage drops in narrow steps between carved walls to a ledge above the hall. By the open shaft, a rope goes down into the dark straight over the middle of the pool. By the overflow channel, a party crawls on its belly through the palm roots and wet stone and comes out at the water's surface under the south ledge, which is the worst way in and the only one the Bodekud uses.

The drawing-basin cut into the north ledge holds clean water. On the ledge, beside a bar set in a slot and a counterweight on a chain, is the stone shutter of the overflow, raised. The carvings on the ledge face show figures with jars pouring water back into the pool, and the long shape beneath each with its arms spread. Voghalkenod's rope lies coiled beside the drawing-basin, and his knife beside it, dark with blood.

Under the south ledge, half in the water, is the air pocket. In it, tied to stones with good rope in a warden's hitch, are the things the Bodekud keeps: the camels it has taken, still breathing; Budaqtoliz, if he was taken, cold and alive; and below the water beneath them, weighted with a stone, the warden.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **The Bodekud** hangs beneath the ledges with its arms spread over the pool. Its body is never fully seen: a pale bulk under the stone, wet, larger than a camel, from which the arms come. It does not hurry. It holds one person at a time, draws him under the ledge and ties him in the air pocket, and lets him go when the debt is paid. It leaves alone anyone who has poured water from the pool back into it before drawing, while that person stays at the pool. Dry heat makes it go still, and fire on its arms makes it pull them under. It does not climb the passage into the dry air and does not follow anyone up the shaft into daylight.
- **Numaqlun**, if she came, goes to the air pocket for her husband and cannot be stopped from it.

> [!TIP]
> Play the Bodekud as an owner receiving debtors. It waits. When the party pours back, the arms draw aside; when a beast or a weight goes into the water, one arm takes it under without haste and the next arm draws back from the air pocket. Let the players feel the transaction before anyone names it.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley, in its own fashion:_ pour water back into the pool, each person in turn, as the carvings show, and walk the ledge to the air pocket. The Bodekud lets them pass while they stay at the pool. It does not let them take what it keeps without payment: a beast or a weight given into the water for each skin still owed, and when the debt is paid, it lets the held one go. This is the way the custom offers, and it is the only way that leaves the spring, the owner and the tribe's standing as they were.
- _Giving something up:_ pay the debt with the party's own beasts and goods, or leave something it will keep in place of each life the party takes back. Numaqlun offers herself in place of Budaqtoliz, and means it.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ trap it below. With the Bodekud lured into the chamber, drop the shutter across the overflow mouth by knocking out the bar; the counterweight drags it home. It is now shut into its cistern with the lid above it. The bar is on the ledge within reach of its arms, so whoever knocks it out must be protected, or must have poured water back first.
- _Force:_ collapse its ledge. The crack that broke the lid ran through the south ledge too, and a party with levers, or with the chains from above hauled by beasts, can bring the ledge down on the arms beneath it. That ruins the south half of the pool with silt for a season and leaves the drawing-basin clean.
- _Force:_ kill it. Fire and dry heat are what it cannot bear. Its blood turns the pool bitter, and a party that kills it in the pool has no water to drink until the spring clears; a party that first fills every skin from the drawing-basin, or draws the creature into the overflow channel at noon when the hot wind blows down it, keeps the water.
- _Stealth:_ go in by the passage at noon, when the dry wind of the overflow channel has driven it deep, and cut the held free while it lies still. The debt stands, and the winding comes again that night.

> [!WARNING]
> The custom answers a fight with blood. A party that strikes the Bodekud while it holds someone is answered at once: it lets the held one go and closes on whoever struck it, to draw him under and drown him. Say so plainly before the first blow lands if the party knows the custom by now; a party that does not has the warden's knife in front of it.

**Clues and rewards.** Everything above becomes plain. The winding was the warden's rope reeled up and let fall, one pull for each skin owed. The rope by the stone was the proof of the drawing, and the knife on the ledge is the proof of the fight. The chains were hauled through the crack by an owner whose well had been lidded against it, and the tether was reeled in like a bucket. The carvings show that the builders knew it, and kept the courtesy, and drew. They do not show where it came from.

**Consequences.** Paid and asked, the Bodekud lives, the spring stays as it was, Budaqtoliz comes up alive, and the party knows what owns the water. Trapped below, the Bodekud lives, the overflow is shut and the palms will die within a few years unless the tribe opens it again. Killed, it leaves the spring bitter for a season and the tribe without its reserve for a dry year. Budaqtoliz, if he was taken, lives if the party reaches him before the second night.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that comes down unprepared is not killed at once: the Bodekud takes one of them, if he drank drawn water, draws him under the ledge and holds him, and lets go when the debt is paid or the held one is cut free of the stone. Survivors can escape up the passage, which it will not climb, or up the shaft into the light at noon, or out through the overflow channel when the hot wind blows down it. A party that flees the chamber still has the dry shelf in Scene Five, which opens off the passage above the reach of its arms. If the party never comes down at all, the tribe's riders arrive at dawn on the fourth day (see Scene Five), and the tribe opens the cistern itself, with the party standing as witness or as debtor.

## Scene Five: The Dry Shelf

**Purpose.** The treasure, the reckoning with the owners the party can see, and a way out.

> At dawn the gravel above the mesa is full of riders. They come down without hurry and spread round the palms until every road is covered. The eldest gets down, walks to the edge of the water, and sits.

**Situation.** Halfway down the passage, behind a slab that sits in a groove and slides aside, a dry shelf is cut into the rock above the highest water the spring has ever reached. It holds the Kuuyoyur's reserve for a ruinous year: silver in sealed jars, strings of turquoise, and two fine swords wrapped in oiled cloth, the price the tribe was paid to end a feud four generations ago. Beside them hangs a cord of passage tokens of the tribe. Numaqlun knows where the shelf is; Budaqtoliz does not.

At dawn on the fourth day, if not before, a dozen riders of the Kuuyoyur come down out of the gravel and spread round the palms so that every road is covered. Their eldest sits at the edge of the water and waits to be offered tea.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **The Kuuyoyur riders**, who ask what happened and compare it with what Numaqlun says. They are not in a hurry.
- **Numaqlun** speaks for the party or against it, according to what it did.
- **The Bodekud**, below, whose debt was the tribe's before it was anyone's.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley:_ give an account and accept what the tribe offers: a share of the reserve, water for every skin, a passage token and a rider to escort the party to the road.
- _Force:_ take the shelf and go before the riders come. A party with the reserve and no guide has three days of open sand ahead of it, and the tribe sends riders after it to the next three wells.
- _Stealth:_ take a little and say nothing. The tribe counts its jars.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ trade knowledge for silver. The tribe needs to know what the Bodekud is, what the warden owed, and how to ask it for water, and it pays for a full account. A party that tells the eldest what the tally-cord and the knife mean has told him that his tribe is a guest at its own reserve.
- _Giving something up:_ refuse payment and ask only for water, a token and a rider. The tribe holds that a debt of this size is the tribe's, not the party's, and a party that refuses payment has made itself a friend of the Kuuyoyur for a generation.

> [!TIP]
> Let the eldest take the account in silence, then get up from the water's edge before the tea comes. He goes to the drawing-basin, or to the crack in the slab, and pours the first water back before anything is drawn. That is how the tribe says it has understood.

**Clues and rewards.** The share is a jar of silver for the party and one of the two swords, whichever the eldest chooses, or both swords if the party brought Budaqtoliz up alive. A party that paid the warden's debt out of its own beasts has every one replaced from the tribe's herd, because the debt was the tribe's. Every skin is filled. The passage token admits the party to any Kuuyoyur well, and their escort, a rider named by the eldest, takes them by the shortest way to the road, where the caravan has waited two days at the next well.

**Consequences.** The tribe judges the party by the customs of the owned oasis: guests who kept guest-right are paid; strangers who drew water without leave owe a skin's price for every skin, set against what they did in the cistern; anyone who fought the owners pays blood or work. A party that killed the Bodekud has killed an owner at its own water, and the eldest names it so: the tribe pays what it promised and takes the party's names, and the party is not welcome at a Kuuyoyur well again. A party that freed Budaqtoliz is owed by the boy's household for life.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that never found the shelf is paid from it anyway if it served the tribe: Numaqlun goes down for the jars herself while the eldest pours the tea. A party whose riders both died takes its account to the dozen at dawn without a witness; the riders find the warden's tally-cord in the party's hands and believe it, if the party has not taken from the shelf. A party that left with nothing has still found water, and Gorenghakhiq walks it to the road by the stars.

## Aftermath

- **If the debt was paid and the courtesy kept.** The Kuuyoyur pour the first draw back again, and the Bodekud keeps its side for as long as they do. The tribe adds a cut to the oldest palm, beside the builders' figure with the jar, and stops calling the thing below a kelodud.
- **If the shutter is down.** The Bodekud is shut in its cistern. The Kuuyoyur lose the reserve while they decide whether to open the overflow and keep the courtesy, or let the palms die and dig a new well forty paces off.
- **If the Bodekud is dead.** The spring runs bitter until the next season, and the tribe drinks elsewhere in a dry year. The body, if anyone raises it, is too large to have come down the shaft. The other tribes of the Khoshiqor hear that strangers killed an owner at its water.
- **If the party robbed the shelf.** The tribe has a feud with the party, and every owned water of the Khoshiqor hears of it by the next season.
- **Whatever happened.** At the next lidded well on the road, Gorenghakhiq stops the string short of it, ties his cloth to the pole, and pours a little from his own skin onto the stone before he lets the camels drink.
