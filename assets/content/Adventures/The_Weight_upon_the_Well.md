A dust storm strips the party from its caravan in the Long Dry, and their guide brings them by the stars to the one water he knows: [[place-qelodwatri|Kelödvootri]], an owned spring of twelve palms whose well lies under a stone wrapped in chains. Its owners are two exhausted riders who forbid anyone to go near the well, and their water-warden went down the shaft three days ago and has not come up. Something lives in the cistern under the palms. It keeps what it takes, it has learned to leave by the overflow channel, and it is pulling the chains down through the stone.

## Overview

The adventure runs from one dusk to the second dawn. Its clock is thirst: the party arrives with a day's water, the owners' spare skins are nearly empty, and the next water is three days off across open sand. The only water within reach is in the cistern, and the cistern belongs to the **Bödeküd**, a [[lore-dreadspawncrtr|dreadspawn]] of the old cistern that the owners have called a [[being-qelodud|kelödüd]] for generations because the truth was too large to say aloud.

The adventure follows the customs of [[lore-ownedoasis|the owned oasis]] throughout. A party that asks for guest-right and keeps it is protected by the owners; a party that draws water, fights the owners or fouls the pool runs up a debt that the tribe collects. Every scene below gives several ways through and says how play carries on if the party skips, fails or cuts short what the scene offers.

## Hooks

- **The thief.** The caravan's master carries a debt-token of the Küüyöyür, and a factor in the string has offered the party a share to learn where the tribe keeps its reserve. The storm puts the party at the reserve's door with the factor nowhere in sight.
- **The mercenary or common soldier.** The party is the caravan's hired guard, and the guard's contract holds them to the string's pack-camels. Four of those camels are with them when the dust lifts, and the contract is paid when the camels reach the hand-off well.
- **The leader.** The party's captain answers for everyone who was cut off with them: the guide, a drover boy and whatever beasts they kept. Every one of them is the captain's to bring back.
- **The lone wanderer.** The wanderer has travelled with the caravan to pay off a passage debt and has nothing on the road but the guide's good opinion, which the wanderer will need to reach anywhere at all.

## The Opening

Read or paraphrase:

> The dust took the caravan at noon. When it lifted, the string was gone, and there was only your guide, your own beasts and the skins hanging flat against their sides. Görenghakhiq brought you north by the stars to the foot of a black mesa and a ring of twelve palms.
>
> A horse skull on a pole faces you down the approach. In the middle of the palms is a well, and across its mouth lies a slab of stone wrapped in three chains, pegged into the ground at three points with stakes of fresh wood. Beside it, in the shade of the palms, lies an older slab, cracked in two. The camels will not go nearer than the pole.
>
> Your guide gets down, tears a strip from his headcloth, ties it to the pole and squats on his heels with his hands on his knees, as the custom requires. In the quiet, from beneath the stone, comes a sound everyone here knows from every well on the road: the creak and pause of a rope wound up a shaft, hand over hand, a bucket coming up.
>
> The owners of a well carry their rope and bucket away with them. Görenghakhiq does not look at the well. He looks at you, and keeps his hands where you can see them.

## Scene One: The Cloth on the Pole

**Purpose.** Entrance and guardian. Establish the custom, the thirst, the owners and the first unease, which still has an ordinary explanation.

**Situation.** The party waits at the pole while the sun goes down. The winding comes from under the stone at intervals: always the same count of turns, then a pause, then a soft splash, as if a bucket had been let fall back into the water and the winding begun again. At last light two riders come down out of the rocks at the mesa foot, on foot, leading one thin camel between them. They have been watching from the cleft since the party came into view.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **Numaqlun** is the elder of the two, a lean woman in a dusty black wrap, the wife of the tribe's water-warden. She offers salt and guest-right at once, because the custom requires it and because she has not spoken to anyone but the boy for three days. Her voice is level and her hands shake. She forbids anyone to go within ten paces of the well, and she says, before anyone asks, that the warden is below at the cleaning.
- **Budaqtoliz** is her nephew, sixteen, with a bow he keeps strung. He says nothing. Throughout the evening he watches the palms instead of the desert, and when the winding starts he turns his head toward the palm roots, not the well.
- **Görenghakhiq**, the party's guide, is a grey, careful man of the grass, who has crossed the Khoshiqor fourteen times and has never lost a string. He knows the custom to the letter and keeps it. He knows the [[being-qelodud|kelödüd]], and he has never heard a lidded well sound like this.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley:_ wait at the pole, accept the salt and ask for water. Numaqlun gives what she has, which is a mouthful each from her last skin, and says the well cannot be opened until the tribe comes. The party is now under guest-right, and protected, and bound by it.
- _Force:_ go to the well, or push past the riders to it. Two exhausted riders cannot stop the party, but a fight at a bödek is a blood-debt the tribe collects (see Aftermath). The chains are pegged tight, and lifting the slab takes four strong backs.
- _Stealth:_ accept the salt and creep to the well after dark to draw. Read Scene Three's chains from the well's edge: the party is the first to see the slab move.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ read the marks. The stakes are fresh and the old lid is cracked straight through. The tribe's sign on the oldest palm has an older carving beneath it, half grown over by the bark. Handprints round the well-ring have dried to a white crust that smells of standing water, and each print has fingers too long for the palm and no lines on the palm at all.
- _Giving something up:_ offer a beast or a blade for water, as the custom allows a stranger. Numaqlun cannot sell what she cannot draw, and says so, and the offer earns her trust at once.

**Clues and rewards.** The winding is not a man at work: it stops at the same count every time and the bucket never arrives. The old lid is cracked. Budaqtoliz watches the palms. The handprints are not a kelödüd's, which has no hands, and Görenghakhiq says so quietly to whoever is beside him.

**Consequences.** Guest-right makes Numaqlun an ally and the party bound to her rules; she relaxes them as trust grows. A party that draws without leave, or fights, has a debt to the tribe and an enemy in Budaqtoliz, who still knows what Scene Three needs.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that arrives in the dark and never sees the pole camps by the palms; the riders come down at first light, sit at the water's edge and wait to be offered tea, and Scene One plays at dawn with the party already in the wrong. A party that kills or drives off the riders finds their camp in the cleft: the warden's tally-cord, his spare sandals, and Numaqlun's three days of scratches on a stone, one for each night the winding came. A party that turns back into the sand meets the thirst: no water for three days, the camels turning their heads toward the palms, and Görenghakhiq refusing to leave a string's worth of beasts to die. He walks them back to the pole at dusk.

## Scene Two: The Wet Rope

**Purpose.** Discovery. The ordinary explanation fails, and the party learns what happened, where the other way in lies and what the tribe has forgotten.

**Situation.** After the meal, when the party goes to the water's edge or when Numaqlun takes them to it, a coil of rope lies beside the well-stone that was not there at sunset. It is wet. It is coiled the way a water-warden coils, flat and tight, and its end is tied off in the warden's own hitch, which Numaqlun knows as well as her own name. It is streaked with blood along a forearm's length near the knot. Budaqtoliz says he coiled nothing, and Numaqlun sits down on the sand beside the rope.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **Numaqlun** tells it plainly once the rope is there. Three days ago the warden, **Voghalyemök**, went down the shaft on that rope to clear the silt. As he went down, the old lid cracked across its middle with a sound like a struck pot, and the ground shook under her feet. The rope went slack at the bottom. They hauled up an empty loop, laid a fresh stone over the shaft and chained it, and have heard the winding every night since.
- **A [[being-bnscrpn|bone scorpion]]** lies in the shade at the mouth of the cleft in the mesa foot, where a cold breath comes up out of the rock. The riders camp on the far side of the cleft from it and have not gone near the passage.
- **Görenghakhiq** knows the [[being-qelodud|kelödüd]]'s habits as every guide does, and he is now frightened, which he has never been on the road.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley:_ sit with Numaqlun and let her talk. She knows the passage from the cleft to the cistern ledge, which the wardens walk once a generation, and the shutter on the overflow, which the warden closes before he clears the sump. Asked about old customs, she remembers her grandmother pouring the first draw of every season back into the shaft, and calling it letting the well drink first.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ compare the carving under the tribe's sign on the oldest palm with the custom. It shows a figure tipping a jar into a pool, and a long shape below the pool with its arms spread. Search the palm roots, where the ground is damp: wet furrows run from a hole among the roots toward the well and back, a forearm wide, with the marks of fingers at their ends.
- _Force:_ clear the bone scorpion from the cleft. It fights until it cannot move, and pain does not turn it.
- _Stealth:_ pass the scorpion as the venom-hunters do, in the last hour before dawn when it is slowest, keeping out of its shade; or bait it out of the cleft with a goat.
- _Giving something up:_ test the well as a hand-off keeper does, by lowering a bag of sand on a rope through the crack in the slab. The rope goes slack. A while later it is wound back up, slowly, without the bag, and its end is tied in Voghalyemök's hitch.

**Clues and rewards.** The passage exists and starts at the cleft. The overflow shutter exists and traps the cistern's water, and whatever is in it. The thing comes out by the palm roots. It is not a kelödüd: Görenghakhiq says a kelödüd does not climb into the open where the wind is, does not return what it takes, and cannot tie a knot. The tribe once poured water back before drawing, and the carving shows the same act. A party that searches the warden's gear in the riders' camp finds his spare water-skin full: the riders have not touched it, because it is his.

**Consequences.** A party that clears the scorpion has the passage open. A party that tests the well with the sand-bag has told the Bödeküd that there are more people at the top, and it comes to the palms that night with purpose.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that never sees the rope at the well finds it on the cistern ledge in Scene Four, coiled beside the drawing-basin, still wet. A party that goes straight into the passage tonight meets Scene Three below ground (see that scene). A party that never hears Numaqlun's account learns the passage from Budaqtoliz in Scene Three, and the shutter from the bar and the counterweight on the ledge in Scene Four, and the pouring-back from the carvings on the passage walls.

## Scene Three: The Chains

**Purpose.** The setback. The horror shows that it is active, it takes something of the party's, and the way back to the road closes.

**Situation.** Midnight. The camp lies quiet at the custom's two hundred paces from the water, or nearer if the party has chosen otherwise. Then the chains begin to tighten. The links grind against the slab, one stake creaks and starts to rise out of the ground, and the slab itself shifts, pulled down into the well-mouth by something that has got the chains through the crack and is hauling on them from below. The winding is loud now. Then, behind the palms, the party's tethered camel is gone. There was no sound. Its tether comes back across the sand a moment later, end first, moving steadily toward the palms, and goes down into the hole among the roots.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **The Bödeküd**, seen for the first time: a pale arm comes up out of the palm roots in the dark, jointed in too many places, longer than the palm is tall, and draws back down the hole like a hand into a sleeve.
- **Budaqtoliz** breaks. He saw the arms two nights ago in the palm roots and said nothing, because Numaqlun would not hear that the warden was anything but lost. Now he admits that the thing has learned to leave by the old overflow channel.
- **Numaqlun**, if anyone suggests the thing is still holding her husband, wants to go down at once.

**Player choices.**

- _Force:_ haul back on the chains with ropes and beasts, or drive the stakes deeper. The party can hold the slab for the night. Whatever pulls from below is strong, but it lets go before dawn.
- _Stealth:_ lie still among the palms and watch the overflow mouth. The arms come up for the next beast, and a watcher sees how far they reach and that they never cross the sunlit side of the palms by day.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ build a fire across the hole in the roots. The arm that touches the heat goes rigid and slowly withdraws, and later the fire's ashes are drawn down into the hole. Tie a wet saddle-cloth to a line as a lure and see where it is taken.
- _Parley:_ get Budaqtoliz to say what he saw, and have him take the party to the cleft by moonlight.
- _Giving something up:_ give it a beast to buy the night. Numaqlun says the tribe did this once, generations ago, and stopped.

**Clues and rewards.** The overflow channel is its road. Dry heat stills it. It takes without a sound and keeps what it takes. Budaqtoliz knows the cleft and the steps below it as far as the first turning.

**Consequences.** Before dawn, the Bödeküd takes one more. Budaqtoliz, if he is still unguarded, goes to watch the palms with his bow and does not come back; his bow lies in the roots, still strung. If he is guarded, the thing takes Görenghakhiq's riding camel and the skin of water hung on it, and the party's water is gone. Either way, the dust rises at dawn and covers every track back toward the road, and the remaining camels will not be led away from the only water they can smell. If the party let the slab go, it drops into the shaft with a long crash. The well-mouth is then open, the winding can be heard all night from inside it, and the open shaft is a third way down.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party already below ground when midnight comes hears the chains clatter down through the crack in the roof above the pool, and watches the camel come down the overflow channel past their lamp, dragged by an arm, without a sound; Budaqtoliz's voice calls their names down the shaft from above, then stops. A party that flees into the sand finds no water by noon, the camels turned back toward the mesa on their own, and Budaqtoliz's bow lying across the track where the dust has not covered it, pointing toward the palms. However the night goes, the party wakes with less water than it had, a companion or a beast in the cistern and no road out.

## Scene Four: The Water Chamber

**Purpose.** Confrontation and revelation. The source of every sign is in front of the party, and the party can use what it has learned.

**Situation.** By the cleft, the passage drops in narrow steps between carved walls to a ledge above a long hall of black water. By the open shaft, a rope goes down into the dark straight over the middle of the pool. By the overflow channel, a party crawls on its belly through the palm roots and wet stone and comes out at the water's surface under the south ledge, which is the worst way in and the only one the Bödeküd uses.

The hall is cold enough to show breath. The drawing-basin cut into the north ledge holds clean water. The chains hang down through the crack in the roof above the pool. On the ledge, beside a bar set in a slot and a counterweight on a chain, is the stone shutter of the overflow, raised. The carvings on the ledge face show the figures with jars pouring water back into the pool, and the long shape beneath each with its arms spread. Voghalyemök's rope, if it was not found above, lies coiled beside the drawing-basin.

Under the south ledge, half in the water, is an air pocket. In it, tied to stones with good rope in a warden's hitch, are the things the Bödeküd keeps: the camel, still breathing; Budaqtoliz, if he was taken, cold and alive; and below the water, weighted with a stone, the warden.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **The Bödeküd** hangs beneath the ledges with its arms spread over the pool. Its body is never fully seen: a pale bulk under the stone, wet, larger than a camel, from which the arms come. It does not hurry. It takes one person at a time, draws him under and holds him there, and lets go of a held thing only to take another. It leaves alone anyone who has poured water from the pool back into it before drawing, while that person stays at the pool. Dry heat makes it go still, and fire on its arms makes it pull them under. It does not climb the passage into the dry air and does not follow anyone up the shaft into daylight.
- **Numaqlun**, if she came, goes to the air pocket for her husband and cannot be stopped from it.

**Player choices.**

- _Cleverness with the place:_ trap it below. With the Bödeküd lured into the chamber, drop the shutter across the overflow mouth by knocking out the bar; the counterweight drags it home. It is now shut into its cistern with the lid above it. The bar is on the ledge within reach of its arms, so whoever knocks it out must be protected, or must have poured water back first.
- _Force:_ collapse its ledge. The crack that broke the lid ran through the south ledge too, and a party with levers, or with the chains from above hauled by beasts, can bring the ledge down on the arms beneath it. That ruins the south half of the pool with silt for a season and leaves the drawing-basin clean.
- _Force:_ kill it. Fire and dry heat are what it cannot bear. Its blood turns the pool bitter, and a party that kills it in the pool has no water to drink until the spring clears; a party that first fills every skin from the drawing-basin, or draws the creature into the overflow channel at noon when the hot wind blows down it, keeps the water.
- _Parley, in its own fashion:_ pour water back into the pool, each person in turn, as the carvings show, and walk the ledge to the air pocket. The Bödeküd lets them pass while they stay at the pool. It does not let them take what it keeps without a trade: a beast or a weight given into the water for each one carried out.
- _Stealth:_ go in by the passage at noon, when the dry wind of the overflow channel has driven it deep, and reach the air pocket while it lies still.
- _Giving something up:_ leave something it will keep in place of each life the party takes back. Numaqlun offers herself in place of Budaqtoliz, and means it.

**Clues and rewards.** Everything above becomes plain: the winding was the warden's rope reeled up and let fall, the coils and the knots were the warden's habits learned and repeated, the chains were hauled through the crack and the tether was reeled in like a bucket. The carvings show that the builders knew it, and kept the courtesy, and drew. They do not show where it came from.

**Consequences.** Trapped below, the Bödeküd lives, the overflow is shut and the palms will die within a few years unless the tribe opens it again. Killed, it leaves the spring bitter for a season and the tribe without its reserve for a dry year. Kept by the courtesy, it lives and the spring stays as it was, now with a tribe that knows what it owns. Budaqtoliz, if he was taken, lives if the party reaches him before the second night.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that comes down unprepared is not killed at once: the Bödeküd takes one of them, draws him under and holds him, and lets go when the held one is cut free of the stone or when another weight is given. Survivors can escape up the passage, which it will not climb, or up the shaft into the light at noon, or out through the overflow channel when the hot wind blows down it. A party that flees the chamber still has the dry shelf in Scene Five, which opens off the passage above the reach of its arms. If the party never comes down at all, the tribe's riders arrive at dawn on the fourth day (see Scene Five), and the tribe opens the cistern itself, with the party standing as witness or as debtor.

## Scene Five: The Dry Shelf

**Purpose.** The treasure, the reckoning with the owners, and a way out.

**Situation.** Halfway down the passage, behind a slab that sits in a groove and slides aside, a dry shelf is cut into the rock above the highest water the spring has ever reached. It holds the Küüyöyür' reserve for a ruinous year: silver in sealed jars, strings of turquoise, and two fine swords wrapped in oiled cloth, the price the tribe was paid to end a feud four generations ago. Beside them hangs a cord of passage tokens of the tribe. Numaqlun knows where the shelf is; Budaqtoliz does not.

At dawn on the fourth day, if not before, a dozen riders of the Küüyöyür come down out of the gravel and spread round the palms so that every road is covered. Their eldest sits at the edge of the water and waits to be offered tea.

**NPCs and threats.**

- **The Küüyöyür riders**, who ask what happened and compare it with what Numaqlun says. They are not in a hurry.
- **Numaqlun** speaks for the party or against it, according to what it did.

**Player choices.**

- _Parley:_ give an account and accept what the tribe offers: a share of the reserve, water for every skin, a passage token and a rider to escort the party to the road.
- _Force:_ take the shelf and go before the riders come. A party with the reserve and no guide has three days of open sand ahead of it, and the tribe sends riders after it to the next three wells.
- _Stealth:_ take a little and say nothing. The tribe counts its jars.
- _Cleverness with the place:_ trade knowledge for silver. The tribe needs to know whether the shutter is down, whether the courtesy holds and how to open the overflow without letting the thing out, and it pays for a full account.
- _Giving something up:_ refuse payment and ask only for water, a token and a rider. The tribe holds that a debt of this size is the tribe's, not the party's, and a party that refuses payment has made itself a friend of the Küüyöyür for a generation.

**Clues and rewards.** The share is a jar of silver for the party and one of the two swords, whichever the eldest chooses, or both swords if the party brought Budaqtoliz up alive. Every skin is filled. The passage token admits the party to any Küüyöyür well, and their escort, a rider named by the eldest, takes them by the shortest way to the road, where the caravan has waited two days at the next well.

**Consequences.** The tribe judges the party by the customs of the owned oasis: guests who kept guest-right are paid; strangers who drew water without leave owe a skin's price for every skin, set against what they did in the cistern; anyone who fought the owners pays blood or work. A party that freed Budaqtoliz is owed by the boy's household for life.

**If bypassed, failed or cut short.** A party that never found the shelf is paid from it anyway if it served the tribe: Numaqlun goes down for the jars herself while the eldest pours the tea. A party whose riders both died takes its account to the dozen at dawn without a witness; the riders find the warden's tally-cord in the party's hands and believe it, if the party has not taken from the shelf. A party that left with nothing has still found water, and Görenghakhiq walks it to the road by the stars.

## Aftermath

- **If the shutter is down.** The Bödeküd is shut in its cistern. The Küüyöyür lose the reserve while they decide whether to open the overflow and keep the courtesy, or let the palms die and dig a new well forty paces off.
- **If the Bödeküd is dead.** The spring runs bitter until the next season, and the tribe drinks elsewhere in a dry year. The body, if anyone raises it, is too large to have come down the shaft.
- **If the courtesy was kept.** The Küüyöyür pour the first draw back again, and the Bödeküd keeps its side for as long as they do. The tribe adds a cut to the oldest palm, beside the builders' figure with the jar.
- **If the party robbed the shelf.** The tribe has a feud with the party, and every owned water of the Khoshiqor hears of it by the next season.
- **Whatever happened.** At the next lidded well on the road, Görenghakhiq stops the string short of it and listens for a while before he lets the camels drink.
