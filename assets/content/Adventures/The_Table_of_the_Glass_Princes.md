A prince bargained with something under the sand for his house's survival, and it kept the bargain. The house is still at table, unchanged, and every guest who sits down is a payment. The party is asked to dine.

## Overview

The palace of the cadet house of [Nimeshun]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}, [[place-relvanninel|Relvanninesh]], is a ruin of fused glass that has not weathered in ninety years. Twelve people live in it, unaged and glass-clear, bound to a long table and a law: the house has never turned away a guest and cannot. The [Tenant]{.name data-type="being/creature"} beneath the palace, a seam of black glass that is a piece of [[lore-theshadow|the Shadow]], takes years from every guest who shares the house's bread. The adventure is a banquet with a prince who wants to die and a thing that does not want him to.

The adventure runs over a day and two nights. The party arrives in the glare of one afternoon, dines that evening, sleeps in the east wing and goes down to the cistern in the small hours or the next noon. Because the Tenant draws years by the hour, time is the resource the scenes spend. The bypass notes carry play on when the party skips or shortens a scene.

## Hooks

- **The thief.** A glass-seller in [[place-ashkarad|Ashkarad]], a thin man with a lens-grinder's squint and a scar across the thumb, pays well for a lens from the palace, the clear sort that the house made in its last years. He keeps one on his counter that shows the street upside down and nothing at all of the person looking. He does not mention that two thieves went for one and came back old, though a boy sweeping his shop floor will say it for a coin.
- **The mercenary or common soldier.** A [Teghumod]{.name data-type="affiliation/polity"} factor needs a party to escort a debtor across the edge of the belt, by a road that passes within sight of the hill. The debtor is a man the factor would be glad to see the hill take, and the factor is careful never to say so in a sentence that could be repeated. The pay is good, and the road is the shortest, and the factor has marked it on the map with a thumbnail.
- **The leader.** A woman of the Ashkarad craft quarter, the great-granddaughter of the house's lamp-maker, asks a captain to bring her great-grandmother's name back from the palace. She keeps a silvered mirror on her wall that shows nothing of her room, and she cannot sleep in front of it. She does not say that the lamp-maker is still alive.
- **The lone wanderer.** Water gone, mount dead, a glittering hill on the horizon. The wanderer reaches the gate more dead than alive and finds the long glass road swept clean of drift, as though someone has just left it.

## Scene One: The Gate of Glass

**Purpose.** Introduce the law of the guest and put the party under it.

**Situation.** The glass road ends at a gate of clear green glass, thick as a man's arm, carved in the form of two cupped hands. It stands open. The sun strikes the hill from every facet at once and the heat comes up off the road in a shimmer that hurts the eyes; the rods of glass along the track hum faintly in the wind like the rims of wet cups. Beyond the gate is a courtyard of black glass, as smooth as a frozen lake, where a figure waits in a steward's robe holding a bowl of water and a folded cloth. This is the steward of the house, and he has been waiting for ninety years. His sandals leave no mark on the floor.

**NPCs and threats.** The steward is courteous, grave and slightly sad. He offers water to every guest. A guest who refuses the bowl is not turned away, but the gate behind them is shut, and the cloth of the steward's hand is wet. He speaks the old form of greeting with a precision that no living member of the [[lore-khazrynclt|Tellumi]] manages, and he never raises his voice or lowers his eyes. He is not dangerous. The glass itself is: the courtyard floor is polished so smooth that in the noon glare a careless step on the slope breaks an ankle, and a man who falls on it sees a dozen of his own faces, all of them looking up from below. The wind off the hill sandblasts bare skin at midday.

**Player choices.** Force: push past the steward and enter armed; the gate closes and the steward does not resist. He steps aside and holds the bowl out to the next person, and the effect on a party is worse than a fight. Stealth: scale the wall and enter by a roof-light, where the glass is sharp as knapped flint and the climb costs skin. Guile: wash the hands and accept nothing else, using the letter of the guest-law against the house; the steward notes it without displeasure. Parley: speak to the steward in the old form of greeting and ask him the house's custom, which he will recite gladly and at length, as a man does who has been given nobody to tell it to.

**Clues and rewards.** The steward says the house has never refused a guest and that this is its glory. He says the table is laid. He says the prince is eager for news. If pressed, he says he is tired. A guest who watches his hands sees that the cloth never dries, and that the bowl is always full no matter how many wash in it. A party that thinks to ask him what became of the last guests gets a pause, and then the answer that they were very welcome.

**Consequences.** A party that accepts the water is under guest-right and cannot be harmed by the house while it stays, nor can it harm the house. The protection extends to the Tenant, which uses the rule as a net. A party that refuses the water is not under the law and is not under protection, and the house will treat it as a thing the sand is still making up its mind about.

**If bypassed.** A party that enters by the roof meets the steward in the corridor below, bowl in hand, where he asks them politely to wash. He has been waiting at that spot too.

## Scene Two: The Table

**Purpose.** Roleplay a banquet with the dead, and let the party learn the bargain.

**Situation.** The great hall is a vault of clear glass lit by a ceiling that gathers every beam of light and throws it down in white shafts on the long table. The table runs the length of the hall, laid with plates of green glass and a feast that does not cool: roast birds, flat bread, dates in honey, a wine so clear it seems to be water. The smell is of hot bread and nothing else. At its head sits [Garmelul]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a tall man of fifty with a glassy complexion and a voice like a struck bell. The household turns to the party as one, with the soft sound of twelve chairs. They ask for news: of the [[affiliation-tribestrzd|Atarzád]], of the caravans, of Ashkarad, of the price of glass. Each of them asks one question and listens to the answer with a fixed, hungry attention.

**NPCs and threats.** Garmelul is gracious and sad. He cannot say what the bargain is, because it is part of the bargain. He drinks nothing and watches the party's cups. His daughter [Yelhule]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a girl of seventeen whose skin has the gloss of a held breath, can say it. She whispers it to the party in a moment when the others are looking away, in a voice so small that a spoon dropped on the plate hides it. His brother is cruel in the way of a man who has sat still for ninety years: he asks pointed questions, laughs when the party hesitates and stares at the party's throats. The physician checks the party's pulses with her cold fingers and nods at what she finds. The lamp-maker, an old woman at the foot of the table who cannot be old, keeps her hands folded and says nothing, and watches the door as though someone she knows might come through it.

**Player choices.** Force: attack the table; the glass cuts the party and the household does not resist, which is worse. A plate shattered across a palm bleeds into the feast and the feast does not stain. Stealth: eat nothing and slip away at midnight, leaving a place laid for the one who goes. Guile: eat, drink and bargain for the lens, the mirror or the story; the household trades gladly and takes a year for each item. Parley: answer the household's questions and learn what they want, which is for someone to say no. A party that tells them plainly how the city fared under the Atarzád is paid in a silence that goes on too long.

**Clues and rewards.** Yelhule's whisper: _the bread is the price_. Every guest who eats at the table gives the house a year, and the Tenant takes what the house cannot use. A guest who stays three nights is a guest forever. Garmelul gives the party the first clue to the cistern: _go below, if you have the stomach, and see what we are fed to_. The physician, asked privately, will tell a party how many years each of them has already given, to the month.

**Consequences.** The meal counts. Each person at the table gives a year. If the party eats freely, they are older when they rise, and the steward carries a mirror to each guest at the end of the meal without being asked. A party that sits and eats nothing is noticed, and the household's smiles do not change.

**If bypassed.** A party that skips the banquet and goes directly to the cistern meets Yelhule on the stair. She tells them the same thing in a rush, with the steward's footsteps coming behind her.

## Scene Three: The Bedchambers

**Purpose.** Spring the trap, with the price of rest.

**Situation.** At nightfall the party is shown to guest chambers in the east wing: rooms of clear glass with beds of woven glass thread. The glass walls show the room next door and the room beyond, as in a series of reflections that does not end, and in every one of them is a bed and a sleeper who is the same as the one who lies in it. The room is warm and silent. A sleeping guest dreams of the table. A waking guest sees the household in the reflections, standing in a ring around the bed, their mouths moving over a sentence nobody can hear.

**NPCs and threats.** The household does not move. The walls do. At the dead of night the doors seal with a sound like a held note. The glass hums, and the sleepers begin to age; the loss of years can be watched in the reflections as a slow greying of hair and thinning of cheek. The hum climbs until it sets teeth on edge. Yelhule is the one who can open the doors from outside, and she is afraid to; her father watches her from the end of the corridor, and what she does he will not forgive and cannot punish.

**Player choices.** Force: break the glass with a blade, a hammer or a thrown stool; it shatters, the hum stops and the Tenant knows, since every shard is a mouth of the same vein. Stealth: stay awake and wait for the dawn, sitting in the dark with the lamp hooded; the hum is loudest in the first sleeping hour. Guile: sleep in shifts, so that no one loses more than an hour, and set the waking watch with their backs to the walls and their eyes shut. Parley: call Yelhule to the door and persuade her to open it, with the plain argument that she has been waiting ninety years for somebody to try.

**Clues and rewards.** The reflections show the history of the house. Every night for ninety years, a guest has slept here. Some nine hundred have gone to the sand. The reflections show their faces: a carter, two Teghumod factors, a child of eight who is not in any ledger, a woman with a shaved head and the mark of a Shekhul. Yelhule gives the party a key of green glass, the key to the cistern, which she has worn on a cord against her skin for as long as she can remember.

**Consequences.** A party that sleeps through the night loses years, and the loss shows at breakfast in hands that shake a little more and in a cough that stays. A party that breaks the glass wakes the Tenant, which turns its attention to the party's sleepers by name for the rest of the visit. A party that frees Yelhule has made her an ally and a target.

**If bypassed.** A party that stays up and in the hall is called to the cistern by the household when the doors seal. The twelve file out together and the steward takes a lamp, and the invitation is not a request.

## Scene Four: The Tenant

**Purpose.** The confrontation with the thing under the house.

**Situation.** The cistern is a round chamber forty feet across, cut into the rock, and its far wall is a vein of black glass that runs up into the dark. The air is cold and tastes of struck flint. Garmelul stands before it with his back to the party, and his reflection in the glass is older than he is. The vein shows the party's reflections, older. A voice comes out of it with a sound like glass being drawn across glass. It offers the party what it has offered the house: the bargain, the long table, the quiet.

**NPCs and threats.** The Tenant does not move. It speaks in the voice of whichever speaker has the greatest wish: a lost mother, a dead partner, a captain who died badly. It cannot be struck, because the blow comes back. It can reach the party's years by the reflections, and it draws a little with every word, so that a long parley costs gray hair. Garmelul is in its hand. If the party attacks, he defends it, since the bargain he made is the only thing keeping his line alive. He is brave about it, which makes it harder, and he weeps without sound as he lifts his sword.

**Player choices.** Force: shatter the vein with an iron maul in full sunlight, which the house's glass will bring down the stair in a shaft; this needs the noon hour and an open roof-light, and a party that waits for noon is paying the Tenant for the wait. Stealth: leave Garmelul at the vein and go; the Tenant lets the party go and adds it to the debt. Guile: refuse the bargain and the Tenant in the same breath, in the form the Tellumi use at a funeral, which is a reading of the dead and not an argument. Parley: take Garmelul's place at the table, or bring a Shekhul to read the house's names in the cistern and give the line the rite it was refused.

**Clues and rewards.** The vein cracks at a strike in sunlight, with a note that rings through the hill for an hour. The household crumbles to dust, finally received. A reading of the names takes an hour and breaks the Tenant's hold without the shattering. A bargain with the Tenant is possible and costs a great deal: it asks the party's years to the day and takes the first ones at the table.

**Consequences.** If the vein breaks, the palace goes dark and begins to weather, and the party has a night's walk out before the stair falls in. If it holds, the party leaves older and the household is as it was. If the names are read, Garmelul stands for the last time with his daughter beside him and does not look at the vein.

**If bypassed.** A party that does not reach the cistern is called back to the table by the household and asked to stay, in the most hospitable terms. The Tenant makes the same offer in the dreams of the party that goes home.

## Scene Five: The Ledgers

**Purpose.** Pay the reward and open the larger question.

**Situation.** Under the cistern, in a dry vault, the house's ledgers are shelved in glass cases. They list every guest for ninety years, by name, day and years taken, in the hands of five different stewards. The vault smells of old paper and cold. They also list silver sent, each year at midwinter, to a house in [[place-amradadrgn|Amradad]], entered in the same level script as the guests.

**NPCs and threats.** If the vein is broken, the household dissolves in the party's hands, and Yelhule dies last, looking at her own fingers. If it is not, Garmelul is waiting, courteous, with the steward behind him and the bowl in his hand. A Teghumod patrol at the foot of the hill, drawn by the light, is the one outside danger; its captain wants the glass and nothing else, and will take it from a party that is slow.

**Player choices.** Force: carry off the cases, and the lenses with them, before the patrol closes the road. Stealth: copy the entries, leaving the ledgers where they lie, so that the exile house does not know they have been read. Guile: use the ledger to blackmail the exile house, which pays well for silence and holds a grudge. Parley: take the account to the [Council of the Princes]{.name data-type="affiliation/governmental"} openly and let the seven houses decide what to do with a kinsman.

**Clues and rewards.** The house's last lenses, clear as water, are worth a great deal to a glass-seller: a few weights of silver each. The mirror in the vault shows nothing of the room. The ledgers show that the silver sent to Amradad went to the [House of Ashkarad]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}, whose head, [Samim]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, is a kinsman. Whether he knew where it came from is the party's to decide. The most recent entry is a man who came in the spring and is still in the house: the line for the years taken stands blank, and the steward has left it open.

**Consequences.** If the house is ended, the exile house's income falls by the share that came from the palace, and Samim learns who did it. If it is not, the silver continues, and every guest who comes is a coin. A party that is seen carrying the ledgers by the Teghumod patrol is a party the Teghumod will remember.

**If bypassed.** A party that leaves without the ledgers learns of them from the glass-seller within the year, who knows by now what was in the vault and which house stands to profit.

## Aftermath

- **If the Tenant is broken.** The palace weathers in a season. The household is received at last. The Teghumod pull down the ruin for its glass, and the lenses that come out of it show the street the right way up. The craft quarter of Ashkarad lights lamps for the lamp-maker, who dies in the party's presence with her name spoken, and the great-granddaughter takes the mirror down from her wall.
- **If the names are read.** The house is released with its rite and the Tenant goes quiet. The palace stands, empty and ringing, and the edge-tribes stop burying their animals.
- **If the party takes the bargain.** The table has new seats, and the guests keep coming. The party's names enter the ledger, and the silver at midwinter goes to Amradad in a hand that is not the steward's.
- **If the party fails.** The party is three or four years older, and the house has new guests at its table. The glass-seller's lens shows nothing of the party's faces, and the party does not ask why.
