The [[affiliation-osketguides|Ösket]] hold the western and central crossings of [[place-graznmntns|the Grazian wall]] by descent, and the rest of what they are follows from that. A hearth is a house, a herd, a high pasture and one road. The road is what gives it standing. Nothing above the hearth binds it, and no body exists that could.

## The Road Is the Property

Herds are wealth and a crossing is standing, and the two are not traded for one another. A hearth that holds [[place-suryadvara|Sūryadvāra]] is rich and lends at interest to caravan-masters who have misjudged the season. A hearth at [[place-tsokhar|Tsökhar]] takes two caravans in a good season and lives off its yaks and its horses. Both are heard at the cols on the same terms, because both hold a road.

A hearth with beasts and no road is fed and is not consulted. The Ösket state this without apology and see nothing in it to explain.

Selling a road is the one capital offense. The man who does it is **Roadless**: out of every hearth, fed by none of them, and taken over the wall by nobody. A people who feed any stranger who reaches a door have made exactly that one exception. What the Ösket say about the two sales of [[place-gudesroad|the Guides' Road]] they say among themselves, and everyone else has the rumor.

## The Guide-mother

The knowledge of a road is a woman's and the walking of it is a man's. A guide-mother holds the weather-signs, the years a shelf has moved, the loads a yak-route will carry, and the count of the dead and where each of them stopped. She says whether the road is open on a given morning, and her word ends the question. The distinction is not ceremonial, and no lowlander has ever successfully bought round it.

A daughter who will hold a road is taught by the woman who holds it now. A son is taught the ground. Neither instruction is written down anywhere.

## The Door

Anyone who reaches an Ösket door is fed and housed, without payment and without exception, and the Roadless are the only people the rule leaves out. The obligation falls on the hearth-warden who keeps the winter stores, and it holds hardest through the closed months, when the store is the whole of a village's economy.

It is the one Ösket custom lowlanders find entirely legible, and the one they most often abuse. The Ösket treat the abuse as the price of the custom and have never proposed a remedy for it.

## Outside the Order

The [[lore-vedyariclt|Vedyari]] order of stations stops at the last [[place-pssshrines|Pass-Shrine]]. A shrine-ushtaka, priest of the summit temple, will eat in an Ösket house on the col and would not do so in the valley, and no court has once been asked to settle what that means.

The Ösket carry no wrist-line and read nobody else's. A traveler's station is not asked for on the wall, and a wrapped wrist means nothing there at all.

## What Is Said, and What Is Not

An Ösket tells an outsider what to do and not why. This is not mystification. It is what a people does when the alternative is arguing at nineteen thousand feet with a man who does not know what he is looking at.

The tongue makes the habit cheap to keep. [[skill-osketlng|Ösket]] marks on every finite verb whether the speaker saw a thing, was told it, or infers it, so a guide cannot report that the pass is open without saying how he knows. The road-pidgin the lowlanders learn is perhaps two hundred words and carries no such mark. The Ösket teach the pidgin willingly and the language not at all, and the misunderstandings that follow are held to be the lowlanders' own.

One subject is closed. The lineages will discuss the weather, the road, the fee and the dead, and every Ösket crossing of the Guides' Road walks through the opening in [[place-shunydvara|the Empty Door]], single file, hooded lowlanders and all. On that they stop talking.

## The Cord

There is no script. A hearth's route-record is a knotted cord—road, stage, load, day, and the dead—tied by its cord-keeper and read by nobody outside the blood. Cord-keeping goes to one person in a generation, and a cord whose keeper dies untimely is not read again by anyone.

A few of the Sūryadvāra hearths read and write lowland Vedyari for their contracts. None of them writes Ösket in it.

## Names

A personal name is one word, most often two syllables, taken from weather, ground, beast or condition, and it is not inherited. What is inherited is the crossing. A stranger who must be told whom he is dealing with is told the road first and the man second.

The names carry two front rounded vowels no lowland tongue has, and a Vedyari who substitutes the unrounded vowel for either is understood and identified in the same breath. The language behind them is related to nothing spoken in any lowland. A Vedyari grammarian spent four seasons at [[place-oskhelt|Öskhelt]] two centuries ago and published a careful, competent account whose conclusion was that there was nothing to connect it to.

## What Is Good, and What Succeeds

**Good is the road kept: the morning's word obeyed, the party taken over and brought down, the door opened to whoever reaches it. Evil is the road sold, the party lost to carelessness, and the door shut on a stranger.** An Ösket weighs a deed by the road: it is good if the party comes down, and reasons are not asked for. A guide who took a caravan over a shelf the guide-mother had refused has put his word above hers, and her word is the one that ends the question.

Success is a road held by descent and a hearth consulted at the cols. Standing comes from the road, not the herd, and the two are not traded for one another: a hearth with beasts and no road is fed and is not consulted. A lineage of Sūryadvāra is rich and lends at interest, a lineage of Meghadvāra lives off its yaks, and both are heard on the same terms, because both hold a road. Failure is a hearth with beasts and no road, a Fosterling who holds no share, and at the bottom the **Roadless**, who are out of every hearth, fed by none and taken over the wall by nobody.

The Ösket value most the road, the guide-mother's word, the door and the count of the dead. They value least explanation, paper and rank that has to be asked for. They carry no wrist-line and read nobody else's, they tell an outsider what to do and not why, and the lowland habit of arguing a refusal on the col does not strike them as manners.

## The Man Admired

**Ürtal**, road-holder of [[place-meghadvara|Meghadvāra]], is the man the western lineages offer as the answer to the question of what a road-holder is. His lineage is among the poorest on the wall, holding the road with the shortest window and a share of two caravans in a good season. When the snow-watcher read the summit at first light and the guide-mother of his hearth said the road was shut, he held a caravan at the hearth, in the teeth of the caravan-master, the master's creditors and the season. The storm took the col that week. The caravan-master wrote the lost month off to Ösket obstinacy, and Ürtal did not explain it, as no Ösket explains a morning's refusal.

The Ösket admire in him what the division of the road asks of a man. The knowledge is a woman's and the walking is a man's, and he walks what she opens and stays where she closes. A road-holder answers for every party he takes over, and Ürtal's lineage has lost none it was told to hold. His cost is the cost of the silence. A man whose whole standing is obedience to a morning's word is blamed in lowland counting-houses for every month the word costs, and he carries the blame to the col and says nothing.

## The Woman Admired

**Yölte** of [[place-suryadvara|Sūryadvāra]], who meets the climbers at [[place-oskhelt|Öskhelt]], is the woman the Ösket name when they say what the door is. She is broad-faced and wears a sheepskin coat, with a guide-cord of knotted hide looped twice around her wrist. She gives the road first and herself second. She looks at a stranger's boots, then his hands, then the sky behind him, in that order, and opens the nearest door, steps aside and points him to the hearth and the steaming pot. She does not ask his business, his name or whether he can pay.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of Ürtal's. He keeps the road against those who would hurry it, and she keeps the rule the road stands on, that whoever reaches a door is fed and housed, with no exception but the Roadless. The cord on her wrist marks a woman who has walked a road, and the three things she looks at are the three a guide reads before taking anyone anywhere. Her cost is the abuse. The door is the one Ösket custom lowlanders find entirely legible and the one they most often take advantage of, the hearth treats the abuse as the price of the custom, and the woman at the door bears it first.

## What the Hearths Keep

An Ösket can point to the things that hold a lineage in place. **The [[miscgear-guidecord|guide-cord]]** is a knotted hide cord recording a road: its stages, loads, days, water and the places the ice moves, and the dead and where each stopped. A cord is read by its keeper and by no one outside the blood. **The common store** at Öskhelt is held by the hearth-wardens and drawn on by everyone, provisioned out of the fees of the Sūryadvāra lineages. **The door** is not an object, and it is the Ösket's oldest rule: whoever reaches it is fed and housed. **The opening of [[place-shunydvara|Shūnyadvāra]]** on the col of [[place-gudesroad|the Guides' Road]] is the one thing every Ösket crossing walks through, single file, hooded lowlanders and all, and the one subject on which the lineages stop talking. **The slab of [[place-himashila|Himashilā]]** at the head of the outflow of Chandraprabhava is the mountain's own; the Ösket step round it without touching. The [[place-pssshrines|Pass-Shrines]]' summit blessing is not the Ösket's, and the Ösket honor it on the five blessed roads.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is reading the summit. At first light the snow-watcher looks at the peak and not at the sky, and what the cloud on the summit is doing settles whether a caravan moves that day. [[place-meghashkhr|Meghashikhara]] is read before a caravan on the western road moves; the reading is given to the caravan-master as an instruction and never as an explanation. The guide-mother says whether the road is open, and her word ends the question. On a storm day no fire is lit in the open and the mules are hobbled close under rock. A stranger at the door is fed. A hearth-warden checks the store. A cord-keeper ties what the day has added. The Ösket hold the whole range the dwelling-place of forces they call by names they do not share with outsiders, and the practice they show a stranger is the practice he can see.

## The Round of the Year

The year is told by the passes.

- **The two months of the market** fall in the open season, when Öskhelt fills with caravan-masters, loads are broken down onto yaks, fee-reckoners sit in the open and the smiths work three deep. It is for settling what each road will carry.
- **The meeting at the cols** is called when a question touches two roads. The Speaker of the Cols convenes it, the lineages concerned argue, and they go home. It is for settling a question between lineages without a larger body to appeal to.
- **The closing of the open months** shuts the doors of the passes and turns Öskhelt back into a village living on its common store, with the smiths shoeing beasts again. It is for counting what the season took, and the cord-keeper ties the season's dead into the cord.

## Favor and the Roadless

Reward is the road and what the road brings. A Carrier who learns the ground a season at a time becomes a Road-holder entitled to take a caravan over his own crossing. A Herdholder who holds beasts and a stake in a high pasture answers for both at the hearth. A hearth-kin is entitled to the shelter of the hearth, its herds and a share of a season's fee. The guide-mother's word ends a morning's question. A hearth with a road is consulted at the cols.

Penalty runs from silence to the wall. A hearth with beasts and no road is fed and not consulted. The fee-reckoner keeps what lineages owe each other, and an unpaid debt between lineages is heard at a col. A caravan unblessed by the summit shrine finds no Ösket willing to take it down. Selling a road is the one capital offense. The man who does it is **Roadless**: out of every hearth, fed by none of them and taken over the wall by nobody. A party lost through carelessness earns the same sentence.

## The Guide-mother and the Cols

An Ösket lineage is a hearth, its herds, its high pasture and one road, and nothing above it binds it. There is no appeal from a lineage to a larger body, because there is no larger body. Where a question touches two roads, the lineages concerned meet at a col and argue it out, and the Speaker of the Cols who convenes them holds nothing afterward. The Pass-Shrines hold the summit rite and the crossing-register, and a caravan they refuse to bless finds no Ösket willing to take it down. Past that, the hearth is the whole of the authority there is.

Inside the lineage the guide-mother holds the knowledge of the road and says whether it is open, the road-holder answers for every party, and the cord-keeper, the snow-watcher, the hearth-warden and the fee-reckoner hold the rest. Cases are heard by the lineages concerned at the col, records are the cord-keeper's and are read by nobody outside the blood, and the fee is the fee-reckoner's to set and collect. On Sūryadvāra, where the kingdom of [[affiliation-vindhyalay|Shikharālaya]] holds the throat and its clan-wardens hold the warden-right by inheritance, the answer divides: the warden names the fee, the lineage names the season, and the party pays one and waits for the other. A warden who overrules a lineage on a question of weather does it exactly once. Above the last Pass-Shrine the Vedyari order does not reach. The Ösket are not Outcastes, which is a place within the order. The order does not address them at all.

## What the Cord Asks of a Tuesday

The Ösket do not say where the dead go. They say where the dead stopped. The cord carries the road, the stage, the load, the day and the dead, and the guide-mother holds the count of the dead and where each of them stopped. The belief that settles a Tuesday is that a road is made of those who stopped on it.

So a stage is not a campsite. A guide-mother who sends a party to a shelf where the cord counts the dead knows what she is sending them past, and a daughter learns the count with the weather-signs and the years a shelf has moved. The names of the dead are kept on the cistern wall of a Pass-Shrine as well, where the ushtakas count them by name. A cord-keeper is taught the dead as part of the road, and a cord whose keeper dies untimely is lost with its dead. A lineage that loses the reading of a cord walks the road again before it takes a party over it, and does not hurry the walking.

The Ösket spend on the road: the common store, the hearth-warden's winter provisioning, the yaks and horses, the cord.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead by walking the road. The cord names the shelf where each of them stopped, and a guide passing it passes them. The Pass-Shrine's count by name on the cistern wall is the second form, and it is the ushtakas' and not the Ösket's own. The Ösket make no claim that the dead answer. A lineage that has lost a cord-keeper has lost the reading of its cord, and the knowledge has to be walked again.

The cost is the keeper. Cord-keeping goes to one person in a generation, and when that person dies untimely the cord and its dead go unread.

## What a Person Owes

Ask an Ösket what a person owes, and the answer comes in order: the road first, because it is what the hearth is; then the party taken over it; then the guide-mother's word on the morning; then the door; then the fee, the half of what lands at the far end. A person owes the lineage the account of the dead on his stages.

In return a person is owed what the hearth gives. A hearth-kin is owed the shelter of the hearth, its herds and a share of a season's fee. A Carrier is owed teaching, a Road-holder is owed his crossing, and every guest is owed the door.

## The Weak

The door is the first resort and the common store the second. Anyone who reaches an Ösket door is fed and housed, without payment and without exception, and the Roadless are the only people the rule leaves out. The obligation falls on the hearth-warden who keeps the winter stores, and it holds hardest through the closed months. A **Fosterling** is taken into a hearth without being of its blood, fed and taught and holding no share. A widow, an orphan or an aged relation is hearth-kin or a Fosterling, and is fed either way.

Scarcity is met by the store and by the herds. At Öskhelt the Sūryadvāra lineages provision the common store out of their own fees. The poor western lineages live off their yaks and horses and take the share of two caravans, and the herd is what outlasts a bad year. At [[place-tsokhar|Tsökhar]], where the ground carries a herd through a winter, the village lives as it did before there was any trade at all.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot walk a road is kept by the hearth, and his work changes. The guide-mother's knowledge is held in a seat and not walked. The snow-watcher reads the summit at first light from the ground, the cord-keeper ties knots, and the fee-reckoner sits in the open with a caravan-master. A hearth-kin entitled to a share of the fee keeps it. A guide too old for the road who asks for one last crossing is told no by his own lineage, and the reason is not given to the party he would have taken.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the door and, past the door, the hearth. Teaching goes by the road: a daughter who will hold a road is taught by the woman who holds it, a son is taught the ground, and the cord is taught to one person in a generation. Neither instruction is written down anywhere. Defense is the road itself. Nothing goes over the western and central crossings without an Ösket guide, and the lineage that holds the road decides who goes over it. At Tsökhar a watch is kept on the approaches through the open months, which no other village on the wall does, and the Ösket describe it as a precaution against weather.

## The Line at Every Age

The hearth is the unit and the road its spine. A child is hearth-kin by blood or a Fosterling by the hearth's decision, and is fed either way. A boy is taught the ground, holds beasts and a stake in a high pasture as a Herdholder, and walks the roads under another's word as a Carrier, learning a season at a time. A girl who will hold a road is taught by the woman who holds it. A man becomes a Road-holder when he is entitled to take a caravan over his own crossing. A woman becomes a guide-mother when she is the senior woman of her lineage. An old person keeps the knowledge, or the cord, or the store, or the summit watch. The dying are attended at the hearth, and the dead are counted into the cord with the place where each stopped.

Membership is blood or the hearth's decision. A hearth holds a crossing by descent, and a name is a single word, most often of two syllables, taken from weather, ground, beast or condition and is not inherited. The crossing is the inheritance. A stranger who must be told whom he is dealing with is told the road first and the man second. The unit is the hearth, which is wider than the pair and their children, and the extended family is the road's lineage.

## What the Ösket Do Not Say

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Ösket's is the silence. The Ösket tell an outsider what to do and not why, and the misunderstandings that follow are held to be the lowlanders' own. Thoughtful Ösket dispute whether the habit protects the road or protects the lineages from having to answer for it to anyone, and the door's abuse, the capital offense and the closed subject at Shūnyadvāra all rest on the same refusal.

The sin is the sale of the road. The Ösket remember in detail the two occasions on which a lineage sold the Guides' Road, and say what they say about them among themselves. A lowlander who learned the road would be worth killing, and an Ösket who sold it is worth more, and the Ösket deal with the seller themselves and have never explained how. The sixth road carries passage for people and packets that cannot be seen to cross, paid in advance and in gold, and the Pass-Shrines' refusal to bless it is older than any of the four reasons given for it. The lineages of the western passes do not discuss the lowlander at all.
