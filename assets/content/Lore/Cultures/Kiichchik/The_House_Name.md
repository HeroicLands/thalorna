**The House Name**, _k'elal kil sh'ulum_, is a word-dance of [[place-kultaqan|Kul'taq'an]], the city of [[affiliation-ixilankul|Ish'ilankul]] where the central dispatch of the Speaking Road stands. Two dancers perform it in the great plaza on the first day of Shok'alal, the war month, when the harvest is in and the campaigning season opens, to a slit drum and two shell trumpets. They wear the armor of feathers and leather that a warrior of the plateau wears in procession, and they circle each other, and each of them stops to speak and turns away when he has spoken. The dancer of the captive is a young noble who has not yet been on a campaign. The dance teaches him how a captive speaks.

It is the speech of a war-leader of Kul'taq'an and of the noble he took in a _ch'utik'at_, a captive-hunt, from the independent city of [[place-qokulqat|Q'o Kul'qat]] (the "Twin Pyramid") in [[affiliation-neokbaate|Ne'ok Ba'ate]], across the days between the capture and the summer solstice, when the captive goes up the pyramid of [[lore-tzenultqandty|Ts'en'ul Tq'an]]. Nobody in Kul'taq'an says when the capture was, and the dance gives no Ts'enmah's name. It turns on the rule of [[skill-kiichek|Kì'ì'chek Language]]. A noble's name has three or more elements; kin, the household and equals use its first element alone, and a person of lower standing never does. The war-leader and the captive are of equal standing, so either may use the other's house name, and through the whole dance neither does. Each calls the other by his full name and his title at every speech, so that the audience hears the full names spoken more than twenty times before the end.

The drum stops before each speech and starts again after it. The speeches are given here as they are danced.

## The First Speech, at the Gate

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, seated at the left hand of its Ts'enmah. I took you in the stubble at the edge of your own fields, at the end of the war month, with the dew still on it. You did not run. I will say that before anybody says anything else: you did not run. You stood in the stubble with your steel and you waited for me, and I came, and it took the whole of the morning. My shoulder is still open where you opened it. Look at it. That is yours. I have brought you through the gate of Kul'taq'an on my own rope and not on a slave's, and I have told the gate whose rope it is. Now tell the gate who you are.

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an for this one campaign, named in the war month and to be unnamed when his captives have gone up the stair, I will tell the gate who I am. I am of the house that built the second pyramid of Q'o Kul'qat, the one that stands beside the first and is a hand's width higher. My mother's mother's father set the capstone. My father took four captives in his life and gave all four to the sun of my city. I took two before you took me. I have come into your city on your rope, and I have looked at your gate, and it is a good gate. It is lower than ours.

**The captor.** It is lower. We build on the plateau, where the wind is. A gate does not need to be high to keep you in.

**The captive.** It kept me in. I am in. I have said who I am.

## The Second Speech, at the Temple

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, I have taken you to the temple of the sun, and the Ch'alish Tq'an has looked at you, and the Ts'enbal has counted the days. I dedicated you on the field. When I bound your hands I said your name to the sun, and I said that you were his. That is the heaviest thing a man of the plateau can say. I said it with my shoulder open and your blood on my hands and mine on yours. The Warden of the Debt has entered you against the summer solstice. You are in the ledger of this city now, on the side of what is given.

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an, I heard you say my name to the sun. I was on my knees in my own furrows and I heard it. I have said a name to the sun twice myself. I know what it weighs. I would have said yours. If my foot had not gone into the furrow, I would have said yours to the sun of Q'o Kul'qat, and you would be in our ledger, on the side of what is given, and I would be standing where you stand, telling you our pyramid is higher.

**The captor.** It is higher. By a hand. I have seen it.

**The captive.** Then I have nothing to teach you, and we can go on.

## The Third Speech, at the Road

The drum is slow here, and the trumpets are silent. A dancer in a runner's dress crosses the plaza between the two warriors and does not stop, because no one stops a runner.

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, the road priests have come to my house. Your city has sent to mine on the Speaking Road. Your Ts'enmah has sent jade, two loads of it, the green that comes out of the mountains behind your city. Your house has sent cacao, more than my house has seen in my life. They are at my door. The road priests have carried them, and the road priests want their share for the carrying, and they will have it, because they carry everything and they sell to everyone. Your city wants you home.

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an, I know my city wants me home. I know what two loads of jade is. You know what it is to dedicate a captive and then take a ransom for him.

**The captor.** I know. The priests have taught it to me since I was a child in the temple school. A man who says a name to the sun and then sells the name leaves a hole in the sun's account. The sun keeps his own account with perfect patience. The hole is filled from the man's own household, in the sun's time, by the sun's choosing. My mother is old. My son is three.

**The captive.** Then send the jade back.

**The captor.** I have sent it back. The road priests have it. They took their share for carrying it both ways, which is twice. I want you to know that I looked at it for a whole night before I sent it back. It is very good jade.

**The captive.** It is the best jade there is. It comes from behind my city. I want you to know that I would have sent it back too. I would not have looked at it for a night.

**The captor.** You would have looked at it for a night.

**The captive.** I would have looked at it for a night.

## The Fourth Speech, the First Request

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an, the days are going. The Ts'enbal comes every morning and moves a stone. I have three things to ask of you. A man in my place may ask, and a man in your place may give or refuse. The first is a small thing. I want to drink the bitter drink of your house, out of your own cup, at your own hearth, sitting where your guests sit.

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, that is not a small thing. A man of the plateau gives his cup to his guest and to his equal. If I give you my cup at my hearth, I have said before my household that you are my equal.

**The captive.** You said it when you put me on your own rope.

**The captor.** I said it to the gate. My household was not at the gate.

**The captive.** Then say it to your household.

**The captor.** Bring the cup. Bring the bitter drink, with the pepper in it, the way it is made in this house. Bring the stool my father sat on. He is sitting where my father sat. He is drinking out of my cup. Let the household see it.

The two dancers sit facing each other on the ground, and the captive dancer drinks from a cup the captor dancer holds, and gives it back, and the drum does not play.

**The captive.** It is bitter.

**The captor.** It is the bitter drink.

**The captive.** In Q'o Kul'qat we make it sweeter. Ours is better.

**The captor.** Yours is sweeter.

## The Fifth Speech, the Second Request

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an, the second thing is the ledger. I am entered there against the solstice. The Warden of the Debt keeps it, and at the close of the month he will say the city stands in surplus, and I will be a part of the surplus. I know what the ledger is. It is bark paper and a scribe. The ledger enters what was given. It does not enter who. I want my name knotted. I want my whole name knotted into the cords of Kul'taq'an, every element of it, so that when your scribes read the cords in a hundred years and read out what this city gave the sun in the year of my death, they read my name out whole, and they say it was given by Q'o Kul'qat.

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, the cords are the priests'. I cannot knot them. I am a war-leader for one campaign.

**The captive.** You can ask the priests.

**The captor.** I have asked the priests. They said a captive is entered as a captive. They said the ledger is the city's and not the captive's city's. I asked them again. They said the same thing again. I asked them a third time, before the Ch'alish Tq'an, and I said that a man of the plateau had dedicated this name to the sun with his own mouth and wanted it knotted with his own hands, and that if the sun's account was exact the sun would want to know whose blood it was. The Ch'alish Tq'an looked at me for a long time. Then he said he would see it done.

**The captive.** Will he?

**The captor.** He said it before the Warden of the Debt. A priest who says a thing before the Warden of the Debt has entered it.

**The captive.** Then it is entered. Thank you.

**The captor.** Do not thank me. You have not asked the third thing.

## The Sixth Speech, the Third Request

The drum stops and does not start again until the end of the dance.

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an, the third thing is a word. When I go up the stair, I will go up alone, the way it is done. You will stand at the foot of it. I want you to call me by my house name.

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, I cannot.

**The captive.** You can. We are equals. You have said it at your gate and at your hearth. Equals may.

**The captor.** Equals may. Kin may. A man of my standing may say it to you in my house. He may not say it at the foot of the sun's stair, in front of the city, on the day of the solstice. At the stair you are not a man of my standing. At the stair you are the sun's, and the sun stands above everyone, and the sun is spoken to by his whole name, and so is whatever is his.

**The captive.** Then I will not hear it.

**The captor.** You will hear your whole name, every part of it, and your title, and your city. I will say it louder than any name has been said at that stair.

**The captive.** That is not what I asked for.

**The captor.** It is what I can give.

## The Seventh Speech, at the Stair

The two dancers stand at the foot of the stair of the plaza's own small temple, which stands in for the pyramid of the sun. The captive dancer climbs three steps and turns.

**The captor.** Ha'maq Ts'alam K'anul Ishkul, noble of the court of Q'o Kul'qat, seated at the left hand of its Ts'enmah, of the house that built the higher pyramid, taken in the stubble at the end of the war month, who did not run, who opened my shoulder, who drank from my cup at my father's hearth, whose name is knotted whole in the cords of Kul'taq'an, who is the sun's: go up.

**The captive.** Kul'iq Shak'nal Ts'umul Ishtem, war-leader of Kul'taq'an for this one campaign, who took me in the stubble, who sent back the jade after looking at it for a night, who asked the Ch'alish Tq'an three times: I am going up.

The captive dancer climbs three more steps. Then he stops, with his back to the plaza.

**The captive.** Kul'iq.

The captor dancer does not move for the length of a breath.

**The captor.** Ha'maq.

The captive dancer climbs the rest of the stair alone and goes in at the top, and the captor dancer kneels at the foot of it with his face in his hands until the drum begins again. The dancers come out together to the edge of the plaza, where the drink is served, and the captive dancer is given the cup first.

## How the Dance Is Argued

The priesthood of Kul'taq'an does not like the end. A captive is the sun's from the moment his name is dedicated, and the solar priests hold that a house name said at the sun's stair is said upward to something the speaker is below, which no one may do. They have asked more than once for the last two speeches to be cut. The warrior houses have refused, and the war-leaders who have danced the captor say the dance has always ended that way, and that a war-leader of Kul'taq'an who takes a captive in the stubble should know before he goes out what he will owe the man at the foot of the stair.

The city tells the matter of the third request two ways. In the warrior houses the captor says the house name because the captive said his first, and so one equal answered another and nobody spoke upward. In the temple school the priests teach that the captor erred, and that the cords of that year carry a knot for his error beside the captive's name. Nobody in Kul'taq'an outside the temple has seen the cords to know.
