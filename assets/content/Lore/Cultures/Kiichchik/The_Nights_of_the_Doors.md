**The Nights of the Doors**, _p'uqal kil shots'emal_, are the tales a household tells through the five days at the end of the [[lore-itsaniclndr#doors|Ki'ich'chik year]], the _shots'emal_ (the doors), which belong to no month and to no god. A hearth must not go out in those five days. A family whose fire dies has opened a door between the worlds, and a fire-priest of [[affiliation-kayikchalix|K'ayik Ch'alish]] must be fetched at once to close it. So the family sits up in turns through five nights, feeding the fire, and borrows _k'ayish_ (firewood) if its own runs short, and tells tales to keep the one on watch awake. The children are allowed up for the tale and sent to sleep after it.

Every house tells its own, and a house's tales are its own property, told the same way every year. These five are the tales of a potters' household in the Street of the Kilns in [[place-kankul2|K'ankul]], as its grandmother, [Toshel Ne']{.name data-type="being/npc"}, told them in the year 4158 of the Fifth Age. She tells one on each night and fits it to the night's name: the empty, the cold, the hush, the edge and the door. The first four are the house's; the fifth is her own, and it ends on a count the whole city was keeping that year, because the doors two years on would close the eightieth round.

## Ts'amul, the Empty

Is the fire fed? Then sit where I can see you. Not you, you're on the watch after me, you sit by the wood.

This is Ts'amul, the empty, the first of the doors. The months have run out. There is nothing left in the count to call the day by, so it is called by what it is, and what it is is empty. The work stops. The kilns go cold. The temple opens no day in the doors for planting or marrying or building or war, and the potters' street does what every street does: it shuts its doors and sits up with its fire. So this is the empty one, and it is about an empty house.

In the year of this tale, which was a long time ago, the rains were late, and then they were too much, and the ceiba wood came down the river green or not at all. The Street of the Kilns burns more wood than any street in K'ankul, and that year it burned what it had in the kilns before the doors came. A widow lived three doors down from this house, where the weaver's house is now. Her name was [Shelem Ts'uma]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and she had two children and a kiln that her husband had built, and on the evening before Ts'amul she had three sticks of wood. Three sticks will not last five nights. Three sticks will not last one.

She did not go to the temple. The fire-keepers sell good wood in the doors, and they sell it at a doors price, and she had nothing to give them but pots, and in the doors nobody buys pots. She did not go to the salt-seller at the end of the street, though he had a store of wood behind his house as high as the roof, because a person who borrows from the salt-seller owes the salt-seller for the rest of her life. She stood in her own doorway in the last of the light and she looked up and down the street, and then she went to the house next to hers, which was this house, and asked.

The grandmother of this house in those days gave her a stick of mahogany, which burns slow, and said, "That is owed." That is what you say when you lend wood in the doors. You say it so it is said, and then nobody has to say it again.

So Shelem Ts'uma went to the next house. The weaver gave her a stick of ceiba and said, "That is owed." The old soldier who lived over the dye-pits gave her the shaft of a spear. He said it had never killed anybody and it might as well warm somebody, and that it was owed. The priest's widow at the corner gave her a bundle of pine kindling soaked in copal, which is a rich gift and smokes, and said, "That is owed," and then she said, "Do not burn it all at once, it will smell like a temple." The water-carrier's boys gave her the cobs of the maize they had eaten that day, a whole basket of them, because cobs burn, and they were too young to know you have to say it, so their mother came out and said it for them.

She went down the whole street, both sides, and every house gave her one stick, and every house said, "That is owed." She went past the salt-seller's door. He was standing in it, and he watched her go by with her arms full, and he did not offer, and she did not ask.

That year, in the doors, the empty house was the warmest house in the Street of the Kilns. It had a stick of every wood that burns in K'ankul. The spear-shaft burned blue at the end where the iron had been. The copal kindling made the whole street smell like the pyramid steps, and the priest's widow said she had told her so. The children took turns with the boys from across the way, and the cobs went on last, on the fifth night, when everybody's wood was low, and they burned fast and bright and made popping noises, and the children laughed in the doors, which is not done, and nobody stopped them.

At the solstice every hearth in the street went out on purpose, the way they do, and was lit again from the temple fire, the way they are. That was the end of the borrowed fire.

At the harvest Shelem Ts'uma paid it back. She did not pay it back in wood. She made a pot for every house in the street, and she made each pot in the shape of what that house had lent her. The mahogany house got a tall pot, brown, with the grain painted down it. That pot is still in this house. It is on the shelf over the water-jar, and you are not to touch it. The soldier got a pot as long and thin as a spear, which is no use for anything, and he kept his maize beer in it until he died. The priest's widow got a little round pot with holes in the lid for burning copal, so it would smell like a temple only when she wanted it to. The water-carrier's boys got a basket that was a pot, painted like a basket, with clay cobs in it.

The salt-seller got no pot. He had lent nothing, so nothing was owed, and she owed him nothing for the rest of her life.

Nobody knotted that debt in a cord, and nobody wrote it. The Warden of the Debt never heard of it. Everybody in the street remembered it, and it was paid.

The fire is still in. That is the one for the empty night. Go to sleep.

## Ha'kik, the Cold

Is the fire fed? Sit close tonight. This is Ha'kik, the cold one, and you are going to want to sit close.

The second night of the doors is the night the fire is most likely to go out. On the first night everybody is awake because it is the first night, and nobody has been sitting up yet. On the second night the one on watch has been awake a whole night already, and the warm comes off the fire, and the eyes come down. So this is a tale about a fire that went out, and who came in.

In a house at the far end of this street, before the salt-seller's family built where they are, there was a boy whose turn it was to watch the fire in the hours after midnight. He was about your size. He was proud of having the watch, because it was the first year he had been given it. He sat down by the hearth with the wood beside him, and he fed it, and he fed it again, and then he decided he would close his eyes only while he counted twenty, because that would be all right.

When he opened them the fire was out.

Not low. Out. The ash was grey and there was not one red thing in it. He put his hand on the hearthstone and it was cold, colder than it should be, cold all the way through, like a stone in the river. And the house was cold. And the door was shut, the way it had been shut all night, and the cold was coming in anyway, as if the door were standing open.

He looked across the hearth, and somebody was sitting on the other side of it.

They were sitting in the warm place, where the old people sit, with their back to him, and their back was wrapped in a blanket the colour of the ash. They did not move. They did not turn round. They were sitting at the dead fire the way you sit at a live one, with their hands held out to it.

The boy did not scream. He did not wake his mother. He did the thing that was in his head from every telling of this tale he had ever heard, which was the right thing: he got up very quietly and he went out of the back of the house, over the wall, to the house next door, and he scratched on their door the way you do in the doors, and he said, "Our fire is dead."

The girl who had the watch in that house was older than him, and quicker. She did not ask him anything. She took a little pot, and she took two embers out of her own fire with the tongs, and she put them in the pot with a handful of ash on top to keep them, and she came back over the wall with him. And her mother, who had woken up, sent her brother running for the fire-priest, because that is the law, and the girl knew it and did it anyway.

The girl went into the boy's house first. She did not look at the warm place. She knelt at the cold hearth, and she put the embers in, and she blew on them, and she fed them with the shavings from her apron until they caught, and the fire came up. When the fire came up the cold went out of the house all at once, like breath going out of somebody.

The warm place was empty. There was a blanket folded on the stone there, the colour of ash. Nobody in the house owned it.

The fire-priest came at a run with his brother priest and a pot of the temple's fire, and he stood in the doorway and saw the fire already burning, and he was not pleased. He said, "Whose fire is that?"

The girl said, "Ours. From our hearth."

The fire-priest said, "Then the door is not closed. A door opened in the doors is closed by a priest of the flame and nobody else. That is the law."

The boy's grandmother was awake by then, and sitting up in her bed, and she said, "Her fire is the temple's fire. Her mother lit it from the temple at the solstice, same as mine. Yours is the temple's fire. Where is the difference?"

The fire-priest said the difference was the rite and the fee. And he was right, and they all knew it, and he did the rite. He put out the girl's fire and lit the hearth again from his own pot, and he said the words over the threshold and over the hearthstone and over the place where the cold had come in, and he burned the ash-coloured blanket in the street, and he took the fee. The fee was a whole year's pots from that house. They paid it.

But when he went out, he stopped at the girl's door, and he gave her mother half of it back, and he said, "For carrying the temple's fire." And he went away up the street with his brother priest and his pot. Some people say that priest was a hard man and some people say he was a fair one. This house says both.

The boy had the watch every year after that, in the hours after midnight, until he was a grandfather, and he never closed his eyes again in the doors, not even to count twenty. He married the girl, which is why that is in the tale.

The fire is still in. That is the one for the cold night. Who has the watch after me? Wake her when I'm done, and sit close.

## Sh'an, the Hush

Is the fire fed? Then quiet. This is Sh'an, the hush. On the third night of the doors nobody in this street speaks above a whisper, not to the children, not to the dog, not to the fire. The hush is the middle of the doors, and you speak low so that what is on the other side of the door does not hear you through it and think it has been called.

This is the tale of the grandmother at the door.

There was a woman in this house, a long time ago, whose mother died in the hot months. They were poor that year. The death-walkers came, as they come to everybody, and the Speaker of the Trials offered the long recitation and the short, and the family had the price of the short, and that is what they bought. You know what the short is. You have sat through one, at your grandfather's. The Speaker names the first rooms of [[place-chaltzumaq|the road under the world]] and the first lies in them, and then he tells the dead to go on by herself, and the family sits through the night and listens and does not speak. That is the rule. The family is quiet so the dead hears only the Speaker.

So they sat, and they were quiet, and at dawn they carried her down to the pool chamber and the water took her. And that was the hot months.

In the doors that year, on the hush night, there was a scratching at the door.

You know the scratching. It is what you do in the doors, when you need something from a house, so as not to knock. And then a voice through the door said the woman's name, the way her mother had always said it, low on the first part. Her mother was in the water.

The voice said, "It's cold out here. Let me in to the fire."

The woman did not open the door. You do not open a door in the doors, not on the hush night, not to anybody. And she knew, and her husband knew, and the children knew, because it is in every telling, that a soul that comes back to the door has lost its way in the rooms below. It is one of the lost, the _ts'uqilob_, and if you let it in to the warm it will sit there, and it will not get up again.

The voice said, "I went down. I went into the black water and I went through it. I went past the hearth. And then he stopped talking. He stopped telling me. And there are more rooms, and I don't know their names, and I don't know which way, and I've been walking in them since the hot months, and it's cold."

The family did not speak. That was the rule. They had been told to be quiet at the night watch, and they had been quiet, and they sat by their own fire and were quiet now, and their mother sat outside the door in the cold and asked to come in.

The voice said all their names, one by one, everybody in the house. It asked after each of them. It asked if the kiln had fired well. It asked if the youngest had his teeth. It was her. It was her voice.

And the woman got up. She did not open the door. She went and sat down against it, on the inside, with her back to it, and her mother was sitting against it on the other side. You could hear it. She put her mouth near the crack at the side, and she started to speak. She said the first room. She said the black water, and the light that comes up behind you, and not to turn round, because it was not dawn. And the voice outside said, I know, I did that. And the woman said the hearth, and not to sit. And the voice said, I know, I did that too. And then the woman said the third room.

She did not know the third room. She had never heard it. They had bought the short one. She said it anyway. She said the rooms the way she thought a room would be, in the words she had, the way you tell a child the road to the market when you have only been there once. She said there would be a narrow place, and to go sideways. She said there would be a lord in the road, and that her mother was not to stop for any lord. She said there would be an animal, and to let it look. She said there would be a door, and a kind voice at it, telling her to come back, and that it would be the voice of her own daughter, and that she was not to listen to it.

She talked all night. Her husband fed the fire. The children did not sleep; they lay and listened. Nobody else in the house said a word, because that was the rule, and she was breaking it for them.

Before dawn the voice outside said, "Is it far?"

And the woman said, "I don't know. Go behind the sun. He knows the way."

And the voice said, "All right."

And then there was nobody at the door.

At the dark of the moon after the doors, at the Calling of the Dead, the woman went down to the pools with the whole street and said her mother's name with the names of all the dead since the last turning, out loud, with everybody, which is allowed. And this house has bought the long recitation for every death since, even when it meant selling the kiln. That is why we are not rich. That is why you will have the long one, when it is your turn, and so will I.

The fire is still in. Quiet now. That is the one for the hush.

## Tq'al, the Edge

Is the fire fed? Good. You can laugh tonight. This is Tq'al, the edge. The worst of the doors is over and the door is not yet here, and on the edge you are allowed to laugh. This is the tale of how the sun-priests lost the argument.

You know that the temple opens the days. If you want to marry, you go to [[affiliation-tzenultqan|the solar priests]] and pay them, and they find you an open day. If you want to build, or plant, or go on a journey, the same. And nothing is opened in the doors, nothing at all, because the doors are no days.

There was a young sun-priest once, very well born, very handsome, very sure of the count, and in the doors he was walking through this street on some temple business with his robe held up out of the clay. And as he came past the house that is the dyer's house now, a woman inside started to have her baby.

You know what that sounds like. He stopped. He listened. And then he went to the door and he scratched on it, and when the midwife came out (she was a water-priestess of [[affiliation-xaqikhanal|Shaq'ik Ha'nal]], an old one, older than me), he told her the child could not be born.

She said, "Can't it?"

He said it was the doors. The doors are no days. A child born in the doors is born on no day, and has no day to be marked by, and no name to take from its day, and nothing is to be begun in the doors, and a birth is the beginning of a person. He said she must wait until the solstice, when the count opens again, and then the child could be born on a proper day, and he would find her an open one. He said all this in the doorway. It took some time.

The old midwife let him finish. Then she said, "You tell her."

And she held the door open, so he could.

He looked in. He looked at the mother. The mother looked back at him. Nobody in this house has ever agreed about what the mother said, so this house tells it every way. Some years it was, "Come in and stop it, then." Some years it was a word your mother would wash out of your mouth. Some years the mother did not say anything at all, she only looked at him, and that was enough.

The sun-priest went away up the street with his robe held up out of the clay.

The child was born at the edge, on Tq'al, in the hour before dawn. The old midwife carried it to the jar and gave it the water-marking at that hour, the way it is given at the hour of any birth, whatever the day, and she gave it its name. Its name had no day-sign in it, because there was no day. She named it for the edge, _tq'an_, which is the edge and the joining both, and for the water she marked it with, and it was [Tq'an Ne']{.name data-type="being/npc"} all its life.

The sun-priests have been making the same argument ever since: that a child should wait for its day. They have taken it to the Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an more than once. They have never won it. Every water-priestess in K'ankul knows this tale, and when a sun-priest starts on the argument at a birth they hold the door open for him and say, "You tell her," and that is generally the end of it.

And Tq'an Ne', the child born on no day? Became a runner. Ran on the Speaking Road thirty years. Never once was late. Some years this house says it was because a child born on the edge is always at the edge of somewhere, and some years it says it was because the old midwife had told it every night of its childhood that it had been born early and had better keep it up.

The fire is still in. Laugh if you want. That is the one for the edge.

## Shots'ik, the Door

Is the fire fed? Then sit where you like. This is Shots'ik, the door, the last of the doors and the thinnest, and tomorrow is the solstice, and the year opens again. Tonight's is not the house's tale. It is mine, and I am the only one in this house who can tell it, because I am the only one who was there.

Every year on Shots'ik you sit up with the fire and keep it in, and at dawn you put it out yourselves and take the pot to the temple and bring the new fire home. That is every year. But once in fifty-two years, on Shots'ik, the priests come round themselves, at first light, and they put out every fire in the city. Every hearth. Every lamp. The temple fires too, the ones that are never let go out. It is the one dark the faith allows. And then for a day and a night nobody in K'ankul makes a sound, because if the fire cannot be drawn again in the morning, the sun has gone back on his word, and [[lore-itsaniclndr#round|the age is over]].

I was nine years old, the last time. That was the year 4108. My mother was the age your mother is now.

I remember the priests coming down the street with their pots of water and their wet blankets, and going into every house, and the hearths hissing one after the other all down the street like a snake going by. I remember my grandfather's face when they did ours. He had kept that fire in through the doors every year of his life, and here was a priest in a wet blanket putting it out on purpose, and he had to stand there and let him. And then the lamps. And then the temple fire up on the pyramid went out, and you could see the smoke stop, because it was the last fire in the city. All that day nobody lit anything, and then the evening came, and the city was dark.

I want you to understand dark. You have never seen it. You think the doors are dark, and they are not; you have the fire. There was no fire anywhere. No lamp on any wall. The stars, and that was all. My mother held my hand all day, and when the dark came she held it all night, and she did not let it go once, not to eat, not for anything. Nobody ate. Nobody spoke. The dogs were quiet. I remember the dogs being quiet. A baby cried somewhere down the street and its mother stopped it, and I remember thinking, how do you stop a baby, and I thought perhaps she had done something terrible, and I was frightened all night about the baby. It was all right. I asked, after.

Then the night was nearly done, and before the dawn of the solstice the whole city went out into the dark streets, and up onto the roofs, and onto the terraces, and onto the steps of every temple, without a sound, and all faced the same way, toward the pyramid, where the Presiding Ch'alish Tq'an and the Master of the Living Flame had gone up in the dark with the mirrors and the jade.

My father put me on his shoulders.

And the sun came up. That was the first thing. You don't know how glad you can be to see the sun come up, because you have never not been sure of it. It came up red over the pools and it touched the top of the pyramid first, before anything else, and we could see the men up there, small, kneeling. And nothing happened. And nothing happened. My father's hands were so tight on my ankles that I had the marks a week.

Then there was smoke. A thread. You could only just see it against the sky. And then there was a flame on top of the pyramid.

I cannot tell you the noise. The whole city at once, every person in it, after a day and a night of nothing. I heard it in my chest. I heard it in my father's shoulders under me. People were crying in the street, grown men, soldiers. The potter from the end of the street was hugging the salt-seller's father, and they hated each other.

Then the Kindlers came down the steps with the fire, running, with torches, and split at the bottom, one to every quarter, and the quarter priests lit from them, and the street shrines lit from those, and every house sent somebody with a pot to the street shrine. This house sent me. My grandfather sent me. He said I was the one to carry it, because I was the one who would see the next one.

I carried it home in this pot. This one. I carried it with both hands and I did not run, because if you run with an ember it goes out, and I did not let it go out. And my grandfather knelt at the cold hearth and blew it up, and the fire that is in that hearth now, that you are feeding now, came out of that pot on that morning. It has been put out every solstice and lit again from the temple, so it is not the same fire, the priests say, and they are right. It is the same pot.

He was right. I am the one who will see the next one. This is the 4158th year. One more year's doors will come and go, and at the doors after those the priests will come down the street with their wet blankets on Shots'ik, and it will be dark. I will be sixty-one, if I am anything, and I am going to be there. And you are going to be there. And you will carry the pot.

Don't run with it.

The fire is still in. Go to sleep. In the morning we put it out ourselves and go and get the new one, and it is a year.
