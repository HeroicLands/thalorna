The Yesoma are the canopy foragers, one of the four traditions of the [[lore-bikoclt|Biko]] forest peoples, and the largest. Their name is _yeso_, the forest, with the ending of a child: they are the forest's children, and they say so of themselves before anything else.

## Where They Live

Yesoma tribes live in the deep interior of the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]], in the country between the great rivers that no outsider has mapped. A tribe ranges over a stretch of forest a few days' walk across, and its bands cross it on the old elephant trails and on paths of their own that no one else knows.

## How They Live

A Yesoma band is a few families, and it moves. It camps in domed huts of bent saplings and leaves, built in an afternoon, and moves on when the game thins, when the honey season calls it elsewhere, or when the camp quarrels. It keeps no gardens and no stores.

The band lives by the net-hunt, the honey and the nut. In a net-hunt the men string their nets in a long curve across the forest floor and the women and children drive the game into them with shouting and beaten branches. In the honey season the bands of a tribe gather, climb for the combs, and eat little else for weeks. Women gather nuts, fruit, mushrooms, grubs and roots the year round. What the band cannot find it trades for at a [[lore-luwandoclt|Luwando]] landing, honey and ivory for fish, salt and metal.

Everything is shared. Every member of the band is owed a _kpeti_, a share, of every kill and every comb, and the hunter whose net took the animal does the dividing and keeps nothing more than anyone else. Keeping back food is the worst thing a Yesoma can do, worse than violence, and a person who does it is mocked until the band laughs it out of them.

## What the Forest Is

To the Yesoma the forest is a parent: _iyemo_, mother-and-father in one word, which no other tradition uses. The forest feeds whoever lives as its child, and a child does not bargain with a parent, keep a garden in its skin or sell what it is given. When the hunting fails or someone falls ill, the band has done nothing to deserve it. The forest has fallen asleep and stopped watching over its children, and the band wakes it.

The _sogola_ is the band of song that does the waking. When things go badly, the men of the band sing to the forest every night, from dark until near dawn, sometimes for a month, until the hunting comes back. Women and children stay in the huts and listen. The songs are never sung to outsiders, and they are never sung at all while things go well.

## The Four Standings, and the Leader

A Yesoma child is a _tolu_ until it kills its first animal in a net-hunt and divides the meat itself, keeping no more than its kpeti. Then it is _wibo_. A stranger the band takes in is a tolu until the band has eaten from that stranger's share without noticing whose it was.

Nobody makes a Yesoma _kpaso_. The leader is recognised, not chosen. A tribe's bands come to camp near one person's fire at the honey gatherings, to bring that person their quarrels, and to give that person the first comb of the season, and when they have done this for a few seasons, that person is the kpaso. The leader decides nothing alone. A kpaso who gives orders is laughed at, and a kpaso who keeps back food stops being the leader the same day, because the bands camp elsewhere. A Yesoma tribe often goes years without a leader and feels no lack.

The _mbeli_ matter more than the leader. They remember which trails are whose, where the honey trees are, and which songs woke the forest last time.

## Kin

Kin is the fire a person eats at. A band forms and re-forms each season as families leave and join it, and whoever shares a band's fire is that band's kin while they share it. Every adult of a grandparent's age is called grandparent, and every adult of a parent's age is called mother or father.

Young people meet and marry at the honey gatherings, and a couple lives with either band, or moves between them. A marriage that does not hold is ended by one partner walking to another band.

## The Dead

The dead are left to the forest. When someone dies, the band lays the body in their own hut, closes the doorway with leaves, and leaves the camp that day. The abandoned camp is _hewo_, and no band camps on it again. The dead person's name is not spoken until a child is born who is given it.

The Yesoma do not speak to the dead as the other traditions do. In their teaching, the dead have gone back to the parent, and that is all there is to say.

## The Other Three

In the Yesoma's account:

- The Luwando sold their mother. They sit in one place, scratch gardens into the forest's skin and hand its gifts to strangers for metal. The Yesoma trade with them all the same, because the metal is good.
- The [[lore-kubindoclt|Kubindo]] are afraid of the forest. They serve one corner of it like servants serving a stranger's house, and never trust the rest. The Yesoma find their manners tiring and their spirit-places worth avoiding.
- The [[lore-pewayeclt|Pewaye]] are hardly forest people at all. They hold the forest as a thing that belongs to them, which no child says of a parent, and they talk like outsiders.

## What an Outsider Sees

Almost nothing. An outsider seldom meets a Yesoma band, and the ones who do meet it on a trail, briefly, before it is gone. What an outsider sees is the honey and ivory arriving at a Luwando landing, a camp of leaf huts empty when the outsider reaches it, and now and then singing in the dark from a direction no one will name. Asked the way, a Yesoma points somewhere.
