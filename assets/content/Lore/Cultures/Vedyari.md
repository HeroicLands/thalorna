The **Vedyari** are one people across a map that has never been one state. A scholar from a southern harbor who lands at a northern pass-kingdom finds the temple, the assembly, the stations and the classical curriculum all intelligible on the first day, and the coinage and the king strange. What holds across [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]] is the station a person is born to, the household that station sits in, and the rites that mark both. What varies is who collects the tax.

A **Ritūja** who keeps the school at a janapada temple, asked by a foreign student where to begin, begins at the wrist. "Before you learn our gods or our grammar, learn where to look when someone gives you their hand. Everyone you meet here has already read yours, and found nothing there, and decided what that means."

## The Wrist-Line

A Vedyari is named eight days after birth, and the naming leaves a mark: two signs in the old abjad, cut small inside the left wrist. The first names the tharana, the order a person is born to; the second the kula, the lineage within it. An **Outcaste**'s is struck through with a single stroke, and a child born outside the order is marked so at the same age as any other.

The mark is the document. A temple gate asks to see the wrist, and so does a sabhā before it seats a speaker, a court before it hears a party, and a bride's household before it agrees to anything. None of them asks for a word about it; they read it themselves.

A foreigner's bare wrist is read as well. It carries no station, so it says nothing about where its owner may draw water or sit, and the safe course for a traveler is to wait to be shown: to the well, to a seat, to the dish. Asking is no discourtesy here; guessing wrong is.

Hiding a station means wrapping the wrist. It is common, and it is no offense in itself. A ship's master does not ask. Neither does a pass-guide, and the guide-peoples of the wall carry no mark of their own to compare it with. The [[affiliation-twilighths|Twilight House]] teaches the wrap as a fashion, which is exactly why a wrapped wrist in a city means less than it would in a village.

## Leaving a Station, Losing It, Hiding It

A station is not chosen and is rarely changed, and the ways out of one are few enough to name.

**It is lost.** A court or a sabhā puts a Karmāja or a Dhanāja out of the order for a judgment it names. A Senāja loses his by refusing the host's call or by one of the dishonors the military manuals set out at length. A Ritūja loses his to Patita, which brands the face, a mark every temple and house recognizes. It is read at every temple gate, and a forgiven Patita carries proof of the forgiveness for the rest of his life.

**It is left.** A Ritūja walks out as an ascetic, and loses the office and not the tharana; he is received with reverence wherever he goes and kept by the devout. Nobody else has this door.

**It is bought out.** A Bonded Servant's debt is paid—at the great pilgrim assembly, where the bond-holders come to be paid, or by a letter of credit from the [[affiliation-mrchntclctvvdyr|Merchant Collective]]. A runaway who reaches the sea or the pass-kingdoms is not returned, and both know it.

**It is hidden.** The wrist is wrapped and the person goes where the wrap is not read: the deck, the road under the traveler's cord, the high country. An [[affiliation-agnipantha|Agnī-panthā]] **Purgation** readmits an Outcaste to the order, and the orthodox hierarchy does not recognize the readmission, so a person so purged is inside the order in one half of the subcontinent and outside it in the other.

## What a Station Asks

The Vedyari answer to what is good is _dharma_, duty, and the answer is specific to the person asked. **Good is the work of one's station done whole and in the open; evil is the petition left unheard and the dead left unburned.** Both are the sins of a king, and the chronicle of [[place-rajapur|Rājapur]] names both against the second of its last six: he stopped hearing petitions, and then forbade the pyre for the men who came to be heard. A well-keeper adds a third from the plateau: the crown that takes its share of a well and does not guarantee the lineage against a raid has broken the whole bargain, whatever it kept.

Success is a station filled out, not a station escaped. A Karmāja who is the man the village sends when the river is up has succeeded, and so has a Dhanāja whose ledger a sabhā will accept without an audit. The marks run in a recognizable order: a household that holds its use-right unbroken, a seat in the sabhā when the village's turn comes, a name the temple writes in its roll, sons and daughters married within the kula with the elders of both houses content, and a pyre lit within the day by people who know whose it is. Failure is the wrap that has to be worn, the debt that ends in a bond-token, the judgment that puts a person out of the order, or a death that leaves nobody who will tend the lamp. Poverty is not on the list. A poor household that keeps its station and its dead is a household in good standing.

A Vedyari values the duty done without being asked to do it, the guest fed before the question, the record kept whole, the craft practiced to the classical standard and the bargain a temple can see. A Vedyari values least the claim that rests on nothing a sabhā can read: a king's word with no guarantee behind it, a priest's fee taken for arms, a station asserted by a mark nobody will show. It gives its highest regard to the plain work of the station of labor when the work is a ferry rope and a flood.

## The Household

The household, not the person, is what the order is built out of. It holds the land or the use-right, it answers for its members at the sabhā, it pays the temple's share, and it keeps the bond-tokens of those bound to it. A Vedyari introduces himself by kula before trade and by village before either.

The household is the people who eat at one hearth, the _kovala_, and the kula reaches beyond it: the kin within a station, whose elders (_sthavira_) settle what the households cannot. Membership is the wrist. A child is named eight days after birth, and from that day it holds a place in both a household and a kula, and the household answers for it at the sabhā until it is of an age to be answered for. A person bonded to a household is kept by it for the work and carries its token and not its mark.

Marriage is within the kula, arranged between households and settled by the elders of both. The bride's household shows the wrist-line and the groom's household shows the ledger. A marriage across tharana is not impossible and is not survivable in a village; the cities make room for it, and the children carry the lower of the two marks.

A life climbs a ladder that the temple marks with the conch. The black conch sounds at a birth, at coming of age and at the last breath, and the household's own rungs sit between. An infant is named at eight days. A child hears the Measuring Cord on the first morning of a year, before the letters and before the count. The young are taught a trade or a classical curriculum by a master, or a bow at Dhanurkota, where the academy's first lesson is that the station of the man beside you changes nothing inside the walls. A young adult is married within the kula and takes a place in the household's work. An adult sits in the sabhā when the village's turn comes, and holds a use-right in the janapada's land. The old are _sthavira_, elders, whose standing in the kula grows as their strength fails, and a Ritūja may walk out as an ascetic. The dying are the household's, with the lamp, and then the Kālavrata's.

## Two Names

The same Ritūja teaches names next, and as a warning. "You will learn two names for everyone here, and you will be given the second one late, if at all. The formal name—the given name and the clan, [[being-pdmvldhnrvdkrtrj|Padmāvali Dhanurvedakīrtirāja]] in full—is what the temple writes in its rolls and what a court reads out, and it is what you say to anyone you have not been asked to call otherwise. The short one is the _calling name_, cut from the given name, and it belongs to kin and friends: her mother calls her Padmi. It is offered, never taken. Use a calling name nobody gave you and you have claimed a closeness you were not granted, and that is a presumption the household will remember." The rule a calling name is cut by stands in the [[doc-vedyarilexcn|Vedyari Lexicon]].

## Water, Food and the Touched Thing

The village well is the plainest reading of the order. Who may draw, who may draw only after, and who may not approach are all settled and all known, and a stranger who gets it wrong is corrected once and watched afterward. Cooked food passes downward in the order freely and upward not at all; uncooked grain passes either way. An Outcaste's touch on a vessel ends the vessel.

None of this is held to be cruelty by the people who keep it. It is held to be the shape of a world in which a birth follows from a life already lived, and in which the duties of a station are the road out of it—not in this life, but in the next.

## The Man Admired

**Taranapāla**, the keeper of the crossing, is the figure the upper-river villages tell about at the hearth once a year ([[lore-ferrymanford|The Ferryman at the Ford]]), and the oath at the center of the telling is one every child of the upper river can say before it can read. He was a Karmāja, born to labor, who kept a flat boat at a ford and took what he was given. In the year the river came up three weeks early he carried the far-bank village across to safety in nine crossings: children first, then the old, then the women with child, then the men who could not swim, then the men who could and would not, then the grain, then the temple's records in a jar held above the priest's head, and then, after the rope tore loose from a terrace wall that nobody laid in living memory, he went across once more without it for the last four on the hill. He asked for nothing and slept a day and a night.

The villages asked him three times to be the man they fed so that one person on the river held what lay between the villages: the ford, the pilgrim road, the channel two villages quarreled over, the gate the raiders came to. He refused twice, because a man paid whether or not anyone crosses stops caring whether anyone crosses. He took it the third time on an oath that bound the villages as well: they feed him, and the day he holds the ford for himself, the ford is theirs again, and they say so before the god. He built no larger house than the rest, took no second measure and died in a bed a Karmāja would recognize, burned within the day on the bank where he had tied the rope.

The Vedyari admire in him a man who had no rank, claimed none, and was the most useful person on the river for thirty years. His cost is the one the telling refuses to soften. The measure that fed the rope fed a house, and the house fed a line, and the line became the kings the sabhā dissolved. The villages revere the first ferryman and are watchful of everything his office grew into.

## The Woman Admired

**Padmika**, a widow of the last village above the ford, is the figure the Agnī-panthā sings ([[lore-borrowedlamp|The Borrowed Lamp]]), and the rule she keeps is every household's. A stranger with ash on his arms came to her door at dusk. She set millet before him before she asked him anything. When he said he was going down to the ford with no fire, she lent him the threshold lamp of her house, the one that had burned four nights by her husband's bed in the winter and done one death as a death is done. Her two sons had gone down the same road with the villages and been kept off by spears through the rains. The lamp, she told him, had done one burning and owed two. The house kept silence until it came back, and the silence had lasted ten years.

The admiration is for a woman who gave the thing she could least spare to a stranger because the stranger asked for the dead. She is the household at its best: the guest fed first, the lamp tended, the dead owed their fire, and the quarrel put down while the lamp is out. She holds no office and has no station beyond the widow's and the house's, and the song holds that her lamp lit the fire that took.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of Taranapāla's. He is admired for carrying a village across, and she for keeping the house that gives a carried village a bowl of millet and a fire. The cost is plain in the song. Two sons dead unburned, a husband burned alone, and ten years of quiet in a house that would not raise a voice until the lamp came home. The orthodox temples teach that the unburned dead are Kālavrata's affair. Padmika's household did not wait for a priest to say so.

## What the Temple Keeps

A Vedyari can point to a handful of things that hold the order together. **The measuring cord** is a knotted cord the master of a temple school hands to the smallest child at the end of the first recitation of the year; the children measure the temple court with it, knot by knot, before they are allowed to eat. **The Oath on the Terrace** is the villages' oath to the keeper of the ford, which the telling says has not changed by a word since it was first sworn. **The roll at Sangama** carries every janapada that has been recognized and every one that has collapsed. **The wrist-line** is the document every gate asks to see. **The record in the temple**, the survey, the boundary stones' register and the genealogies, is what a janapada governs with, and a temple that burns with its record has lost its fields twice. **The threshold lamp** and **the black conch above the doorpost** are the household's own scripture, and **the Coin of Passage** is the whole of its theology in a copper disc.

The classical education starts from **the commentaries, the legal codes and the epics**, and the Memory-Keepers of Rājapur keep **the Recitation** of the six last kings and the decree of the Forty Days, read through at the Day of the Dissolution.

## The Year

Vedyara keeps the [[lore-mdhvndrcnt|Madhusthāna count]]. The public year is sighted by the [[affiliation-trimurtisampradaya|Triyanga-sampradāya]] at the [[place-suryatempl|Sūrya temple]] where the [[place-chandrmahi|Chandramahī]] comes out of the ice, and computed, separately, by the reckoners at [[place-chandrapur2|Chandrapur]]. The two figures differ by a day often enough that a court states which one it follows before it fixes a coronation, a Weighing or a festival, and a household that has traveled for the wrong one has no recourse.

The ritual year and the agricultural year are the same year. The monsoon sets both, [[affiliation-meghanatha|Meghanātha]] is petitioned at its onset by every household that grows anything, and the great pilgrim assembly falls in the dry season because that is when the roads carry.

## The Year's Rites

The year is full of rites that settle one thing each. On the first morning of the year the master of the Vyālendra school at Rājapur recites the Measuring Cord to the new children of every village of the janapada, so that the shaper's knot is the first thing a child learns. The **Spring Festival of Vyālendra** draws the neighboring janapadas to the great temple. At the onset of the monsoon every household that grows anything petitions Meghanātha, and the priest who keeps the drum tells a new family in the last dry week to take the biggest pot they own and a spoon and strike it at the first beat. The **Procession of the Three** at midsummer carries the three forms of the Triyanga through every city that keeps a Triyanga temple to a combined dedication, and the **Reconciliation of the Year** at the winter solstice is the day on which community quarrels left unresolved are brought to the Triyāchārya, with the three forms as witnesses.

At Dhanurkota the **Festival of the Drawn Bow** at the spring equinox presents each year's new students to the temple, and the **Festival of the Returning** in autumn welcomes the graduates home from a year's service abroad, in a court where the reading does not end until the gate is opened to them. The **Night of the Kindled Road** at the autumn equinox lights the Agnī-panthā's ember-vessels into one fire. The **Night of Silver** is the Svapnasāri-samāja's vigil under the full moon. Rājapur alone keeps the **Day of the Dissolution**, on which no work is done and the whole town hears the Memory-Keeper recite the decree from beginning to end. Every twelfth year the Mahā-Mela on the Mela Ground runs forty days: thirty of worship and ten of the confederation's business.

Each is for something. The year-start gives a child a measure. The monsoon drum asks for rain. The Reconciliation puts down a year's quarrels. The Returning welcomes the people the janapada sent out. The Dissolution keeps the reason a kingdom ended in front of everyone who could repeat it. The Mela sets the water, the roads and the next twelve years.

## Letters and Learning

An educated Vedyari reads [[skill-vedyarlng|Classical Vedyari]] in the [[skill-vdykshrscrpt|Vedyākshara]], and the learning is the same learning from one end of the subcontinent to the other: the commentaries, the legal codes, the epics, and the mathematics and astronomy that carry their own notation and cannot be read without the syllabary. A merchant's clerk learns the running hand and no classical at all, and is not called educated.

The temple is the school, and it is also the record office, the granary and the bank. Boundary stones, genealogies and land grants are its documents, and its authority over a dispute is documentary before it is priestly.

## Making, and Devotion

Devotion in Vedyara most often takes the form of making something. Dance, music, sculpture, poetry and the mural-work of the temple complexes are codified to a degree an outsider mistakes for stiffness, and a musician or a sculptor at the top of the classical tradition is held to be a spiritual practitioner of a demanding kind, not an artisan who happens to be devout. Weaving, metalwork and gem-cutting are hereditary and are held the same way at a lower pitch.

## The Threshold

A Vedyari household expects to be told when a death is coming, and to have time. The threshold lamp is lit at the onset of a final illness and tended until the passage is finished; nobody in the house quarrels or speaks loudly while it burns. [[affiliation-kalavrata|Kālavrata]]'s conch is sounded at the last breath, the [[miscgear-coinpassage|Coin of Passage]] is put in the hand, and no part of what follows may be hurried.

The pyre follows within the day. Between the conch and the pyre there is a gap, and in the polities that license it a [[affiliation-thresholdkeepers|Threshold-keeper]] may be called into that gap to put one question. Most households never see it done. Every household knows the gap is there, and a family that declines the question when a court asks for it is understood to be declining for a reason.

## What the Next Life Asks of Today

A birth follows from a life already lived, and the duties of a station are the road out of it, not in this life but in the next. That teaching reaches an ordinary day at several points. A Vedyari does the station's work properly, because doing it badly is worse than doing it unwillingly and the next life is made of it. The household's lamp, the conch above the doorpost and the Coin of Passage are kept ready, so a death is met without scrambling. A Karmāja pays the temple's share in labor and a Dhanāja in coin.

The same teaching shapes law and burial. Where a household's succession turns on what a dead person meant, a sealed will stands over any answer the Threshold-keepers bring back, and the answer weighs only against the documents. Burial is the pyre, within the day, and a household that cannot afford a struck coin files a tāmra blank. The dread that corresponds to all of it is the field where the pyre was never lit.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the household's rites and, where a court warrants it, through the Threshold-keepers. The rite is the lamp, the conch, the Coin of Passage in the hand and the pyre within the day, and it asks nothing of Kālavrata, who is not petitioned and answers no request for long life or safe passage. The Threshold-keepers put one question to the newly dead in the gap between the conch and the pyre, with two Witnesses to write down the answer. The practice is warranted for two things only, inheritance and an unwitnessed killing, and it is forbidden for war and for trade, because a practice that answers a question of advantage would be used at every death worth an advantage, and the households would stop lighting the lamp. The gap belongs to the household. A family may decline, and a court reads the refusal as a reason without treating it as an admission.

The dead reach the living in two ways. The ancestors, _sorhu_, have a lamp in the window on the anniversary of each family member's passing, and the household keeps their names. The restless dead, _olūra_, reach the living where the pyre was never lit: on a blood-field, where the way behind the souls never closed, and nothing living goes in bodily. The Agnī-panthā's priests burn such ground with a fast, a confession of the district's sins and grass-fires walked across it in lines; the orthodox temples hold that the unburned dead are Kālavrata's affair and not a priest's.

## The Road

Travel is a religious act before it is a commercial one. The traveler's cord is knotted before the household hearth the evening before a long journey, a knot for each shrine the traveler undertakes to honor, and untied at the same hearth on the return with each shrine named aloud. A coin or a pinch of millet goes to the [[affiliation-pavanajitras|Pavanajitras]] at every crossroads and every waterway.

A guest is fed before he is questioned. A household that turns a traveler away at nightfall is talked about, and a household that turns away an ascetic is talked about for a generation.

## What Station Earns and What Breaking It Costs

Reward is a station kept, widened by the household's standing. The Karmāja who is trusted with the village's turn in the sabhā; the Dhanāja whose tithe the temple counts as sound; the Senāja who answers the host's call and is answered as a gentleman; the Ritūja who holds the temple's court and bears arms for it and never for pay. In the janapadas the temple's roll holds the household's name, and the sabhā's trust follows that record. In the passes a Mahārāja's court gives its Sāmanta land and a fort.

The penalties climb the same ladder downward. A janapada that ignores the Mela's consensus meets its peers' disapproval and the slow withdrawal of cooperation, which can end its water arrangements and its trade, and one judged to have behaved shamefully is denounced in public at the Mela. A person is dealt with by a court or a sabhā, which puts a Karmāja or a Dhanāja out of the order for a judgment it names. A Senāja loses his station by refusing the host's call. A Ritūja who takes pay for arms is Patita, put out of the order for good and branded on the face, and the brand is read at every temple gate. Below the order's floor is the Outcaste's stroke across the wrist, a mark a person can be born under.

## Hall and Temple, King and Sabhā

In the janapadas authority is one institution wearing several hats. The temple is the school, the record office, the granary and the bank. The sabhā in its hall, convened by the senior priest, is the assembly; the priest mediates, a court of the temple hears the dispute, and a _dandanāyaka_ enforces the judgment. A channel that breaks below a field the week before planting goes to the temple clerk, who finds the channel on the survey and the share of water the field is owed, and to the next sitting of the householders of the three villages that drink from it. The arrangement is slow, public and very hard to overturn.

A kingdom is two institutions: a Mahārāja with a martial council of clan-chieftains, and a priestly court of ushtakas, and the two can disagree about who is to be acclaimed. The Widow's Acclamation is the precedent each side quotes: when a Mahārāja died in the open season with his son a child, the Martial Council refused the Court's candidate and acclaimed his widow Jvalasundarī, who reigned until her son was of age. A city-state is governed by a council of merchant houses and temple patrons.

Where the answers conflict, the record decides. A claim is heard where the temple's survey, the boundary register or the genealogy names a hearer, and the authority with no document in the matter has no standing in it. A court states which year-count it follows before it fixes a coronation or a Weighing. Nobody collects between the janapadas except by consensus; the Mahā-Sangha convenes and does not govern, and the Mela's consensus is the whole of the confederation's coercion.

## What a Person Owes

Asked what a person owes, a Vedyari answers in the order the debts fall due: the household first, then the kula, then the village and its sabhā, then the temple, and the king last and only for what he has actually guaranteed. A station's duties are owed whether or not the station is comfortable, and doing them badly is worse than doing them unwillingly.

In return the household owes its members the hearth, the lamp and a place in the kula's account. The kula owes its members its elders' judgment and a marriage within the lineage. The village owes a Karmāja use-rights, a turn in the sabhā and the well; the temple owes the household its record, its granary in a lean year, the school and the court; the janapada owes its villages the water they are entitled to; the king owes only what he has guaranteed, and the plateau's wells show what the lineages do when he does not. The Mela owes the janapadas a place to be heard.

Revolt against the order is rare to the point of curiosity. Reform inside it is constant, loud, and conducted mostly by Ritūjas.

## The Weak

Care is the household's first, the kula's second and the temple's third. The temple holds the janapada's famine store, and the sabhā draws on it in a lean year. In Suvarnagiri one quarter of everything the mountain yields goes into the common share against famine, irrigation works and whatever else the sabhā authorizes; at Rājapur a modest endowment from centuries of pilgrim donations pays for famine relief, and the dry western terraces of Suvarnagiri put millet in the common store for every village's lean years. At the Mahājaya temple of Bhāranya a pilgrim is fed from the granary for three days, and the limit is enforced at the gate by a man with a tally. A guest is fed before he is questioned, in every household. A household that turns a traveler away at nightfall is talked about, and one that turns away an ascetic is talked about for a generation. A widow, _nushā_, keeps the house she married into until an heir, _hetha_, is of age; an orphan is a kula's business before it is the temple's. An ascetic is received with reverence wherever he goes and kept by the devout.

The limit is the wrist. The Bonded Servant is kept by the household while the bond holds, and the bond can be bought out. The Outcaste is nobody's by the rule: barred from the well, the court and the temple, and kept alive on the decks, the roads and the high country, where nobody asks to see a wrist. A runaway who reaches the sea or the pass-kingdoms is not returned.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who is injured or too frail to work stays in the household that holds the use-right, and the household feeds him from its own portion; the station is the household's to keep, and the household does not lose it because one member cannot work it. The old are _sthavira_, elders, and the lineage-elders of a janapada sit in its sabhā. When the harvest fails, the common store carries every household: the quarter-share in Suvarnagiri, the millet from the dry western terraces, the endowment at Rājapur. A household in a lean year pays its share in what it has, labor from the Karmāja and coin from the Dhanāja.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household's and the temple's. A guest is fed before he is questioned, and the pilgrim roads have temple granaries that feed a pilgrim for a fixed number of days. When a family cannot keep its young, the kula takes them.

Teaching is the temple's. The temple is the school, the schools of the Vyālendravāda are tied to the great academies and the building trades, and the academies of Dhanurkota train archers for every kingdom. A merchant's clerk learns the running hand and no classical, and is not called educated.

Defense is the janapada's own militia, raised from its villages at need, with Senāja households as its core and Karmāja households for the numbers. A janapada that cannot answer a serious threat seeks a patron, a city-state or a regional kingdom, and sends grain, labor and sometimes auxiliary troops in exchange for the protection of its standing forces. The confederation has raised an army three times; the last time it put an invader back out through the door he came in by and then dissolved, leaving a garrison at the Sangha-fort manned by rotation.

## What the Order Does Not See

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Vedyari's is the person the order has no seat for. The teaching holds that a station is a road out of itself, in the next life. The Outcaste, the one born outside the order, has no station to practice and no road to walk. Thoughtful Vedyari dispute whether a teaching that makes every duty a road can leave the born-outside's duty without one.

The sin is the unburned dead. A king at the ford of Olūratarana forbade the pyre for the villages that came down to be heard, and the ground stands cursed to this day. The orthodox temples did not light it afterward. When the Agnī-panthā's founder spent a season burning the ford, the temples held that the unburned dead were Kālavrata's affair, and the cities put his priests out. Some centuries later the kings of the Hundred Crowns forbade the sect to touch Gajasthalī, and three capitals banned its priests for going anyway. The ground is a recurrence still, and the villages along its foot have built its bones into well-heads and walls. A people whose whole rite is to see the dead off within the day left a field of dead standing in the doorway for centuries, and the orthodox account is that it was never theirs to light.
