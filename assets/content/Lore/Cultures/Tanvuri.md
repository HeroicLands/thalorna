Tānvür holds that the cosmos was made by sorting, that the sorting is incomplete, and that the work of holding the sorted world apart from the unsorted one is the business of every living person. Everything else about the Tānvüri follows from that sentence, including the parts a foreigner finds cruel. A Tānvüri does not experience his caste as an injustice or as a blessing—he experiences it as his classification, in the same way a comet has one, and the ministry that records his is a branch of the ministry that records the comet's.

## The Eight

Society runs in eight castes, which dictate profession, legal standing, dress, marriage, and the name—if any—by which a person may be known.

Two features of that list unsettle every foreigner who reads it, and a Tānvüri explains both without heat.

The sorcerers rank second and they are property. The Vëndük stand above generals and scholars and are chattel, bound to the houses they serve, and this is not a contradiction: their power is exactly why they are ranked high and exactly why they are owned. An unbound sorcerer is not a free man in Tānvüri thinking—he is an unclassified thing, which is the worst category there is.

The merchants rank seventh, below the peasants. A farmer feeds the empire and a merchant moves what other men made, so the Shükrën are frequently far wealthier than the Zāthük above them, and the wealth buys comfort and not one inch of standing. Foreign traders find this maddening, and Tānvüri officials find foreign traders' indignation instructive.

## Classification Is a Sacred Act

The state religion is a Celestial Bureaucracy, and the earthly government is modeled on it and not the other way round. The Celestial Dragon is the First Classifier—the principle of order itself—and beneath it the Jade Sovereign, the Minister of Storms, the Minister of the Moon and the Five Elemental Lords administer their domains as ministries administer provinces.

A Tānvüri clerk filing a return is therefore not doing paperwork that happens to carry religious cover. He is performing a small act of the same kind the Dragon performs, and doing it badly is not inefficiency but impiety. The ministries that track the movements of comets also track the ritual purity of provincial officials, and no Tānvüri sees the two tasks as different tasks.

Against all of it stand the Infernal Realms—the zones where the Great Sorting was never finished, and where the Unclassified presses on the borders of the sorted world. Imperial ritual, the labor of the bound sorcerers, and the moral discipline of the entire population are understood as one continuous custodianship of that border. Let it go and the cosmos does not fall or burn; it dissolves, back into the undifferentiated thing it was sorted out of. That is the Tānvüri nightmare, and it is why a people this rigid are not, in their own estimation, being rigid at all. They are holding a line.

## The Ledger

Ordinary spiritual life is ancestor worship, and it works as a ledger: merit and default are recorded, carried and owed, a descendant inherits both, and the rites are the instrument by which the account is serviced.

This makes the Tānvüri unusually concrete about morality. A man does not ask whether an act was good; he asks what it entered on the ledger and who will be paying it. A family that neglects the rites is not impious in the abstract—it is defaulting, and the creditors are its own dead. It also makes descent the most important fact about anyone, and a Tānvüri therefore introduces himself by lineage and caste, in that order, and why the introduction is legally operative.

## Names, and Who May Have One

A name in Tānvür is a privilege attached to classification and not a possession. The eighth caste may not hold one in the ordinary way, and a person whose caste is stripped loses the name with it.

The practical effect is that a Tānvüri's name is a piece of public documentation: it states his caste, his lineage and often his generation. Foreigners who ask a Tānvüri his name and receive a long formal answer have not been given a courtesy. They have been handed a record.

## Manner

The Tānvüri manner is formal, indirect and exact. Precedence is worked out before a conversation begins and governs everything in it, and a foreigner who cannot be placed in the eight castes is placed provisionally somewhere low and treated with a courtesy that is real and entirely without deference.

Frankness is not valued. A direct refusal embarrasses both parties by forcing a classification into the open before it has been agreed, and a Tānvüri will go to considerable lengths to decline without saying no. Ankarians read this as evasiveness; it is closer to the opposite—an unwillingness to assert a thing until it has been properly established.

Omens are read professionally and taken seriously in practice. A Tānthëi's reading can postpone a campaign, and nobody involved regards this as superstition: the omen is a datum about the state of the classification, and acting against it is acting on bad information.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Tānvüri what a person owes, and he answers in the order the ledger falls due: the ancestors first, then the lineage, then the caste whose duties define what he may and must do, then the Emperor whose Mandate holds the classification together.

Beneath all four he owes the sorting itself—the small daily correctness of doing what his classification requires, in the form it requires, at the time it requires. He is not asked to believe that this is pleasant. He is asked to understand that the alternative is dissolution, and on that point the Tānvüri are entirely serious.
