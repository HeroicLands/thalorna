The Cedar People live on the western coast of the north, between the sea and a wall of snow peaks, and everything they are follows from a coast so rich that wealth piles up faster than anyone can use it. They call themselves the [[affiliation-kelwanuk|Kelwanuk]], the people of the cedar, after the tree that gives them their houses, their canoes, their boxes and their clothes. About 200,000 of them live in villages of cedar-plank houses facing the beaches, every house a lineage with its own names, its own crests and its own fishing places. Each year the salmon come up their rivers in runs so great that a village dries a year's food in a few weeks, and the rest of the year goes to what the Cedar People care about most: giving that wealth away, in public, to prove who they are. The south calls them _Ish'bal Ki'ahk_, after the eagles carved on the poles of [[place-tsilkasis|Tsilkasis]], Eagle Beach, where its traders first met them.

## Where They Came From

The Cedar People say that in the first days the sea rose over the whole land. The first people tied their canoes to the highest peaks and waited, and the time they waited is called the [Great Water]{.name data-type="lore/history"}. When the sea fell, beings of the sea and the sky came down to the empty beaches, took off their masks of feathers or fin, and became the first chiefs. The first [Tsilkas]{.name data-type="being/npc"} came down as an eagle to the beach that bears his name, and an orca walked out of the sea at [[place-malkisis|Malkisis]], Orca Beach. Every house descends from one of those beings, and its crest (_kwemla_) is the right to carve, wear and dance the being its first ancestor met.

The first people were cold that first winter, and naked, and they had nothing to build with. A woman of the first houses, the [Cedar Woman]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, walked into the forest and stood there until she became a tree. Her hair became the bark the people weave, her bones the planks of their houses, and her arms the hulls of their canoes, and she asked nothing back. She is the first cedar (_kelwa_), and her gift is the reason the Cedar People hold giving to be the first good. A carver who cuts a cedar still speaks to it first, tells it what it will become and asks it to give.

The rest of their history is the history of the houses, rising and falling by what they give. A house that gives the greatest feasts climbs the order of seats; a house that cannot answer the gifts it receives falls. The [Eagle House]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} of Tsilkasis, the _Tsilkasanuk_, has sat first on the coast for four generations of chiefs, and its chief holds the first seat (_kenwatukwa_). The worst thing in that history is the [Killing Feast]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, told below.

## The Gift Is Everything

**Good is the gift given before witnesses and the gift answered with a greater one. Evil is taking without return: the hoard, the theft, the debt left unpaid, and wealth kept out of sight.** The Cedar People hold that wealth exists only when it is given. A house that piles blankets in its boxes and never feasts has nothing, because nobody has seen it give, and a chief who hoards is spoken of as poor however full his boxes are.

This belief governs manners down to the smallest. A guest is fed before anything is said, and fed more than he can eat, and he takes the rest home. A child learns to share a catch before it learns to count it. A gift is never refused, because refusing a gift is refusing the giver's claim, and that is an insult answered with a greater one. A person who receives a gift owes it back with interest, at a feast of their own, and a debt (_humkwa_) is not a shameful thing so long as it is answered in time.

Wrongs are judged by the same measure. Theft is the worst of the ordinary crimes, worse than a blow in anger, because it is taking that nobody sees and nobody can answer. A killing is answered with a payment from the killer's house to the dead person's, in blankets and coppers, and the payment wipes away the shame of both houses. A killing left unpaid is answered with another.

## Standing on the Coast

Success is a name with a high seat, made good at a feast and remembered by witnesses. Every house owns a set of names (_yekwa_), and every name carries a seat (_tukwa_) at the feasts of the coast, an order that runs from the first seat of the first house to the last seat of the least. A person climbs by claiming a higher name and making the claim good: giving a feast, giving away enough to make the guests accept it, and paying witnesses to remember that they did. The order of the ranks is plain. A slave (_helayekwa_) has no name. A commoner (_temlamis_) is of a house but holds none of its names. A noble (_yekwamis_) holds one or more. A house chief (_hemkis_) holds the highest name of a house.

Failure is shame (_tsapis_) left unanswered. A person shamed in public, mocked at a feast, refused a seat or caught in a lie loses face (_saiwat_), and only a gift wipes it away: the shamed person gives blankets to everyone who saw, and the shame goes with them. A person who cannot pay to wipe it away carries it. A house that cannot answer the gifts it has received falls down the order of seats, and its chief may give up his name to a richer kinsman rather than see it fall further.

The Cedar People value most the names, the gift, the cedar, the salmon, the copper, a long memory and a steady face. They value least the hoard, the secret, haste, and a raised voice, because a chief who must shout to be heard has a Speaker who has failed him. They also hold work done without a name in low regard, and that contempt costs them, as told below.

## The Man Admired

[Kwemlatsuma]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Raven House is the master carver (_tsumamis_) of Tsilkasis, and the villages of the coast name him when they say what a man should be. He is a commoner. He has no seat at any feast, and he has carved the house posts and crest poles of half the noble houses of the northern fjords. He chooses his cedar in winter, walks to it, tells it what it will be and asks it to give, and he will not cut a tree that stands too near a stream, because the salmon need its shade. A pole of his takes a year, and he has never delivered one late. When he was young, a chief ordered a pole in a hurry for a feast, and Kwemlatsuma told him that the pole would take a year or the chief could find another carver. The chief waited and moved the feast.

The coast admires three things in him: the patience of the cedar, the keeping of his word, and the skill in his hands, which the coast holds to be a gift from a power of the forest. His cost is plain to everyone. His poles carry the crests of other men's houses and stand before other men's doors, and the names they honor are never his. When he dies his own name, a commoner's name, is lifted at no feast.

## The Woman Admired

[Kalunukwas]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Orca House is the Keeper of the Count (_kalumis_) at Malkisis, and the women of the coast name her when they say what a woman should be. She is the seventh to hold the name. She holds in memory every gift the Orca House has given and received for forty years: who gave what, at which feast, before which witnesses, and what is still owed. At every feast she stands behind her chief, and when a gift is given she says its count aloud. When two houses disagree about an old debt, they send for her.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of Kwemlatsuma's. He makes the things a house gives; she keeps the truth of what was given, and without her the gifts mean nothing. Three winters ago her own chief wanted to give a feast to shame a rival, and she told him before his whole house that the count could not pay it, and the house would be in debt for ten years. He did not give it. Her cost is that people fear her. She can name any house's unpaid debts to its face, and few chiefs on the coast want her at their feasts.

## What the Houses Keep

The Cedar People write nothing down, and what holds them together is carved, owned or remembered.

- **The names** are the first of their artifacts. A house's names, in order, with their seats, crests and rights, are its charter, and its Speaker can recite them all.
- **The crest poles** (_kwemlagelas_) stand before the house doors and carry the crests of the house, one above another. The house posts inside hold up the roof beams and carry the beings of the house's histories. A pole is raised at a feast and never repaired: when it falls, it is left to rot, and a new one is raised at a new feast.
- **The coppers** (_lukwa_) are beaten sheets shaped like shields, each with its own name and its own history of the feasts at which it changed hands. A copper's worth rises every time it is given. A chief who breaks a copper, cutting off a piece and giving it to a rival, has destroyed a fortune in front of witnesses, and the rival must break one of equal worth or lose face.
- **The bentwood boxes** (_tukala_) hold a house's masks, robes, rattles and whistles. A box is steamed and bent from a single plank, so tight that it holds water.
- **The count** is kept in the memory of each house's Keeper of the Count, and the witnesses (_nilkwamis_) paid at every feast hold the rest. A claim no witness remembers was never made.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the [[lore-nakpulalas|Meeting]], the faith of the coast, is courtesy to the things that feed the house. A woman stripping cedar bark in spring takes only a strip from each tree and thanks the tree as she pulls it. The bones of the first salmon of each run go back into the river whole, so that the salmon can rise again and return next year. Every evening, before the house eats, a little of the meal goes into the fire for the dead. Every morning before dawn, young men and the men who hunt sea game bathe in the sea and scrub themselves with hemlock branches until their skin is raw, for strength and to be clean before the powers of the sea.

Nobody says the name of a person who has died until an heir has lifted it, because a cold name (_tsulayekwa_) spoken aloud calls its dead owner back hungry. People who must speak of the dead say "the one who held the name" and let the listener work it out.

## The Round of the Year

The year has two halves, the summer of fishing and work and the winter of feasts and dances, and the order of the year is set by the rivers.

- **The Welcoming** comes when the first salmon of the year enters the river. It is laid on a new cedar mat, sprinkled with eagle down, sung to, and eaten by the whole village in one sitting, and its bones go back into the river. It is for keeping the salmon coming. A village that insults the salmon, by wasting them or by throwing their bones on the midden, loses the run.
- **The salmon run** (_tsamalala_) fills late summer. The whole village works at the weirs and the drying racks for weeks, and nobody gives a feast while the fish are running. It is for food.
- **The giving feasts** (_kitala_) follow the run, from autumn until the winter dances begin. A chief who takes a new name, raises a pole, marries his daughter, or lifts the name of a dead predecessor invites the other houses, feeds them for days, and gives away or destroys a fortune in blankets, canoes, grease and copper while his Keeper of the Count counts it aloud. It is for making claims good.
- **The winter dances** (_hilamala_) fill the dark months. The houses put away their summer names and take their winter names, and the dancers come out in masks to act the meetings of the first ancestors with the powers. It is for showing the crests their meaning, and for bringing back the Wild Ones.
- **The eulachon run** comes at the end of winter, when the small oil fish (_hulis_) crowd the river mouths. The village rakes them out by the canoe-load and renders them into grease (_melum_) in pits and boxes. The stink carries for miles. It is for the grease that every feast must give.

## Praise, Shame and the Nameless

Reward is a seat raised and a name given. A child who learns well is given a better small name at a feast its parents pay for. A young man who brings back a whale or a captive canoe is given a name from his house's store, and a chief may give a commoner who has served the house well a minor name, raising him into the nobility. A person the coast honors is seated higher at the next feast, served first, and given the first gift.

Punishment runs from mockery to the loss of the name. The first is mockery at a feast: a host's song names a guest's unpaid debt or a past cowardice, and the whole house hears it. The second is a lower seat, given by a host who does not think a guest's name has been made good. The third is payment: a person who has shamed another, or been shamed, gives blankets until the shame is wiped. The fourth is the loss of a name, which the house takes back and gives to someone else. The last is being put out of the house: a person without a house has no name, and a person without a name belongs to nobody and can be killed or enslaved by anyone.

## Summer Names and Winter Names

The Cedar People have one institution, the house, and it wears two hats by season. In summer the houses are ruled by their names: the chief (_hemkis_) holds the highest name, decides when the village fishes and where, takes a share of every catch, and gives it back at his feasts. In winter the order of the names is put away, and the dance societies rule. Their heads are often commoners, and a chief who has no dance place sits at the back.

There is no rule above the house. The chief who holds the first seat speaks first at any council of chiefs, is served first, and leads when the houses go to war together, and that is the whole of his authority. A house chief follows him while his feasts are the greatest on the coast, and stops when they are not.

Cases are settled between houses. When a person of one house wrongs a person of another, the two chiefs talk through their Speakers (_talwamis_) until they agree a payment. The Keeper of the Count of each house remembers the agreement, and the witnesses at the next feast hear it announced. Nobody collects a tax: the chief collects from his own house's catch and work, and pays it out again in gifts. When two answers conflict, the feast decides. A claim the guests accept, by taking the host's gifts, is true, and a claim they refuse, by leaving the gifts on the floor, is not.

## Names Do Not Die

A person who dies goes to the village of the dead, which lies at the bottom of the sea off the end of the world, and lives there as on the coast, in houses, by fires. But a dead person has no name there until an heir on the coast lifts it. A cold name (_tsulayekwa_) leaves its dead owner hungry and nameless, and the hungry dead come back in dreams to ask for food, and sometimes bring sickness when they are refused.

That belief settles an ordinary day more than any law does. Every evening a little food goes into the fire, and the smoke feeds the dead. A dying person names, aloud, before witnesses, who will lift their name, and often gives away their own property before they die so that their heir has the wealth for the feast. An heir saves for years to lift a name, because the feast that lifts it is also the feast that makes the heir its holder. Inheritance follows the names, not the persons: whoever lifts a name takes its seat, its crests, its fishing places and its debts. An unpaid debt of the dead is a debt of the name, and its new holder must pay it at the feast where he takes it.

The dead are laid in a cedar box with their robes and tools, and the box is set in a small grave house above the beach, or, for a great chief, on top of a carved mortuary pole. A family spends on the lifting feast before it spends on anything else.

## Feeding the Dead

The living reach the dead through fire. Food, grease and blankets burned in the hearth reach them as smoke, and a house burns more in the months after a death. The great reach is the lifting feast, which gives the dead person a name in the village of the dead and gives the heir the name on the coast.

The dead reach the living through dreams and sickness. A healer (_hemnis_), a person whom a power of the forest or the sea has taught, goes into a trance, travels to the village of the dead and asks what the dead person wants. The healer charges blankets, and charges more for a chief. The great cost is the feast: lifting a chief's name costs a house the wealth of a year or more, and the commoners of the house pay most of it in work.

## What a Person Owes

The Cedar People state what a person owes in order: an answer to every gift, with interest; then a share of every catch and every day's work to the house; then food in the fire for the dead; then respect to the salmon and the cedar; then the lifting of the names that come to them. A noble owes feasts. A chief owes the whole house its food in a hard winter.

In return a person is owed what the house gives. A member of a house is owed a place by its fire, food when the house has food, a share of the gifts at every feast the house attends, a small name at least, a seat for whatever name they hold, burial in a cedar box, and the lifting of their name after death. A slave is owed food and nothing else.

## The Weak

The house is the first and last resort. A widow keeps her own names and her own property, which never belonged to her husband, and stays in his house or goes back to her own with her children as she chooses. An orphan belongs to the house of either parent, and the house that takes the child in gives it a small name and raises it with the rest. A person who has lost everything at feasts goes back to their house's fire and is fed there.

The chief's duty to feed his house in a bad winter is a duty, not a kindness, because the face of the name depends on it. A chief whose house goes hungry while he saves for a feast loses face, and his own people leave him for a kinsman's house. Nobody of a house is homeless. A stranger who reaches a village is fed, and then asked what house he belongs to. A stranger with no house on the coast and no pipe of the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] has no name and is anyone's to take.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot fish, haul or paddle is kept by the house and finds work that the house needs. A hunter crippled by a sea lion mends nets and makes harpoon heads. A woman whose hands are too stiff to weave teaches her granddaughters to weave, and keeps the songs. A blind elder recites the names and the histories of the house, which are learned by ear. The house's Speaker often takes as his apprentice a youth who cannot paddle, because the work is to remember.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the house. Teaching is the house's work: a child is taught to work by its parents, taught its names and histories by its grandparents, taught to handle a canoe and a harpoon by its uncles, and taught a craft by apprenticeship to a master of it, such as a carver, a weaver or a canoe-maker. Defense is the village. The houses of a village stand together on the beach, and most villages keep a refuge on a steep headland nearby, walled with logs, with water and dried food stored inside, where the people go when raiders come.

The Cedar People fight with clubs of whalebone and stone, copper daggers, spears and bows, and they wear armor of wooden slats and elk hide, with carved wooden helmets. Their war canoes (_yakwis_), seagoing dugouts each cut from a single cedar, carry crews along the coast to raid other villages in feuds and to take slaves on distant coasts, most of them to the south among the [[lore-shuqashclt|Shell-Bead People]], whom the Cedar People also court as trading partners. Neither side forgets either fact. The Cedar People have no iron and have never faced southern steel, which they know of only from what the Turquoise People's traders say. They keep the compact a month's journey away for the prestige of a seat at its council. A seat is something the Cedar People understand.

## The House

The household is the house (_temla_): one great plank building, and the lineage of thirty to eighty people who live in it. Each family has its own fire along the walls, and the chief's family has the back of the house, farthest from the door, where the seat of honor is. Nobles, commoners and slaves all live under one roof, nobles nearest the back and slaves nearest the door.

Membership comes from either parent. A child can claim the names of its father's house or its mother's, and often claims names from both over a lifetime, and a person may live in one house and hold names in another. Marriage moves names: a bride's family gives the groom's family names, crests and coppers to hold for the children of the marriage, and the groom's family pays a bride price that the bride's family must later repay with interest. A house can adopt a child or an adult and give them a name.

The age ladder is plain. A child is given a small name at its first year, carried everywhere, and set to fetching water and picking berries as soon as it can walk. A young person takes a second name at a feast their parents give; a girl at her first blood spends a season apart in a small house, learning from her grandmothers, and comes out to a feast and a woman's name; a boy bathes in the winter sea and learns the canoe. Adults claim names, marry, fish, weave, carve, raid and feast. The old give their seats away while they are alive, to an heir at a feast, and keep the honor of having held them; an old chief who hands on his name in time is honored more than one who dies in it. The dying name their heir aloud. The dead wait, cold, for their names to be lifted.

## The Killing Feast and the Nameless

The sin the Cedar People committed is the Killing Feast. The first [Lukwahakwa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, chief of the Eagle House, and [Kwemulkita]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, chief of the Orca House, were rivals (_lalkis_), and each feast between them was greater than the last. At a feast at Tsilkasis, after giving away every blanket in his boxes and breaking two coppers, Lukwahakwa had twelve of his slaves led onto the beach and clubbed to death before the guests, to show that his house could lose twelve slaves and not notice. The next winter, at Malkisis, Kwemulkita killed twenty.

The Eagle House was preparing its answer when the first Kalunukwas, Keeper of the Count of the Orca House, stood up at the next feast and refused to count the dead. A gift, she said, is a thing someone receives, and a dead man is given to nobody. Nobody received the twenty, so nothing was given, and nothing is owed. The chiefs of the coast accepted her count, because the count is the only record the coast has, and since then a killing at a feast earns no seat and no answer. The ruling ended the killing feasts. It did not stop the Eagle House's rise: the house has sat first on the coast for four generations of chiefs, and the chief who holds the first seat today is the ninth Lukwahakwa. The Cedar People hold that a person is their name, and they argue among themselves whether the name carries what its first holder did.

The blind spot is still there, and thoughtful Kelwanuk argue over it. The coast holds that a person is a name, and a slave has none, so a slave is property and the child of a slave is born nameless under the house roof, never captured in any raid and never a person. Those who argue against it point to the first Kalunukwas: if a dead slave was given to nobody, then a living slave is somebody. A few houses have begun to free slaves at their feasts, giving a slave his freedom as a gift before witnesses, and the coast cannot agree who receives that gift. Some chiefs refuse to count it.

A second argument runs alongside it. The commoners of a house do the fishing, the rendering, the weaving and the carving that make a feast's gifts, and the chief gives them away. A chief who ruins his house in a rivalry ruins his commoners first, and the commoners hold no names and have no say. Kwemlatsuma's poles stand before other men's doors, and some of the coast's commoners say that this is the whole of the arrangement.
