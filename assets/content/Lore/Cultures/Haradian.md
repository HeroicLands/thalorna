Haradian culture is mercantile to the bone. Wealth, reputation, and the ability to strike a profitable bargain are the virtues, named in that order, and a man good at all three is the nearest thing this coast has to an aristocrat.

Twelve years ago the Haradians won their independence from the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian Empire]] by force of arms, and the [[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Confederation of Haradian States]] is what they made of it. The veterans of that war are alive, in their forties, and watching the merchant oligarchs who funded it hold the freedoms they bled for. Everything uncomfortable about Harad today sits in that sentence.

## The Houses

The great trading houses are the real power: sprawling family enterprises combining banking, shipping, warehousing and political influence in a single concern, with warehouses on every harbor, agents in every port on the Vylarian Sea, and reach into the courts of Vylaria, [[place-provenzrgn|Provènzia]] and [[place-helionis|Heliónis]].

A house is a family and a firm at once, and a Haradian does not separate the two. A marriage is a merger, a son is an apprentice and an heir and a hostage to the alliance his betrothal sealed, and a daughter who can read a ledger is worth more to her house than a son who cannot. The houses have been unsentimental about that long enough that women captain ships and hold partnerships without anyone remarking on it.

Above the houses sits the [[affiliation-auricompct|Auric Compact]], the banking cartel, and every Haradian knows that a confederation with a magnificent navy and a borrowed treasury is a confederation that does what its creditors prefer.

## Three Harads

The guild elite live in palatial urban estates, patronize the arts, and school their children in rhetoric, mathematics and mercantile law. They are cultivated, cosmopolitan, and entirely clear-eyed about what their money is for.

The middle—independent merchants, skilled craftspeople and ship captains—is large and is the actual economic backbone. A Haradian captain owning his own hull is the characteristic figure of the region: proud, indebted, competent, and one bad season from working for a house.

The harbor districts are cramped, poor and increasingly restless. Dock workers and sailors carried the war, and a decade of being told that liberty has been achieved has not improved their temper.

Apart from all three stand the inland tribes, culturally distinct, speaking archaic dialects, keeping older traditions, and resenting the coast. They supplied a great many of the foot soldiers for independence and have seen almost none of the benefit, and coastal Haradians tend to forget they exist until a levy is needed.

## The Bargain as a Social Form

Haggling is not rudeness here, and refusing to haggle is. A price offered and accepted without discussion insults both parties—the seller because his opening was not taken seriously, the buyer because he has been treated as someone not worth the time.

The negotiation is also how relationships are formed. A Haradian learns what kind of man he is dealing with over an hour of tea and argument, and he would sooner trade at a worse rate with someone he has read than a better one with a stranger. Foreigners who send a factor with fixed terms and no authority to move are regarded as having declined to meet.

Hospitality is lavish and is a display of credit. A house that feeds forty people well is telling the harbor something specific about its liquidity.

## Reputation and the Long Ledger

A Haradian's credit is his name, literally: the houses lend on reputation across distances where no writ runs, and a merchant who defaults has lost money and made himself unfinanceable from [[place-miravel|Míravel]] to Heliónis.

So the coast is scrupulous about debt in a way that surprises people who expect merchants to be sharp. Sharpness is admired in a negotiation and despised in a settlement. The Haradian view is that a bargain hard-driven is a bargain, while a bargain not honored is theft, and that distinction is the whole basis of the trade.

## Faith, Lightly Worn

The [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aurèldían Pantheon]] is kept, with the prosperity and voyage gods drawing the real devotion and a great deal of sincere private prayer before a sailing. Temples are endowed by houses, and the endowments are publicized.

Haradians are not much interested in doctrine and are extremely interested in omens, luck and the specific patronage of the god who governs their particular sea route. A foreign priest arriving with theology finds the ports polite and inattentive; one arriving with a reputation for effective blessings finds them generous.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Haradian what a person owes, and he answers: the house first—his family and his firm in one breath, because they are one thing—then his partners, then his crew, then the city whose harbor he uses.

He owes his creditors payment and his debtors patience, and by the coast's reckoning the second is the harder discipline and the more profitable one. He does not put the Confederation high on the list: it is twelve years old, it is run by people he can name, and a Haradian reserves loyalty for institutions that have outlasted him.
