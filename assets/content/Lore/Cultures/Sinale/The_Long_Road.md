**The Long Road**, _Tirvali_, is a tale (_ennedhi_) the [[lore-flksinale|Sinalë]] who hold office in [[affiliation-tanvurempr|Tānvür]] tell one another, and they tell it on one occasion. Under the old arrangement between the imperial court and the forest enclaves, which neither side explains and both keep, the [[lore-tanthei|Tānthëi]] hall of a western prefecture holds one or two Sinalë scholars at a time, and on the evening a new one is entered on the hall's roll the eldest Sinalo present tells the newest this story, after the entry is made and before the classification tablet is issued, in a plain voice and with no point drawn from it. The title is the clerk's entry: three signs of a four-syllable name, which in Sinalë is the word for a long road. The tellers say the tale is funny and tell it as a funny story to the end.

It is the one Sinalë story that reached human hands without being given. A gloss in the [[skill-clthndscrpt|Veldirno]] hand stands against a single line of one prefecture's planting roll, written there by a Sinalë scholar of that hall long after the line itself, and the empire re-files its rolls at every change of era and a copyist copies what stands on the page, so the gloss has gone up with its roll to the Warlord's archive and on to the chancellery in the hands of copyists who cannot read it. The rendering below is from the gloss, which is the tale written down, with what the tellers add in speech kept where they add it.

Under the arrangement, an enclave owes a prefecture a scholar. The enclave above the prefecture of this tale had sent one down for as long as the prefecture had kept a roll to enter one on, and when the scholar before went back up into the forest the enclave sent **Tirvalira Solmerinto**. Her hearth was the hearth of the smell of rain. Her given name was the long road, with the ending that makes a word a woman's name. She had watched the sky for longer than the prefecture had stood, and she had never in her life been written down, because her people keep their names in mouths and an enclave has no roll.

The hall had held Sinalë before her, one at a time, back past the oldest copy of its roll, and every one of them had gone onto the page without trouble. The scholar before her had carried a given name of three syllables, as it happened, and the three cells had taken it as if they had been cut for it. Nobody in the hall had ever had reason to count the cells.

The hall of the Tānthëi in that prefecture kept the prefecture's part of the [[lore-castelaws|Great Person Record]], the roll on which every person in the empire stands, from the Son of Heaven to the slave who carries his owner's name on a tablet. The clerk who held the brush at the roll that year was the second son of the keeper of the roll. He was nineteen. He had held the brush for one year, and in that year the roll had not been wrong once, and he had the manner the roll gives to the people who keep it, which is the manner of someone who has never needed to raise his voice because the page is on his side. The Sinalë who tell this do not give his designation, which ran to eleven characters and stated his clan, his father's office and his district, and they do not shorten it either. They say "the clerk," which is not a name, and so breaks no rule.

She came down in the autumn with the Warlord's letter, which classified her as Tānthëi because the enclave had sent a scholar and a scholar is what the fourth caste is for. The hall stood at the top of the prefecture town, a court of gray tile with the records store along its east side and a flat roof above the store where the charts were kept under oiled cloth, and she was brought to the roll before she was brought to the roof. The clerk read the letter twice, set it in its place and opened the roll. Those who tell this like to give the columns, and they give them in the roll's own order: the caste, in one character; the clan, in as many characters as the clan takes; the household; the district; the sex; the name, if the person has one, in three cells; the meaning of the name; the year of entry; the hand that entered it; and the events, which for most people stay empty all their lives. A Sinalo has a name cell filled where most of the fourth caste have none, because the Tānthëi teach that the Elder Races were sorted at the Great Sorting with the names they already carried, so the roll enters the name an enclave gives. The three cells are cut to the empire's ordinary name, which runs to two elements or three; a granted name of honor runs to four signs and is written on the roll of honors, since four is an unsorted number and a filing of consequence does not carry it.

The caste he wrote from the letter. The clan he asked for.

"Solmerinto," she said, and she gave him the gloss: the hearth of the smell of rain. He wrote the sound of it in the clan column and the meaning of it in the meaning column, and found room for both, since clans in the empire carry long names and the column is cut for them. The household was the enclave, which the roll called by the name of the nearest village, having no other name for it. The district was the district. The sex he marked.

Then the name.

"Tirvalira," she said.

He wrote _Tir_, and _va_, and _li_. The fourth cell was not there. He looked at the name he had written and at the woman who had said it, and he asked what the fourth syllable said.

"That I am a woman."

He turned the roll so that she could see the column where that was already marked. "The roll has a column for it," he said. "A record does not say a thing twice, and a name is not written in four."

She looked at the three cells for some time. The Sinalë who tell this give the pause its length, and then they give her line, which is the line the tale is told for.

"Then you have registered a road."

He considered that. He asked what the three signs said, and she told him, because he had asked: _tirvali_, the long road. He wrote Long Road in the meaning column in the empire's own characters, laid the brush down, and said, "Now the roll knows what it holds."

"It holds a road."

"It holds a Tānthëi of the hearth of the smell of rain, from the enclave above the village, a woman, entered this year by this hand, whose name means the long road. The roll is not confused."

And the roll was not. The tellers stop here and say so, and they say the rest of it plainly, since both rules are real. In the empire a name is a piece of public documentation, a privilege attached to a classification, and a roll that already records a person's sex does not spend a cell saying it again; the clerk had entered what the form held and glossed it correctly, and the Caste-Registrar who read the entry that winter passed it without a mark. _Tirvali_ is a road. It was a road on the roll, and it was a road on the hardwood tablet the hall cut for her the next morning, with her caste and her clan and the magistrate's seal in lacquer on the back, and she carried that tablet in her sleeve for forty years, a long road with a seal on it, and showed it at every gate.

The roll was not confused. The index was. The index clerks of a hall read the meaning column, since a thing is filed by what it is, and the meaning column said Long Road, so everything that bore her entry went where roads go. Her first reading of the autumn sky, which gave the Warlord's office its date for the river levy, was indexed to the prefecture's Bureau of Passage and lay there between a petition about a washed-out ford and the account for gravel. In her first Tūryüt, the month of earth, when the roads are repaired and the inspector of roads goes through the file of what the prefecture holds in that kind, he came to her entry and, being exact, went and looked at her. He found her on the roof of the hall with the star-charts. He wrote what the file asked him to write, which was the condition of the thing and the work it needed: "Sound. No repair required this year." Some tellers give it as "Long; sound," and either is kept. The file carried her every Tūryüt after that, and whoever held the inspector's office that year came up to the roof and wrote the same line under the one before, so that her page in the roads file is a column of forty entries saying she needed no work. In her thirtieth year a new inspector, finding a Tānthëi of very great age on the roof, wrote "Long; sound; old," and the keeper of the roll sent it back, because old is not a condition the form admits for a road.

Her stipend was drawn once from the roads allocation, because the allocation clerk followed the index. The Bureau of Allocation queried it, as a bureau does, and the query came down to the hall, and the clerk answered it in the form a query is answered in: "The roll is correct as entered." It was, and the stipend was paid, from the roads, and in a year when a bridge had gone in the spring floods and the roads allocation was short, it was paid late, in Tūryüt with the gravel, the first time in the prefecture's memory that a Tānthëi's pay had waited on a bridge.

Her letters came down from the enclave written in the Veldirno, which nobody in the hall could read, addressed to Tirvalira Solmerinto. The sorting clerk, finding no Tirvalira on the roll and a Tirvali of the right clan, sent them where the index sent everything of hers, and she read her hearth's letters in the Bureau of Passage's room, among the surveys, which she did not mind, since it was the quietest room in the hall.

Word of the roll went up into the forest, as words do, and in Jīkyüt of her first year, when the inspector had been and gone, she went up after it. The tellers give the hearth scene short. Her mother's mother asked what the empire called her, and she took the tablet out of her sleeve and laid it on the hearthstone, caste up, and the hearth read the three syllables on it and said nothing for the length of time her people say nothing, and then laughed, and did what the Sinalë do with a thing worth keeping, which is to add it. Among her own people she is Tirvalira Sirvali, which is the long road twice over, the clerk's three syllables standing worn after her name as an epithet stands, and the hearth of the smell of rain has called her so ever since. The empire took a syllable off her and her people put three on, and the hearth was content, and the roll was unchanged.

She did not leave it unchanged for want of trying. On the first day of [[lore-clndrstrlgy|Jëityüt]], which is the new year, when the Emperor opens the celestial filing and every bureau in the empire settles its ledgers and takes in the year's petitions, she filed hers. She had learned the imperial hand in her first winter, because a Tānthëi reads the sacred texts and the sacred texts are in it, and she wrote the petition herself, in the same words every year, which the tellers give exactly:

"Tirvalira Solmerinto, entered on the roll of this hall in the clan of the hearth of the smell of rain under the hand of the second son of the keeper of the roll, is not a road. Let the fourth sign be entered."

The clerk read it, as he read every petition, and ruled on it in the same words every year, which the tellers also give exactly:

"The roll holds what the roll holds. The fourth sign states what the column of sex states. The record is correct as entered. Denied."

The hall came to count its year from it. Hers was the first petition filed in the prefecture on the first morning of Jëityüt, before the lanterns were down and before the Emperor's audit had reached the provinces, and the junior clerks of the records side came in from the festival to watch it filed and ruled on, and went back out to the feast. The tellers say that a Jëityüt on which the road had not petitioned would have felt to the hall like a year left unopened, and that no such Jëityüt came.

A denied petition is carried on the hall's books until Thürtyüt, the month of frost, when every filing in the empire is either processed or formally withdrawn, and hers was processed, and in the spring after, in Hötyüt, the month of fire, it was burned with the other denied petitions of the year, which the empire holds to be how a paper goes up to the archive above. The tellers draw one thing from this and no more: if the Tānvüri are right about where burned paper goes, the celestial archive holds her name whole forty times over, which is forty times more than the roll below, and the celestial clerks have not answered either.

Once, in her ninth year, she filed it differently. The petition that year came in from the road. "The road entered on the roll of this hall in the clan of the hearth of the smell of rain requests to be entered as a woman," it said, and the clerk read it, and ruled on it in new words, which the tellers keep beside the old ones: "The petitioner is a Tānthëi of the hearth of the smell of rain, entered under this hand. A road cannot petition. Denied." It was the one time in forty years the roll said in so many words that she was not a road, and it said so in order to refuse her. She filed in the old words the next year, and he denied her in the old words.

Those who hear the tale for the first time ask here why he did not add the syllable himself, and the tellers answer that he could not. The [[lore-zeisik|Heavenly Law]] makes an alteration to the roll without a granted petition an unauthorized reclassification, which is the crime the empire kills for, in the open, so that the lesson carries. The one lawful way to a fourth cell was a petition, and the petition's only ground was a ground the roll already answered in another column. Both of them understood this. Neither said so. She filed on the first day of Jëityüt and he denied her, and the year went on, and the stars did what she said they would.

For that was the other half of her office, and the tale does not forget it. She gave the hall its dates. She said when the river would be safe to levy on and when the passes would close, and once, when the bureau's chart showed a comet due and the chart was wrong by a season, she said the comet would be late, and it was, and when the Warlord's man asked how she knew she said, "I have seen it come the last three times," and the hall entered her counsel as received and unverifiable, as the hall enters any Sinalë counsel, and acted on it.

In her twelfth year the commandant of the Warlord's column came to the hall himself, in Thürkyüt, with the bureau's almanac under his arm, because the column was to go over the high pass with the winter's grain and the almanac gave the pass as open for a month yet and the hall's Tānthëi had given it as closing in ten days. He asked the keeper of the roll for the scholar who had given the date. The keeper opened the roll and read him the entry, caste, clan, household, district, sex, name and meaning, as the roll gave it, and the commandant said he had not asked for a road, and the keeper said that the hall held no other Tānthëi from the enclave and that the commandant would find her on the roof. He found her on the roof. "The almanac is a book," she said. "The pass is a pass. It closes in ten days." The commandant went up with his wagons on the almanac's word, and the snow came on the ninth day, and the column came down without the wagons and without the grain, and the Warlord's office opened a file on the loss, which the index put with the roads, where it lay beside her. In her thirteenth year the commandant came up to the roof before he came to the roll, and the column went when she said, and in every year after. She was right for forty years. The hall never wrote that down, because there is no column for being right.

The clerk married in the empire's way, in Jëityüt of his fourth year, by petition to the spirit bureaus and the transfer of his wife's tablets to his father's shrine, and had two sons. His father died, and the office of keeper of the roll passed to him, and his designation changed by two characters, which the roll recorded in the events column, in his own hand, as the roll required. In his seventeenth year at the roll the Censor of Purity came to the prefecture, which is the most feared visit a hall receives, and the Censor's clerks took the roll apart line by line for a month and found nothing. The Censor's chief clerk came to her line in the course of it, an entry whose meaning was Long Road and whose events column was empty, and asked the keeper why the prefecture carried a road on its roll of persons. "It is not a road," the keeper said. "It is a Tānthëi whose name means a road. Read the caste column." The Censor's clerk read the caste column, and the clan, and the sex, and the hand, and found every cell filled and every cell true, and passed over the line, as the roll gave him no reason not to.

In his twenty-third year a new index clerk, young and exact in the way the keeper had been young and exact, found a Tānthëi filed among the roads and moved her to the persons, and brought the keeper the file to show him the correction. The keeper read it and sent it back. "The index follows the meaning column," he said. "What does the meaning column read?" The index clerk said it read Long Road. "Then she is filed with the roads," the keeper said, "until the roll says otherwise, and the roll does not say otherwise." The index clerk asked whether the keeper wished the roll to say otherwise, which was a question nobody in the hall had put to him in twenty-three years, and the keeper said that what he wished was not a column. He would not let anyone else correct it either.

His elder son learned the roll at his side from the age of eight, as he had learned it at his own father's, and the keeper taught the boy on her entry. It was the entry he chose when he wanted to show what a correct line looked like, since every cell of it was filled and the meaning was given and nothing in it had ever needed amending, and the boy copied it out on waste paper until his hand had it: the caste, the clan, the household, the district, the sex, _Tir_, _va_, _li_, Long Road, the year, the hand. The boy grew up believing her name was Tirvali, because the roll said so and his father had taught him the roll. He called her so on the roof when he carried the charts up, and she corrected him once, in full, and he said, "The roll says Tirvali," in his father's voice, and she let it stand.

He never earned a personal name. A name in the empire is granted for a thing done that heaven notices, a reading that turns the court or a star found where no star was charted, and the keeping of a roll without an error for forty years is what a Tānthëi does. Nobody is named for it. The roll gave him what it gave every unnamed Tānthëi, his clan and his office and his district, and he answered to it, and she answered to a road, and the hall did its business between them.

She counted the years her own way, in twenties, and when the roll had counted forty she had counted two. Each year, when the inspector had been and gone, she went up into the forest in Jīkyüt and came down again in Vëngyüt, the month of winds, when the Tānthëi read the year's dispatches out of the sky. She showed the tablet at the town gate and at the bridge and at the last post on the road up, and at the forest's edge she put it away in her sleeve, since the road ended there, and so, for the summer, did Tirvali. In Vëngyüt she took it out again at the last post and was a road to the town gate. Some Sinalë who serve a term in the halls do not come back out of the forest when it ends. She came back every Vëngyüt for forty years, and nobody in the hall asked her why, and the clerk did not ask either, having no column for it.

In his thirty-fourth year the archivist of the hall, going into the records store at night for a lamp, heard the keeper of the roll alone among the shelves saying one word, low, with the pitch falling at the end of it, and took it for a recitation from the sacred texts, and went out again without the lamp.

The illness came on him in Thürkyüt of the fortieth year, the month of winter, when the empire draws in and conserves and a sick man near sixty is expected to do the same. His household sent into the town for a physician, and the physician came and said what a physician says in that month. His elder son kept the roll in his place and entered nothing, since nothing had yet happened that the roll records. She went to the house the next day, down through the town with the tablet in her sleeve, and was asked for it at nobody's door. She was of his caste, and his household let her in and left her at the door of his room, which was a small room with a shrine shelf and a brazier and no paper in it anywhere.

He said, "Sirvalira."

It was the whole name, worn for calling, with the fall at the end that closes a thought.

She stood a long while, and then she went out.

He died before the month was out. His sons did what the empire's sons do: the tablet carved with his clan and his office, set in the household shrine, the offerings burned, the death entered in the events column of the roll by the elder son, who had the brush now, in the same hand his father had taught him. Where he went the tale does not say.

In Mütyüt, the month of wood, when the Bureau of Continuity is ascendant and everything planted in the empire is planted and registered, she came down from the roof at first light with a birch sapling wrapped at the root in wet cloth, which had come down out of the forest that week with her hearth's letters and had waited, as her letters waited, in the Bureau of Passage's room among the surveys, and she set it against the east wall of the records store, on the hall's ground, where the morning reaches first and the wall keeps the north wind off. Her people plant a tree for one of their dead and do it privately, and this was not private. Ground that belongs to a hall is classified ground, and a tree set in it is a classified living thing, and the planting roll must hold it, and the form requires the planter present and the planter's name taken at the time of planting. So the elder son came out of the store with the planting roll under his arm while she was still treading the earth down, and opened it on the wall, and asked her, as the form asks, the name of the planter.

"Tirvalira Solmerinto," she said.

He wrote what the roll held. The line is the one the gloss stands against, and the tellers end on it, in the roll's own order:

"One birch. East wall of the records store. Mütyüt. Planted by: Tirvali."
