**The Gathering on the End**, _Olvandiren aulirarno_, is what came down from [[place-ethalosse|Ethalossë]] when the court at [[place-beravel|Béravel]] asked, through the [[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan]] who carry, how the [[lore-flksinale|Sinalë]] hold that the world ends. The question went up in the ordinary way, in the mouths of two elders asked to carry it, and what came back down in the same mouths is the account of an Aulirarno, the open convocation of the faith of [[lore-goddreams|Aulveira]], called on that question in the [[lore-sinalearrv|Age of Stars]]. An Aulirarno speaks until it reaches accord. This one has not, so by its own rule it has never closed: a Sinalo may rise and add a speech to it in any age, and the latest was added in living memory. The carriers were given the account as the Sinalë keep it, and they brought it down as it was given, adding nothing. Where a passage was withheld from them they say so, where they did not follow what was sung they say that too, and the court's scribe kept both in the copy he took down from their mouths, which is the copy below. The archive at Béravel keeps that copy under the two words the carriers gave for its standing, no accord, and the copy does not say why the court asked. The [[affiliation-bardicolgs|Bardic Colleges]] have read it and have not set it, since it is prose with the speeches sung inside it, and nothing in it is theirs to sing.

## The Calling

The account tells the time of the gathering by a tree: it was called in the winter the first oak on the eastern slope seeded. The carriers asked which slope, and were told that it was the slope.

A child not yet named asked the question. The child had been watching the fires of the young bands at the edge of the wood, where a band had put one of its own in the ground that autumn and sat about the fire afterward and then gone on, and the child came up from the edge and asked the Ansorunno who read the dreams of that hearth whether the world also stops. The account keeps the child's words: "They put him in the ground and sat by it. Then they went on. Does the world stop too? When?" The Ansorunno said: "I do not know." The account keeps that too, and the carriers say that the Sinalo who gave it to them sang those three words and said all the rest around them. The Ansorunno called the gathering, which any of them may do. The account does not say what the child was later named.

They came from the hearths within a season's walk, and the account names the hearths by what each keeps and not by who came: from the wide sky, from the rising note, from the low fog, from the cold spring, from the long dusk, from the cupped hand, from the split log. It counts forty-seven of the Ansorunno and the Ilthorinno, and more than that again who were neither and came to hear. They met in a clearing below the eastern passes that the account calls only the clearing, and sat on the ground in no order. It was the first night of the snow, and nobody remarked on it.

Nobody presides at an Aulirarno. Whoever rises speaks or sings, and sits when the speech is done, and whoever rises next has the clearing. The Ansorunno who had called this one sang to the god first, as they do at a sitting, and the account keeps no words of that song because it had none in it that asked for anything; and then that Ansorunno put the question as the child had put it, and did not make it smaller.

Asked the child: when stops the world?

That is the question as the gathering took it. The carriers say the order of the words is the order the Sinalë gave them, and that they were asked to keep it.

Before anyone rose with a speech, an Ilthorinno who is not named asked what the dreams of that winter had said, since every hearth that came had read its dreams before setting out and would read them again in the clearing. The Ansorunno answered that they had been read, and the account lists what the dreams of that winter were of, hearth by hearth, in the order the hearths had come: a ford in low water; a hand on an unstrung bow; bread, and the smell of it; a stranger at a hearth, welcome; a fox on the snow at the edge of the wood, looking back; a child counting stars and losing the count and beginning again; still water with nothing on it. The reading was that the god had sent nothing about an end, and the account says that nobody in the clearing took nothing for an answer, and that the one who had asked sat down.

## The One Who Keeps the Long Song

The first to rise kept the long song of the stars, which is sung from the first star that stands out over the eastern passes in the evening to the last that the morning takes, and which fills the whole of a winter's night in the singing. She had kept it since before the oaks on that slope were oaks. The account gives her name at the end of her speech, as the Sinalë give a speaker's name, and the carriers kept it there. They asked how many stars the long song holds and were told that it holds the ones that are there. She sang:

: The Speech of the One Who Keeps the Long Song {#long-song}

```poetry {form=evesirno lang=en}
There is a song that is sung from the first star to the last.
  It begins when the first stands out over the passes and it ends with the one the morning takes.
I have held it since before the oaks on that slope were oaks.
  It is one song. It has one shape, and the shape is the whole of it.
Listen to the way it is held at the end. The last line is not let fall.
  The singer stops. The line is held in the breath after, and then there is the morning.
That is an end. I have made it every winter, and I know its shape from the inside.
  Nobody has asked me whether the stars stop when I stop. They do not.
The song stops, and the sound goes on. A song is one shape of the sound.
  Sing the same stars in another order and that is another song, and a true one.
We know this. Of all who sit here, we know it, because we have stood where
  […]
I say the world is one shape of what is, as the song is one shape of the sound.
  I say it ends when its singer stops, and that the breath after is also sung.
Who sings it, I do not say. Ask me whether the sound ends and I will stand silent, because I do not know.
  Ask me whether this ends: yes. It is a song. It is being sung now. Hear how it is held.
```

The carriers were told that eleven lines stand in the gap and were not given to them, and that the singer did not finish the line before the gap either, when she sang it.

The account names her here: **Tellavira Eilavanto**, the small bell, of the hearth of the wide sky.

The one who kept the oaks rose while the last line was still being held, which is not done, and the account keeps that he did it. He said, before he sang: "You say the stars stop when the singer stops."

She said: "I said the song."

He said: "I heard the stars." And he sang.

## The One Who Keeps the Oaks

He kept the oaks on the eastern slope, the one that had seeded that winter and the older one above it, and he was the one who had told the gathering which winter it was. The account says he sang with his back to the clearing and his face to the slope, so that the oaks could hear it, and that nobody thought this strange.

: The Speech of the One Who Keeps the Oaks {#oaks}

```poetry {form=evesirno lang=en}
There is an oak on the eastern slope that I have tended since it was a shoot the height of my hand,
  and there is an older one above it with rot in the heart, and I tend that one too.
Every year the leaf turns. Every year it falls, and the ground takes it, and by the thaw it is ground.
  Nothing I do stops that. I do not try to. The ground is owed the leaf.
Something eats the heart of the old oak from the inside, a hand's width in a twenty of winters.
  I cut back to the sound wood and seal the cut and the tree stands. It has stood for all of my tending.
What eats it is not the ground. The ground gives back. What eats the heart gives nothing back.
  We have a name for the great ones who are like that: the ones of the Dark. It is our name. They have not told us theirs.
They eat what is made. The leaf, the heart, the hearth, the song: whatever is raised, they take down,
  without anger, as far as I have seen; as the ground takes the leaf, because it is there to be taken.
And they are slow. That is the whole of what I know and it is enough to tend by.
  The eating is slower than the growing while someone tends. The day it is not is the end.
No day is that day while a hand is on the tree. I have not seen the day. I have seen the hand.
  Leave the moss on the north side. It holds the wet off the bark. I say that to whoever comes after me.
Tellavira sang that the world ends when its singer stops. I say it ends when its tender stops, and I say it is slower.
  So tend. That is my speech. It is not long, because the oak is waiting and the rot does not wait with it.
```

The account names him: **Rivydhë Syllëntë**, the turning leaf, of the hearth of the rising note.

The one who kept the meadow stood then and said: "You say the Dark is slow."

He said: "I said the eating."

She said: "Then your end is a speed." They laughed here. The carriers say they did not understand it, and did not ask.

## The One Who Keeps the Meadow

She kept the meadow below the passes, the tall grass that the fog lies in, and she had kept it longer than either of the others had kept anything; she was the eldest in the clearing. The account says she sang sitting, which an elder may, and that she did not look at the two who had sung before her while she answered them. The carriers say that the Sinalo who gave them the account sang her speech lower than the other two, and slower.

: The Speech of the One Who Keeps the Meadow {#meadow}

```poetry {form=evesirno lang=en}
The grass on the meadow below the passes goes down under the snow every winter, every stem of it.
  By the first thaw it is black and flat and smells of the ground. Seen once, that is an end.
I have seen the spring. I have seen it more times than the old oak has rings.
  The grass stands in the fog, as it always does. It is not the same grass. It is the grass.
You two have sung of a singer stopping and a tender stopping. Who stops? Name one of us who has.
  None of us has ended but by choosing to go, and the ones who go are not ended. They are elsewhere.
We heard the question first from the fires at the edge of the wood, in the changing words the bands had then,
  when one of them had been put in the ground and the rest sat and asked the fire where he had gone.
We did not answer them. We left them to their becoming, which was right, and which I would do again.
  I say we leave ourselves to it also. The end is their word. It is a word for a people whose lives have one.
Ours have not. Put the question down. It is not ours to lift, and we did not lift it for them when it was theirs.
  Sellavira, you sang that the breath after the song is also sung. Then nothing has ended. You have sung my speech.
The grass goes down. The grass stands. Between the two there is a winter, and that is all there is between them.
```

The account names her: **Lenthila Thalvento**, the tall grass, of the hearth of the low fog.

Tellavira said: "I sang that the breath is sung. I did not sing that the morning is."

Lenthila said: "You will, if you sit long enough."

Rivydhë said, to the slope and not to her: "The old oak will not stand up in the spring. I have had my hand in its heart. There is a spring it will not stand up in."

Lenthila said: "Then it is your oak that ends. I did not hear the child ask about your oak."

Rivydhë said: "The child asked at the edge of the wood. The oak is in the wood." And the account says that he sat down after that and did not rise again that night, and that the moss on the north side of the old oak was there in the morning, because he went up the slope before the light to look.

## What Stands Between

The gathering spoke the rest of that night and the nights after it, and it did not agree. When the hearths were ready to go back, the Ansorunno who had called it said this, and the account keeps it in these words: "It has not agreed, so it is not closed. Let it wait. Whoever has a speech may rise with it, in this age or in another." A gathering had been left open before, for a season or a winter; one had not been left to wait across ages, and nobody spoke against it. The child who had asked was asleep.

This is how the gathering has been kept open since, as the carriers were told it. A Sinalo who has a speech to add does not add it alone. The gathering is called again, as any Ansorunno may call it, and those who come sit in the clearing, and before the new speech is sung every speech that stands in the account is sung again in its order, by whoever keeps it, with the exchanges between them said as they were said, so that the one who rises last has heard the whole of what they are answering and the gathering has heard it too. The account is never recited for its own sake; it is recited because someone is about to add to it. The Sinalo who gave it to the carriers told them that this is why they were given it whole: "Nobody sings a part of it. I sang it in the clearing in the year a speech was added, and I have not sung it since."

The gathering holds other speeches between these three and the last, from the ages between, and those were not given to the carriers. The account they were given goes from the meadow to the north, and the carriers say that they were told the distance and did not ask what filled it.

One thing from the ages between was given to them, because it is not a speech. In one age a sitting of the gathering, having sung the speeches again and heard a new one, sat on through the night without anyone rising, and toward morning one of the Ansorunno said that the gathering had sat with the question long enough, that the oak-keeper's speech was the nearest thing in it to something a hearth could tend by, and that the gathering might reach accord on that and close. Someone the account does not name answered: "An accord reached because we are tired is only sleep. The child did not ask us to sleep." The sitting stood up at that and went back to its hearths, and the one who had proposed the closing went with them and did not speak against the answer. The carriers were given this so that they would know the gathering had been offered an end and had not taken it. They were told nothing about the speech that sitting had added.

## The One Who Stood at the Edge

The latest to rise was an Ansorunno of a hearth in the high valleys, and he had been a guest, once, at [[place-haulonna|Haulonna]], the hearth of the unfallen leaf in the north, in the season it was named for, when the leaf does not fall. When word came south he went north again, alone, as those who have gone north since have gone, and stood at the edge of that valley, and came back and called the gathering. Every sitting since the first had been called to add a speech; he called this one on a question of his own, as the first Ansorunno had called it on the child's, and found when he stood in the clearing that what he had to say was a speech. He called it at the same clearing. The oaks on the eastern slope were not the same oaks, and the meadow was.

The account names the hearths that came by what each keeps, as before: from the wide sky, from the rising note, from the low fog, from the cold spring, from the long dusk, from the cupped hand, from the split log, from the turning year, and from others that were not hearths when the gathering was first called; and from the hearth of the unfallen leaf, nobody. Whether the three who sang first sat in the clearing that night, the account does not say, and the carriers did not ask. The speeches were sung again in their order, and the exchanges said, and the one who had called the gathering stood when the meadow's last line had been held and let fall, and sang standing, facing north.

: The Speech of the One Who Stood at the Edge {#edge}

```poetry {form=evesirno lang=en}
I have been a guest at a hearth in the north in the season it was named for, when the leaf does not fall.
  I have gone back to it this year and stood at its edge, and I will tell you what I saw and no more than I saw.
The trees stand and are dead. The water runs and is dark. Nothing with wings goes in.
  The air in the middle of summer is the air under a stone. The wards are there. They are turned inward.
The people are there. They have not gone. That is the thing I came back to say, and I will say it once:
  the dead of Haulonna have not gone where the dead go. They are held.
Tellavira sang that the world ends when the singer stops. The singer has not stopped. The stars came out over that valley while I stood at its edge.
  Rivydhë sang that the end is slow. It was one night. It was one night, and the leaf did not fall, and the people did not go.
Lenthila sang that the end is a word for a people whose lives have one, and that none of us has ended.
  I have no answer for her. I have where I stood.
I say that a world ends: a hearth, a valley, everyone in it, in a night,
  and the rest of the world does not know, and the grass on the meadow below the passes stood up that spring as it always does.
That is the end as I have seen it, and I have seen one. A valley with its people kept in it.
  One place at a time, and the next place not knowing, and the next.
We know where the dead go. Aulveira takes them to **Ollevirno**. On that there has never been a gathering, because there is nothing in it to speak against.
  I do not know whether Ollevirno outlasts the world. I asked two who chose to go there and have slipped back. They are no wiser about it than I am.
```

The account marks a silence after this the length of a long line, and nobody took it up.

The account names him: **Hyssëdhë Soirunto**, the held breath, of the hearth of the turning year, and after the name an epithet the carriers rendered as the one who stood at the edge. He went north again when the gathering had sat its nights, and his speech stayed in the clearing with the one who keeps it now, as each of them is kept by someone until the gathering is called again. The account does not say that he came back, and the Sinalo who gave it to the carriers did not say either. Of Ollevirno the carriers were told that the name is the gathering of the dead, and nothing else, and they asked nothing else.

## The One Word

The carriers say that the account was given to them at Ethalossë over three evenings by one Sinalo, who sang the speeches and said the rest, and who told them at the end of the third evening to carry the speeches sung and the rest said. They said that they could not carry a Sinalë song as it was sung, having neither the pitch nor the breath for it, and that they would carry the words. They were told to carry the words, then, and that the words were the lesser part, and that this was understood. The court's copy carries the words.

The court heard the account read and sent the carriers up again with a second question, which was to have the gathering's answer in one word.

They carried it up and brought the answer down the same day, which the carriers say is the only time either of them has carried anything down from Ethalossë on the day it was carried up. No gathering was called for it. The Sinalo who had given them the account gave them the answer at Ethalossë, and said that it was the gathering's standing and not a new word from it: "We have not agreed. Ask again when you have a speech of your own."

The carriers say that they asked, since the answer was short and the day was long, whether a speech of the court's own would be heard if one came up, and that they were told that the gathering has no door, and that nothing was said about whether it would be answered.

The carriers gave the account's last line as it was given to them, in the order the Sinalë gave it, and asked that it be written as it was said and not turned into a sentence of the court's. It stands so in the copy, and nothing stands after it:

Speaks the gathering.
