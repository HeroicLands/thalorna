Bethûa is a matriarchy, and not the gentle kind a foreigner imagines on hearing the word. Property, title and authority pass from mother to daughter; men hold no political, religious or military standing; and—uniquely among the realms of the region—men hold no standing as free persons either. A Bethûan explains this without embarrassment, because to her it is not a policy but the order of things, sanctioned by goddesses, and the arrangements she sees in foreign ports strike her as both unnatural and badly run.

## Harmony and Its Enemy

The Bethûan word for the good that holds a life together is _harmôdhin_, harmony, and the saying runs that harmony holds the world together. **Good is the order kept: each woman in her House, each man in a woman's charge, each goddess on her bench, and the word given to a House honored by the House that gave it.** Wisdom (_sôthira_) is called a good thing and a goddess's gift. **Evil is the order broken, and the breaking has two faces.** One is the hand raised against the women who hold the realm: a bound man who strikes his mistress is a capital matter, and a man with a weapon or a trained arm is a danger the realm has engineered against for as long as it has existed. The other is the House betrayed, the daughter who throws away what her mother kept.

Success is a House that other Houses marry into and borrow against: the daughter named to follow her mother, the seat on the Council of Houses, the Great House whose letter is good at any other. A woman succeeds by the standing of her House and by her office within it, and a man succeeds as an asset of one, prized for his strength, his skill and his beauty. Failure is to be Houseless: stripped of a commission, cast out of the House that bore her and unspoken for by anyone with a seat.

The Bethûan value most the House, the water that the engineer-priestesses bring to the dry interior, and the fleet that keeps the realm free. They value least the free man trading on his own account, which is why a foreign man at a Bethûan quay is served and condescended to in a single breath.

## The House

The House is the unit of everything. A matriarch leads it, it owns the land, the workshops, the orchards, the fishing-boats and the cargoes, and it answers for every person attached to it. A Bethûan woman introduces herself by House before anything else, and the standing of the House is the standing of every daughter in it.

Daughters inherit, and a matriarch names her successor from among them. The naming is public, contested, and frequently the most important thing that happens in a House for a generation. A woman passed over does not usually leave; she takes a lesser office inside the House and waits, because there is nowhere better to go.

## The Place of Men

In law and custom a Bethûan man is the dependent and charge of a woman—his mother, then the House matriarch, then the wife or mistress to whom he is bound. He cannot own property, inherit, make a binding contract in his own name, or stand before a court except through the woman responsible for him. He is, in plain terms, a kind of property: valued, often cherished, and property nonetheless. Below the free-born man sits a small population of outright male slaves, bought and sold, and the difference between the two is one of regard, not of right.

What a man is prized for is his person—his strength, his skill and his beauty. A handsome, well-made man is an ornament to a House and a mark of his mistress's standing, and a gifted craftsman, husbandman or seafarer is a genuine asset. Such a man may be courteously styled _kýros_, master, a title of real esteem that confers, pointedly, nothing. The labor of Bethûa's workshops and fleets is largely men's labor; the workshops and fleets belong to women. A man does not trade in Bethûa—he trades _for_ the woman who holds him.

Beneath the regard runs a constant and sober fear. The matriarchy has never forgotten that its men are on average the stronger sex and outnumber its warrior-women many times over, and the nightmare of every sovereign is the rising in the harbor-quarter. The order is engineered against it: no man may possess a weapon or a piece of armor on pain of death, martial training for men is forbidden and actively watched for, and men are watched in the wards, on the docks and in the fields as a permanent and unremarkable fact of civic life. The velvet of Bethûan regard is wrapped tight around that iron, and a Bethûan sees no contradiction in it whatever.

### The Spádai

The single exception is the Spádai, the sworn eunuch-warriors who fight beside the women of the Spear-Sisterhood. They are the most honored men in Bethûa, and they are trusted with steel precisely because they have been set outside the thing the matriarchy fears: no House to raise, no sons to make patriarchs of, no stake in any male revolt.

Bethûa repays this with genuine reverence. The Spádai are praised in song and ceremony, equipped at the crown's expense, and held up as the ideal of what a man may become once he has surrendered what makes men dangerous. A Bethûan mother of a beautiful and gifted son may put him forward for it, and be proud.

## The Grace

The Spear-Sisterhood trains from youth in arms and tactics and in the Charíssa—the Grace—an open-hand and kicking art built to turn a woman's agility into a weapon and let her beat a larger, stronger opponent. It is neither a sport nor an ornament. The realm's security rests on the proposition that a trained woman defeats an untrained man, and Bethûa intends to keep that proposition true.

Above the army stands the fleet, which is what Bethûa is actually famous for: the best navy in the region, escorting its convoys, guarding its coast, and waging a deniable privateer war on Vylarian shipping as the maritime proxy of its Khelâthi patron.

## The Man Admired

**Dôrashôs**, a Spádon posted to the quays of Bethûra, is the man the harbor-quarter and the Houses both remember, and the story is told in the wards as often as in the halls. Taken young and sworn, he was a son of that quarter before the oath, and when the oar-gangs of the harbor stopped rowing and a rising began on the quays, he went into the crowd with his blade sheathed and named the men in front of him one by one. He knew their mothers' Houses. The gangs rowed on the next tide, and the Houses whose granaries stood sealed opened them within the week. The Spear-Sisterhood was never loosed on the quarter.

The Bethûan admire in him what the realm asks of every man and asks hardest of the Spádai: strength held under restraint, and loyalty that prevents the nightmare and does not need to answer it. He is the proof offered for the whole order, that a man can be armed and safe. His cost is that he belongs to neither side of it. The men of the quarter do not forget which hand wears the blade, and the Houses honor him in song and do not let him own the bench he sits on.

## The Woman Admired

**Matriarch Aselia of House Khalath-Mara**, the senior voice of the Council of Matriarchs, is the woman the trading Houses point to when they say what a matriarch is for. Her House is the oldest and most prestigious of the Great Houses, seated at [[place-ashira|Ashíra]], and trades in premium wine and olive oil. She has held her place for decades, and she is the most politically capable of the Bethûan trade-house figures of her generation. She is the dominant voice on the Council when she chooses to speak.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the Spádon's. Dôrashôs holds a quarter quiet with his own body, and Aselia holds a federation of rival Houses to one standard of credit with her own word. Her cost is the weight of that silence. A matriarch who speaks only when she chooses answers for everything the Council does when she does not.

## What the Realm Keeps

A Bethûan can point to the things that make the order work. **The Purple Veil**, the murex-dyed mantle and face-veil of the Mêtríssa, is the realm's standard, and the same purple dyes the fleet's sails. **The Archivist's rolls** are the nearest thing Bethûa has to a founding document: the genealogies on which every House's claim to standing rests, with the daughters' names set down in order. **The seal** (_kathûr_) closes a warrant, a contract and a letter of credit alike, and a House's seal on its paper is the whole of its credit. **The sealed rolls of the Purple Warrant** are the crown's commission for privateers. **The scales** (_halzât_) are the oracle's weighing: a name carried to the oracle of Zûnêlâs is weighed, and the sovereign's election is made sacred when it is found true. **The aqueducts, cisterns and qanats** are the monument everyone sees, and the Order of the Waters keeps them as a priesthood keeps a shrine.

## The Goddesses on the Bench

Bethûans keep the Aurèldían pantheon, the same gods venerated across much of the world, and they venerate both sexes freely—a warrior prays to Árdavon, a smith to the Sacred Forge, a sailor to Táranon. What is distinctively Bethûan is the state cult.

Sacred authority descends through goddesses: Lúsinía of creation and beauty, who is the sovereign's own patron; Zûnêlâs of dreams and the unseen; Ólvenía of prosperity and pleasure. Those three orders anchor the realm's sacred government, and the Mêtríssa rules as a high priestess of a goddess. The gods men favor are honored in their temples and have no place on that bench, and no Bethûan finds this any stranger than that a fisherman does not sit in the Council of Houses.

An ordinary Bethûan's observance is the practical kind. A merchant spends the night before a contract at an Ólvenía shrine and brings a portion of a good profit back to the altar. A sailor keeps a small shrine to Táranon aboard and offers the first portion of a meal. A woman heavy with child goes to the temples of Lúsinía, where the priestesses tend gardens, herbs and midwifery. A House asks the oracle of Zûnêlâs when a decision turns on what cannot be seen. The Sélmoran priesthood buries the House's dead.

## The Year at Sea and Ashore

The festivals that matter are the ones that open a season of work. The **Festival of First Growth** in early spring belongs to Lúsinía, with planting in the communal gardens, blessed fields, and young couples pledging themselves to the goddess. The **Thunderhead Vigil** at the onset of spring is kept by sailors and storm-priests on hilltops and ship decks, waiting for the first storm of the sea-year. The **Festival of the Golden Harvest** in late summer belongs to Ólvenía and runs three days, the most openly hedonistic celebration of the Aurèldían year; merchants and craftswomen close their shops while the traders come home with laden hulls. The **Silent Passage** of Sélmoros in late autumn closes the temples to the living for three nights while the priesthood buries the year's dead.

Each is for something concrete. First Growth is for the field and the marriage, the Vigil for the voyage, the Golden Harvest for the cargo and the credit, and the Silent Passage for the House's dead.

## Rank, Reward and Penalty

Reward runs through the House. A daughter named to follow her mother is rewarded with the seal, a Dómissa with a seat in the Council of Houses, a Hégissa with a canton, a Hiérissa with a place in the College and an Izet-Mû with the water of the realm. A warrant-captain of the fleet keeps the bulk of what she takes, and a Charíssa master carries real prestige. A man's reward is regard: the courtesy of _kýros_, a place in the household he serves well, and for the Spádai the songs, the quarters and the equipment the crown provides.

Penalty climbs the other way. A woman passed over for the name takes a lesser office and waits. A House's seal that is dishonored loses its credit in every House that lends on it. A woman who fails her House or is made the failure the Houses need is stripped of her commission, cast out of her House and exiled, which is the worst the realm can say of a woman short of death. A man who strikes a woman or takes up arms without leave is put to death. A runaway bound-man is hunted. A Spádon who is cast out joins the Houseless of the south.

## Where the Veil Ends and the Temple Begins

Authority is divided and neither half governs alone. The sacred pillar is the Sacred College, the assembled high priestesses of the three goddess-orders and the great temples, and it is the realm's highest religious court and the electoral body that makes a Mêtríssa. The secular pillar is the Council of Houses, the assembled Dómissai, which ratifies law, votes the levies and judges disputes between Houses. The Mêtríssa sits where they meet, Queen and High Priestess at once.

A Dómissa sits in judgment over her own clansfolk, a Hégissa holds the courts, the levy and the water-quotas of a canton in the sovereign's name, and the Grammatíssai keep the rolls and draft the law. The Archivist keeps the genealogies, the Izet-Halzi the royal fisc, the Harbor-Mistress the port. The Trade-Houses form a third, unofficial power whose matriarchs sit in both councils besides. Where the pillars disagree, neither can overrule the other, and the Mêtríssa plays one against the other just enough to stay indispensable to both. The oracle of Zûnêlâs holds a quiet veto over any election. A weak Mêtríssa finds the pillars leaning past her toward each other.

## Learning and the Foreign Eye

The Bethûan tongue is related to Helonic by an ancient migration, and Bethûan scholars keep up a correspondence with Helionite intellectuals that both sides find useful. An educated Bethûan woman reads both.

Toward foreigners the realm is exact and cool. A foreign man trading in a Bethûan port is suffered, not welcomed: served fairly, his coin taken, his contracts honored, and met throughout with a condescension he can do nothing about. Part of it is simple affront, since a free man trading on his own account presumes as of right to do what no Bethûan man may do. A foreign woman trading on her own account meets none of it, and often finds Bethûa the easiest market on the coast.

The one exception is an official of Aû'Khelâthu, received with marked deference regardless of sex—a public, daily acknowledgment of the patron relationship that everyone, and the Vylarians most of all, is meant to notice.

## What the Gate Asks

The Aurèldían faith teaches that the soul passes through the gate that Sélmoros, the Silent Judge, keeps. His priests sit with the dying, and the dying person speaks a final testimony: wrongs confessed, goods affirmed, so the soul meets the gate with an unburdened heart. The Threshold Keepers keep the temple's records of the dead.

The belief changes an ordinary day in the ledger. A Bethûan woman settles the House's accounts, names her successor in public and leaves the House solvent, because a House is judged by its payments and the Archivist's roll keeps what she did. A House endows its crypt and pays for the perpetual care of its tombs, since the temples live on endowments and the temple's record of the dead is what a House's standing rests on. A man's testimony is spoken to the priest in his own voice, and it is one of the few places in a life where he speaks for himself and is entered in a record under his own name. He is otherwise entered under his mother's House.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the temple and the crypt. The priesthood of Sélmoros opposes any practice that raises the dead or violates a tomb, so the temples give the living no way to question the dead, only to honor them. The honoring is the funeral, the tomb in the House's crypt, the endowment paid for its care, and the Silent Passage, when the priesthood performs rites for the year's dead for the families that ask. The dead do not reach the living. They reach the roll: the name in the Archivist's genealogy, the debt left open, the endowment kept up.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Bethûan woman what a person owes, and she answers in order: the House first, then the matriarch who leads it, then the goddess whose order her House stands under, then the realm. A daughter owes her mother obedience until she is named or passed over, and competence afterward.

A Bethûan man, asked the same question, answers that he owes everything to the woman responsible for him—and he answers without irony, because the alternative has never been available to him and the realm has been careful that it never will be.

In return the House owes every person attached to it an answer in court, a place at its table and a name in its roll. A matriarch owes a daughter her inheritance and her training, and the House owes a man the protection and the keeping of the woman who holds him. The realm owes the Houses the fleet that keeps the sea-lanes open, and the Houses owe the realm the levy the Council votes.

## The Weak

Care is the House's first. A House answers for every person attached to it, so the destitute of a House are its own: the widow, the orphaned daughter, the ageing sister who was passed over and took a lesser office. The temples tend what the Houses cannot, and the working priestesses of a temple tend its lands and its charities. A woman with no House at all has nothing: no seat, no name that lenders read, no one to speak for her. Some of the Houseless end in the south among the Jackals, a warband of exiled women and cast-out Spádai.

The limit is the roll. A person the roll does not hold has no one to answer for her.

## Those Who Cannot Work

The House carries its injured and its old as long as its seal can afford them. A man who cannot work is still a charge: his mother's House, the matriarch or the woman to whom he is bound owes him keeping, and she is answerable for him in law. An old woman who has named her successor advises her House. A Spádon past his service is quartered and honored at the crown's cost. In a bad year the Houses' granaries and the Order of the Waters stand between the interior and hunger, and a House that opens a sealed granary buys the quarter's quiet.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the House's, and when a House cannot, the temple's. Teaching is graded by sex and by station. A Parthéna, a consecrated girl, spends the long temple education before ordination. A girl of a lettered House learns the rolls, the ciphers and the dense legal tongue of warrants and writs. A free girl learns the spear and the Charíssa young. The Order of the Waters trains the engineer-priestesses, and a boy learns his trade at the bench of the man his mistress sets him under. A beautiful and gifted son may be put forward for the Spádai.

Defense is the Spear-Sisterhood and the Spádai, the fleet and the household guard of a Dómissa. The Houses raise the levy the Council of Houses votes, and no man bears arms for the realm unless he has been unmade for it.

## The House at Every Age

The House is the mother's line, and membership is the mother's name. A daughter is born to her House and an infant son is entered under his mother's. A girl is a Parthéna, a trainee of the Lonkhai or a pupil at the bench of a lettered House. A young woman takes a lesser office and waits, serves in the Spear-Sisterhood or on a galley, or rises in the temple or the rolls. A mature woman holds the seal of a House or a seat on one of the councils. An old woman advises, and a dying woman names her successor in public. Marriage is a matter of the matriarch's, since a matriarch commands her House's property, its ships and its marriages.

A boy's life is a series of charges: his mother, then the matriarch, then a mistress. A young man works the oar, the orchard or the bench. A gifted one is put forward for the Spádai; a beautiful one becomes an ornament; a skilled one an asset; the rest are hands. An old man is kept as a charge until he dies.

## What the Order Fears

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Bethûan's is that the order is built on fear. The nightmare of every sovereign is the rising in the harbor-quarter, and the whole apparatus of the law on men exists so that the nightmare cannot come. Thoughtful Bethûans dispute whether an order that must be defended this carefully is an order or a standing siege, and whether a realm that loves its men and fears them in the same breath can ever settle which it does more.

The sin is the Spádai. The realm takes boys young, unmakes them, binds them by oath and sings of them, and a mother who puts a beautiful and gifted son forward does it with pride. The Houseless are the other half of the account. A woman is cast out when the Houses need a failure to blame, and some of those cast out are the Spádai the realm unmade, now loose in the south with a grievance the Court of the Veil would rather not admit it made. The order is paid for by the men it holds and the women it casts out, and no council has put either account on its agenda.
