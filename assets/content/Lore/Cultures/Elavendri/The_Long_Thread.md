**The Long Thread** is the tale the women of the inner coast of [[place-lirve|Lirve]] tell at their spinning bees, in the villages of the worked forest between the coast road and the deep wood, on the long nights of the dark half of the year. It is told in turns, the way the bee spins: one woman tells while the others spin, and when her thread breaks she stops where she is and the next wheel takes the telling on, so the tale is never told the same way twice and never by one voice. The breaks stand in the text where they fell on the night it was taken down, at a bee in a village under the wood a morning's walk from [[place-liranel|Liranel]].

The tale is of [Áuvenna]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a spinner, who spun on a hinge-night ([[affiliation-groveconcl|The Grove Council]]) when the hedge-wife had told her not to, and who made a bargain at her own door with one of the deeper places ([[lore-faecourts|The Fae Courts]]) that bound her line to keep a thread unbroken; of her daughter [Thélvara]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who asked what was not offered; and of the knot. The bargain is said in verse and the women say it together. The one at the door is never named, because the tale is about not asking. The bees of that coast keep a long thread that runs from the bee-house window into the wood, and at the end of the night every spinner takes it through her fingers until she finds the knot, and says the name. In the houses of the inner coast nobody cuts a thread on a hinge-day; it is broken with the hands, and whoever breaks it says whose it was.

: The Long Thread {#tale}

Lusinia, mind the wheel. Here's the long one, and it's long, so draw your wool out fine.

In a village under the wood, this side of Liranel, there was a spinner called Áuvenna, and she spun the best thread on the coast. You know what that means. It means the weavers of the basin sent for it by name, and she never had a bit of her own, because the weaver who bought it set the price and the weaver who sold the cloth set the other one. She was the last of her house. Her mother had been a spinner and her mother's mother, and before that nobody knew, because a spinner's house has no bard, and she was the last of it, unmarried, past the age, with a wheel and a window and a debt to the weaver she would be working off till she was buried.

The window looked at the wood. It was a good window for spinning, the light came in clean off the trees, and at dusk the bark went silver the way it does on that coast and you could spin by it a little after the lamp should be lit. She liked that. She had nothing else she liked so well.

Now you know what the hinge-days are. The four days the year turns on, and the Grove sits at the great groves, and the wood is thin, and the hedge-wife goes round the village the day before and says: no spinning tomorrow, no weaving, no knots tied, nothing turned on a wheel. Because a wheel turning on a hinge-day turns something else as well. Nobody can tell you what. The hedge-wife couldn't. But you don't.

Áuvenna owed the weaver. The weaver wanted twelve hanks by the new year. The new year came the day after the winter hinge. And so on the winter hinge-night, the longest night, she sat down at the wheel by her window, in the dark with one lamp, and she spun.

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

She spun, and the wood was very still, and the thread came off her fingers fine and even, and she thought, I'll have the twelve, and I'll not tell the hedge-wife. And she had spun about a hank when there was a knock at the door.

A knock at the door on the winter hinge-night. Think of that. She sat with her foot off the treadle and the wheel running down, tick, tick, tick, and she didn't move. And it knocked again, three times, not loud. Courteous. And she thought: it's a guest, and you don't argue the right of a guest under your roof, not on that coast and not anywhere else in the kingdom, and she got up and opened the door.

He was tall. That's the first thing they all say. Taller than the door, but he came in under it without bending. He had a face you'd remember if you could remember it, and nobody who saw him could say after what color his eyes were or whether he had a beard. His cloak was grey, or it was the dark. And there was a smell of the wood on him, not the leaf and the moss, but the cold, the inside of a hollow tree. He was one of the deeper places. Not the old ones of the wood that you see sometimes at the edge, tall and still. Those are people. This was something else, and she knew it the way you know a thing is hot.

She gave him the stool by the fire, because that's what you do, and she put the last of her bread on the board in front of him, because that's what you do. He looked at the bread. He did not eat it. He looked at the wheel.

"You're spinning," he said, "on the hinge."

She said she was.

"Good thread," he said. "Spin me some."

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

Now here's where you listen, because this is the bargain, and a bargain with one of them is kept whole, every word, the ones you heard and the ones you didn't. She said, "How much?" And he said, "From your door to mine." And she said, "Where's yours?" And he said, "In there," and nodded at the window, at the wood. And she said, "What do I get for it?"

He said, "What do you want?"

And she thought about the weaver, and the debt, and the twelve hanks, and then she didn't think about any of those. She thought about her mother, and her mother's mother, and nobody before, and herself at the end of it, past the age, with a wheel and a window, and when she was buried there'd be nobody to plant a tree. And she said, "I want my line not to end."

And he smiled. It was not a good thing to see. And he said the terms, and these are the words, and say them with me:

```verse {form=bargain meter="iambic tetrameter" rhyme=AABB lang=en}
Spin me a thread from your door to mine,
and while it runs, so runs your line.
I'll come along it on the hinge-night
and sit at your fire till the first light.
Set bread for me. I will not eat.
Ask me no name. Keep to your seat.
Spin it, and when your spinning's done,
your daughters spin it, every one.
And if it breaks, your door and mine
are shut, and so is your whole line.
```

"Daughters," she said. "I have none. I've no man."

"Spin," he said, "and see."

So she spun. And she took the end of the thread she had spun that night, and he took it from her fingers, and went out the door with it, and across her yard, and into the wood, and the thread ran off her spindle after him, out the window, fine as a hair, and silver where the dusk caught it, and she could not see it at all by day.

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

You want to know if it came true. It came true. They always do; that's the trouble with them.

That spring a haulier came down the coast road with a team of oxen, **Kelthar**, a big quiet man from the timber yards at Liranel, and he stopped at her door for water, and he came back for water every week all summer, and there's only so much water a man needs. They were married at the autumn hinge. She'd not have chosen the day, but he didn't know, and she couldn't tell him. And the year after there was a daughter, Thélvara, and that was the line, running.

And on every hinge-night, four times a year, the one from the wood came along the thread. She'd hear the knock. Kelthar slept like a felled oak on the hinge-nights, he never woke, not once in all his life; that was part of it. She'd open the door, and the tall one would come in under it, and sit on the stool, and she'd put bread in front of him, and he'd not eat it, and he'd watch her spin. She spun his thread on those nights and no others. She never asked him anything. Not his name. Not where the thread went. Not why he wanted it. In the morning he'd be gone and the bread on the board, and she'd give it to the hens.

And when Thélvara was old enough to hold a spindle, Áuvenna taught her to spin, and when Thélvara was old enough to keep a secret, Áuvenna sat her down by the window and put the end of the long thread in her fingers. You could feel it in the dark, you couldn't see it. She said, "This is ours. You'll spin it after me. And on the hinge-nights, a guest comes. Set bread for him. Don't ask his name." And Thélvara said, "Why?" And her mother said, "Because it isn't offered." And that was all.

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

Thélvara grew up handsome and quick and sharp, sharp as a carding comb, and she spun as well as her mother, nearly. And on the hinge-nights she sat up with her mother and the guest, and set the bread, and watched him not eat it. And it ate at her. You know the kind of girl. A thing she wasn't told was a thing she had to have.

She was eighteen, on a summer hinge-night, the short one. Áuvenna had a fever that night and was in her bed, and Thélvara sat up alone with the guest and spun the long thread for him. And she spun and spun, and the night was short and warm and the window was open, and the wood breathed in through it, and he sat on his stool and watched her, and she thought: I'll ask him. Just once. What's a name. Everybody has one. And she stopped the wheel and turned round on her stool and said, "Sir, what do they call you?"

And the thread broke.

You could hear it. A sound like a harp-string, and then nothing. And the guest stood up, and he was very tall in that little room, and he said, "That is not offered." And then he wasn't there, and the door hadn't opened.

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

Áuvenna heard the string go from her bed, and she knew. She got up with the fever on her and came out and found Thélvara at the wheel with the broken end in her fingers, crying, and outside the window the other end gone off into the wood. And your door and mine are shut, and so is your whole line. The line was Thélvara. It meant Thélvara.

Now the bees don't agree on all of what came next. This is how our mothers told it at this bee; the next village tells it otherwise, and both are kept.

Áuvenna took the broken end off her daughter. She took it out of the house and across the yard to the edge of the wood, in her shift, with the fever on her, in the last of the short night. She didn't go in. You don't. She sat down on the ground at the edge, where the moss starts, and held the end in her hands, open on her knees, and waited. And before the light came he was there, at the edge, the way they come: you don't go to them.

She said, "It broke."

He said, "It did."

She said, "Then my door is shut, and yours, and my line."

He said, "Those are the terms."

She said, "The terms said: if it breaks. They didn't say it can't be tied."

He looked at her a long time. They keep the words whole. Every word. And there was no word in it against a knot.

"Tie it, then," he said. "With what?"

She had nothing in her hands but the broken end. No wool, no flax. So she pulled a hair from her head, one long hair, grey already, and she took the end of his thread out of the dark where he held it, and she knotted the two ends together with her own hair through the knot, the way a spinner joins a broken thread so it won't show, and she pulled it tight.

"It holds," he said.

"It holds," she said.

"And the hair," he said, "is yours, and it's in my thread now, and it'll stay there. So will you."

And she got up, and the tellers say she wasn't ill any more, and she wasn't grey. And she turned and looked at her house, once, with the lamp in the window and her daughter at the wheel, and she went into the wood with him, along the thread.

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

Thélvara looked for her in the morning, and Kelthar woke and looked, and the village looked, and the hedge-wife came and looked at the wood and said nothing, which was the most she could say. And Thélvara spun. What else was there. She sat at the window and spun the long thread, and she found the knot in it with her fingers, a little hard place in the fine, and she held it, and she spun.

And on the autumn hinge-night there was a knock at the door. And she opened it, and it wasn't him. It was her mother. Young. Younger than Thélvara. In the shift she'd gone out in, with no fever, and her hair dark, and her feet wet with the dew from the wood. She came in under the door, and sat on the stool, and Thélvara set bread in front of her, because that's what you do. And her mother looked at the bread, and smiled, and did not eat it. And Thélvara did not ask her anything. Not one thing. She spun the thread, and her mother watched her, and at first light her mother was gone, and the bread on the board.

It had been one night, for Áuvenna. One night, the short one, in there. And Thélvara grew old spinning that thread, and married, and had daughters, and taught them, and every hinge-night her mother came along the thread, young, with wet feet, and sat, and watched, and never ate, and was never asked. When Thélvara was an old woman with grey hair she opened the door to her mother on the winter hinge, and her mother was a girl of nineteen, and they sat together till the light. And when Thélvara died, they say her mother came to the funeral, at the edge of the wood, and stood there, and did not come out of the trees.

[A thread broke here, and the telling passed to the next wheel.]

And that's the long thread. It's that one. You've had it through your fingers. It goes out of that window there, into the wood, and it doesn't break, because we don't let it; it's the bee's now, not one house's, because a house runs out of daughters and a bee doesn't. And you've felt the knot. Áuvenna's knot, with the hair in it. When you find it tonight you say her name, and you don't ask anything, and you don't follow it.

Who comes along it now, on the hinge-nights? We don't ask. We set the bread.

And that's why nobody on this coast cuts a thread on a hinge-day. If one breaks, you break it the rest of the way with your two hands, and you say whose it was. Lusinia, mind the wheel. Draw your wool out fine.
