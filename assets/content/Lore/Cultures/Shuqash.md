The Shell-Bead People live on the golden western coast of the north, south of the Cedar People's fjords, where a chain of islands lies a morning's paddle offshore and the grass of the hills behind the beaches turns gold every summer. They call themselves the [[affiliation-shuqash|Shuqash]], the people of the bead, after the drilled shell beads (_shuq_) that are the only true money in the north. About 350,000 of them live in some sixty towns on the beaches and the islands of the [[place-shuqhalaqrgn|Bead Coast]] and in hundreds of villages in the [[place-tulishlumuqrgn|Oak Valleys]] behind the coastal mountains, and no town rules another. The sea gives them fish, seals and shellfish, the oaks give them acorns, and the plank canoes of the [Canoe Kin]{.name data-type="affiliation/guild"} carry beads from the islands to the mainland and acorns, seeds and baskets back. Everything has its price in beads, and every price is counted.

Mountains close the coast to the east and desert closes it to the south, and the Shell-Bead People have never had to stand with anyone. The [[affiliation-kelwanuk|Cedar People]] come down the coast to trade for beads and to raid for slaves, and the [[affiliation-teyumi|Turquoise People]] send traders over the mountains for beads and shells. The south has no name for them. Its traders have never reached the coast, and the few beads that cross the desert pass through the Turquoise People's hands first.

## Where They Came From

The astronomer-priests teach that the sky once held more stars than it does, and that the stars that fell into the western sea became the small olive-colored snails whose shells the islanders drill. A bead is a piece of the sky in that teaching, and counting beads is a small copy of the work the priests do when they count the days. The islanders add that the snails are thickest off [Shuq Siwaq]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, Bead Island, because the most stars fell there.

For most of their past the coast towns were fishing camps of a few families each, and their reed rafts reached the islands only in calm weather. The towns date their wealth from [Shiyawkal]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Sea Arriving, who sewed the first plank canoe on the beach at [[place-kulaywil|Kulaywil]]. He split planks from driftwood logs, drilled their edges, sewed them together with plant-fiber cord and sealed the seams with tar from the seeps along the shore, and his canoe crossed to the islands in a morning. He taught the art to his sons and to no one else, and the Canoe Kin are his descendants. With his canoes the islands' beads reached the mainland in quantity, the towns began to price their goods in them, and the families who held the most beads became the chiefs of the towns.

The northern raiders came later. The Cedar People's war canoes first came down the coast for slaves in the lifetime of the present elders' great-grandparents, and what the coast towns did about them is the story the Shell-Bead People argue over most, told at the end of this note.

## The Count Is Everything

**Good is a true count: a strand of full length, a debt paid when it falls due, a gift answered, a killing paid for. Evil is the short strand: the cheat, the thief, the debt denied, and the wrong too large for any count.** The Shell-Bead People hold that the world stays in order while its accounts balance. The astronomer-priests count the days so that the sun comes back at midwinter; the chief's counter counts the town's beads so that the town keeps its peace; and every household counts what it owes and what it is owed. A person who keeps a true count is a good person, whatever else they do.

Wrongs are judged by that measure. A theft is a debt the victim never agreed to, and the thief owes it back twice over. A killing is the largest debt a person can run up. It is paid to the dead person's kin at twenty strands for a life, man or woman, and once it is paid the kin may not take revenge. A debt denied is worse than a theft, because the debtor has broken the count itself, and nobody can trade with a man who will not say what he owes. The worst wrongs are the ones no strand can pay: killing one's own kin, stealing a crossing strand, and guiding raiders to another town.

## Standing in a Town

Success is beads that move. A rich man who sits on his strands is mocked as a strand asleep; a rich man who gives feasts, lends to his neighbors and pays the debts of a family that has fallen behind is the man the town admires. Standing rises with that giving. A child is born a townsperson (_kilawash_); a family that gives feasts and pays its way rises toward the chief's kin (_hilaqash_), the lineage each town takes its chiefs from; and the chief (_hilaq_) is the richest and most generous person in the town, because the office requires both.

Failure is debt. A man who cannot pay what he owes goes into his creditor's house as a bond-worker (_tutuq_) and works there until the debt is paid, and his family may pledge a son or a daughter in his place. A bond-worker keeps his crossing strand and his name, and he goes home the day the count is square, but every town knows whose house he works in. Below the bond-workers are the exiled, put out of their town for a wrong no payment covers.

The Shell-Bead People value most the bead, the canoe, the count, the order of the sky, their own town, and a long crossing strand. They value least war, which they hold to be what poor peoples do because they have nothing to pay with, and the work of the acorn. A coastal family eats acorn meal every day and pays the valleys for it, and still calls a fool a _kowiqash_, an acorn person.

## The Man Admired

[Sitiqnush]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Star Walking, is a canoe master (_alkulay_) of the Canoe Kin at [[place-tikoqhipay|Tikoq Hipay]], the northernmost town of the coast, and the coast towns name him when they say what a man should be. Three times a year he takes his canoe north along the coast, past the last town and into the Cedar People's waters, with a crew of eight and a cargo of beads. He goes to the Cedar People's villages and buys back captives, people of the coast and people of the valleys alike, at whatever price the Cedar People ask, and brings them home. In twelve years he has brought back sixty-one people, and most of them were no kin of his.

The coast admires three things in him. He crosses rough water in a sewn canoe, which is the bravest thing a man of the coast can do. He bargains hard and pays exactly, so that no Cedar chief can say the Shell-Bead People cheat. And he spends his beads on strangers, keeping the count for the whole coast. His cost is plain. Sitiqnush is poor: his canoe is old, his own crossing strand is short, and his wife's family complains of it in public. The chief of Tikoq Hipay praises him at every feast and has never paid toward a voyage.

## The Woman Admired

[Ayulchim]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Moon Rising, is the counter (_alpilik_) of [[place-shuqwil|Shuqwil]], the bead-drilling town on Bead Island, and the women of the coast name her when they say what a woman should be. She has drilled beads since she was seven and taught more than a hundred girls to drill them, and her strands are the ones the island measures against; a full-length strand is called "an Ayulchim strand" on every beach of the coast. When the chief of Shuqwil died in a coughing sickness, leaving a daughter of nine, Ayulchim held the town's beads for three winters, paid its debts, gave its feasts in the child's name, and at the child's first feast as chief counted every bead back to her in front of the town. The count came out to the bead.

Her admiration is a different thing from Sitiqnush's. He spends beads boldly, and she keeps them exactly; he crosses the sea, and she holds a town together on land. The Shell-Bead People say that a count is only as good as the one who keeps it, and Ayulchim is the reason the island's beads are trusted on the mainland. Her cost is that she has never been anything but a keeper. She held a chief's beads for three years and has none to speak of herself.

## What the Towns Keep

The Shell-Bead People write nothing down. What holds them together is strung, sewn, painted or remembered.

- **The bead** (_shuq_) is the first of their artifacts: a disk of shell ground smooth and drilled through the middle, smaller than a fingernail. Islanders drill them with stone points on a pump drill, and a good driller makes about one strand a day.
- **The strand** (_hamil_) is the unit of every price. It is a cord of beads long enough to reach once around a grown hand, a little shorter than a forearm. A basket of acorn meal costs about a strand; a good bow costs four; a bride costs ten to forty; a life costs twenty; and a canoe costs a hundred, and only the Canoe Kin may buy one.
- **The [Measure of Kulaywil]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}** is a cord of exact length kept by the astronomer-priests at Kulaywil. At every midwinter each town's counter brings his town's measuring cord to be checked against it, and a cord found short is cut and retied in front of the gathered chiefs.
- **The plank canoe** (_kulay_) is the largest thing the coast makes and the most valuable. Its planks are split, shaped and sewn by the Canoe Kin, and the art of fitting them is the Canoe Kin's secret.
- **The painted caves** hold the astronomer-priests' paintings: red and black wheels, suns, stars and the beings of the sky, painted under the dream drink on the walls of rock shelters in the mountains. The largest is [Tishal Hapuq]{.name data-type="place/site"}, the Painted Cave, above [[place-tulishwil|Tulishwil]] in the Oak Valleys. A painting keeps a vision and marks a day. It does not write a word.
- **The crossing strand** (_hipalshuq_) is the beads every person keeps apart from childhood to pay their way to the Far Island after death. It is told below.
- **The chief's storehouse** holds a town's wealth: its beads, its dried fish and its acorns. It stands beside the chief's house, and the town's counter knows what is in it to the bead.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is the sun and the sea. A woman rises before dawn and throws a pinch of acorn meal on the fire for the sun, and the household eats nothing more until the sun is over the mountains. A man going out in a canoe drops one bead into the water past the kelp line, to pay the sea for the crossing; a man on foot leaves a few seeds at the feathered pole that stands on every hill above every town. Men sweat in the town's earth-covered sweathouse most evenings, and come out to plunge into the sea.

The sky belongs to the astronomer-priests (_altiyup_). They name every day of the year that matters, and nobody launches a canoe, marries or buries on a day they call bad. Once in a life, at coming of age, a boy or a girl takes the dream drink (_lipush_) under an astronomer-priest's eye, sleeps a day and a night, and wakes with a guardian: a bird, a beast or a being of the sky that came in the dream and will stand by them for life. Nobody takes the drink twice except in mortal sickness, because a cup too strong kills.

## The Round of the Year

The astronomer-priests name the days of every festival, and the chiefs give the feasts that go with them.

- **The [Holding of the Sun]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** falls at midwinter, when the sun rises farthest south and seems ready to leave. The astronomer-priests set up a pole hung with feathers and beads at each town's sacred ground and sing over it for four days, and on the fourth morning the sunrise starts back north along the mountains. The chiefs of many towns gather at Kulaywil in those days, every town's counter checks its cord against the Measure, and every debt in every town is counted. It is for holding the sun in the sky and squaring the year's count.
- **The [First Crossing]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** comes in spring, when the winter storms end. The Canoe Kin launch their canoes together at Kulaywil and race to the islands, and the first canoe to beach on Bead Island wins its crew a strand from every chief. It is for opening the sea road.
- **The [Acorn Gathering]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** comes in autumn, when the acorns fall. Coast families walk over the passes to Tulishwil and the other valley towns with beads, dried fish and shell, and go home with acorns, seeds and baskets. Marriages between coast and valley are arranged there, and the young men play the hoop-and-pole game for strands. It is for feeding the coast through the winter and keeping the valleys tied to it.
- **The [Feast of Strands]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** comes every few years in autumn, when a town has enough dead to mourn. The families of everyone who has died since the last one give beads, food and the clothes of the dead; the astronomer-priests sing the name of each dead person; and the clothes are burned. It is for releasing the dead and paying what they still owe, told below.

## Payment, Shame and the Bond

Reward comes from the chief and from the town. A chief gives beads to a hunter who brings in a whale, a driller who finishes a hard order or a herald who carries a dangerous message. A family the town respects is seated near the chief at his feasts, and the herald (_altuku_) names its gifts aloud. A chief's child who shows promise is put forward to the astronomer-priests, which costs the chief a fortune and is the highest honor a family can buy.

Punishment runs from shame to death, in this order. The first is shame: the herald names the fault at the next feast, and the offender must sit through it. The second is payment: the offender pays the wronged party in beads, twice the loss for a theft and twenty strands for a life. The third is bond-work: an offender who cannot pay works as a bond-worker in the wronged party's house until the count is square. The fourth is the loss of the town's goods: the chief refuses the offender a share of his feasts and a seat in the town's canoes. The fifth is exile from the town. The last is death, by the chief's order and at the hands of the chief's kin, for a killing within a family, for stealing a crossing strand, and for guiding raiders to another town.

## The Chief and the Sky Keepers

The Shell-Bead People keep their civil and their religious authority in two institutions that need each other. The chief rules the town. Every canoe that lands on the town's beach pays the chief a bead in ten of its cargo, every household brings the chief a share of its catch and its acorns, and every fine passes through the chief's hands. From that store the chief feeds the poor, gives the feasts, pays the town's debts to other towns and ransoms the town's people from raiders. The chief tries cases with the counter beside them, because the counter knows the debts of every household. The herald carries the chief's word to other towns. A chief may be a man or a woman, and the office passes within the chief's kin: a dying chief names an heir, the herald announces the name, and the town accepts the heir by coming to the heir's first feast.

The astronomer-priests are one sworn society across all the towns, and no chief commands them. Entry costs a fortune in beads, so most of them are the children of chiefs, and every chief's heir is expected to join. They name every child, read the sky, keep the calendar and the Measure, paint the caves, give the dream drink, heal, and judge whether a misfortune is sorcery. Their eldest, the [Sky Elder]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, sits at Kulaywil.

Nobody writes a record. The counter keeps the town's count in memory and recites it at every feast; the astronomer-priests keep the sky's count in memory and on the cave walls. When chief and priests disagree, the calendar and the Measure are the priests' and everything else is the chief's. A quarrel between towns, or between a chief and the priests, waits for the Holding of the Sun, when the chiefs gather at Kulaywil and the Sky Elder hears both sides. Towns rarely fight. When two towns cannot settle, their men meet on a set field, shoot arrows at each other until one man falls, stop, and pay for him.

## The Crossing

The soul (_tuway_) stays near the body for three days after death. Then it walks west along the coast to [[place-hipalhalaq|Hipal Halaq]], the Crossing Shore, on the westernmost headland of the coast, bathes in a pool in the rocks, and goes down to the water at the point. A canoe of the dead waits there, sewn by the dead of the Canoe Kin, and it carries souls across the sea to [Hikiw Siwaq]{.name data-type="lore/cosmology"}, the Far Island, where the dead live in a town with no hunger, no debt and no raiders. The fare is a strand of beads. A soul with no strand to pay must swim, and the swimmers who do not reach the island are the sea lions that bark off the headland.

That belief settles an ordinary day more than any law. Every child is given a first bead by its mother's mother, and begins a crossing strand (_hipalshuq_) with it. A person adds to it all their life, keeps it apart from every other bead, and never spends it, lends it or pledges it; no creditor may seize it, not even from a bond-worker. Stealing one is a killing, punished as a killing. A chief owes a crossing strand to anyone of the town who dies without one, and a chief who has let the town's dead swim is a chief the town replaces. Nobody hunts sea lions within sight of the Crossing Shore.

Debts do not cross. A dying person calls in what is owed to them, pays what they owe, and says aloud before witnesses what remains. At the grave the counter reads out the dead person's count, the creditors are paid from what the dead left, and then the crossing strand is laid on the body and buried with it. An heir takes what is left after the debts. An heir who cannot cover the debts may take them on, or may refuse, and the creditors lose them; most heirs take them on, because a family that refuses is not lent to again.

## Paying for the Dead

The living can still pay a dead person's fare, through the astronomer-priests. At the Feast of Strands a family that fears its dead had too short a strand pays the priests to sing the dead person's name, and the priests take a strand for every name sung and throw a strand into the sea at the Crossing Shore for every name they were paid to send. The swimmer is then counted as carried. The priests are rich from this work, and nobody disputes that they should be paid for it.

The dead reach the living through dreams. A sleeper who hears sea lions barking in a dream owes something to one of the dead, a debt unpaid or a strand too short, and the astronomer-priests are asked whose. The answer always costs beads.

## What a Person Owes

Ask one of the Shell-Bead People what a person owes, and the answer comes as a count: what they borrow, returned at the time agreed; to the chief, a bead in ten of every cargo landed and a share of every catch and harvest; to the kin, a feast at every marriage and every death; to the dead, a crossing strand kept whole; to the sky, a pinch of meal at dawn and a bead to the sea at every crossing. A person owes the town their peace: a quarrel goes to the chief, never to a club.

In return a person is owed food from the chief's storehouse in a bad year, ransom if raiders take them, a crossing strand if they die without one, a hearing before the chief and the counter, a share of the chief's feasts, and payment in full for any wrong done to them. The count runs both ways, and a chief who will not pay what the town is owed loses the town.

## The Weak

The household is the first resort and the chief the second. A widow keeps her own beads, which a woman always holds apart from her husband's, and she keeps her house, which belongs to her family. A widower goes back to his mother's or his sister's house. An orphan goes to its mother's kin, and if the kin cannot feed it the chief takes it into his house, where it works and is fed until it is grown; the chief owes it that by right. A household that cannot pay its debts sends a member to work as a bond-worker, and this is the commonest way the poor are kept: fed, housed and working, in someone else's house, for years.

A stranger who reaches a town is fed once at the chief's house and asked his business. A stranger with no kin on the coast and nothing to trade is fed and sent on, and the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]]'s pipe gives him no standing here.

## Those Who Cannot Work

The Canoe Kin keep the only common store on the coast. Every voyage puts a share of its cargo into the brotherhood's store, and a member drowned, crippled or too old to paddle is paid from it for life, and so are his widow and his children. Everyone else depends on kin and chief. A man who cannot paddle or hunt sits at the bead drill, and a woman who cannot walk to the oaks weaves baskets; a blind elder sorts beads by touch, because a good bead and a bad one feel different under the thumb. The old keep counts, teach drilling and weaving, and sing the long songs at the feasts.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household's house, and past it the chief's. Teaching is the family's work and then the guild's: a child learns to fish, gather and drill from its parents, a son of the Canoe Kin learns to sew a canoe from his father's brothers, a girl of the valleys learns to weave from her grandmothers, and a chief's child who joins the astronomer-priests learns the sky from them over many years.

Defense is weak, and the Shell-Bead People know it. Their towns have no walls except at Tikoq Hipay, which faces the north. They fight with sinew-backed bows and stone-tipped arrows, spears and hardwood clubs, and they have almost no copper, which the Cedar People bring down the coast only in trade. Watchers on the headlands send up smoke when war canoes appear in the north, and the people of the towns take their beads and run for the hills. The northern towns pay the raiders not to come, as told below. Against southern steel they have no answer at all, and most of them have never heard of it.

## The House

The household lives in a domed house (_hukay_) of willow poles thatched with reeds, wide enough for fifteen to twenty people around one fire. It holds a married couple, their unmarried children, the wife's parents and often a married daughter and her husband. A new husband lives a year in his wife's parents' house and works for them; afterward the couple choose where to live, and most stay. A chief takes several wives, each from the chief's kin of another town, and each marriage binds two towns: the wives' towns trade with the chief's town first and do not fight it. Children belong to both parents' families. A chief's office passes within the chief's kin, and the Canoe Kin's membership passes from father to son.

The age ladder is plain. An infant is named on its fourth day by an astronomer-priest, who reads the sky of the birth night and gives a name that joins a being of the sky to an act, such as Moon Rising or Star Walking; a family that dislikes the name may pay for another, and the priest's second reading is said to be kinder. A child gets its first bead and is set to gathering shellfish and acorns as soon as it can walk. At coming of age a youth takes the dream drink; a girl of a drilling family takes her first bead order, and a boy of the Canoe Kin paddles to the islands and back with his father's crew. Adults fish, drill, gather, weave, trade and marry. The old count, teach and sing. The dying call in their debts and say aloud what they owe. The dead walk west to the Crossing Shore and pay their way over.

## The Pointing and the Count

The sin the Shell-Bead People committed is the [Pointing]{.name data-type="lore/history"}. In the lifetime of the present elders' great-grandparents the Cedar People's war canoes began to come south every few summers, and each time they took people from the northern coast towns. [Hiluqchim]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Sun Rising, chief of Tikoq Hipay, met the Cedar captains on his beach and made an arrangement. He paid them a hundred strands, and his herald guided their canoes past the coast towns and up the rivers into the Oak Valleys, where the villages had no chiefs rich enough to pay and no watchers on the headlands. The raiders took several hundred valley people that summer. The chiefs of the other northern towns paid shares of the strands, and none of them refused.

The arrangement became the [Northern Strand]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}. Every spring since, the chiefs of the northern towns send the Cedar People a cargo of beads, and the Cedar war canoes leave the coast towns alone. The guiding stopped when Hiluqchim died. The raids on the valleys did not. The valley people's own name for themselves is _tulishash_, the oak people; they remember the Pointing as the coast's betrayal of its own kin, and a valley village that sees a coast canoe in a river mouth in spring still sends its children into the hills. [Kowiqnaq]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Acorn Seed, a basket maker (_alsiliq_) of a valley village, led the villages of her valley into the hills the summer after the Pointing and set the watch fires that the valley towns still light. The valleys count her as their hero, and the coast towns have come to count her as one of theirs.

The blind spot is still there, and thoughtful Shuqash argue over it. The coast holds that anything can be settled with a count: a theft, a killing, a war between towns, a raid from the north. That belief keeps the peace between sixty towns that answer to no one, and the coast is proud of it. It also prices people. A debtor's child works off the debt in another's house, a valley village that cannot pay is the one the raiders find, and the Northern Strand buys the coast's safety with the valleys' risk every spring. Some chiefs hold that the Strand is the count working as it should, and that the valleys should pay their share and be covered. Others answer that the Pointing put a price on the valley people that no one ever agreed to, and that a count the counted never agreed to is a short strand.

A second argument runs alongside it. The Turquoise People's traders say that the compact makes kin of everyone who smokes its pipe, and that pipe kin of the compact owe each other safe passage, the Cedar People included. If the Shell-Bead People joined, the raids would have to stop. The chiefs answer that the compact would ask for warriors against the south, a people they have never seen, and that the Strand costs less. The young men who paddle with Sitiqnush say that nobody has ever asked the valleys which costs less.
