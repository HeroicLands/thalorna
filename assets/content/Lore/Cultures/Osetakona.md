The Maple People live among the great lakes and the maple forests of the northeast, and everything they value is done in the open, talked over and agreed. They call themselves the [[affiliation-osetakona|Osetakona]], the people of the maple, and picture their league of eight nations as a sugar bush, the Grove (_osetaten_): many trees, each tapped in the thaw, whose sap goes into one kettle and boils down to one sugar. About 750,000 of them live in palisaded villages of bark longhouses that move every ten to twenty years, when the fields wear out and the firewood is a long walk away. Their women own the houses and the fields, and their clan mothers name the men who speak in council. The south calls them _Shik'ul Ch'akal_, after their lakes, and its traders call them the Otter People, after the otters carved on their canoe prows.

## Where They Came From

The rememberers begin the story in the Bitter Winters, before the Grove, when every nation of the lakes was at war with its neighbours and no one could say who had begun it. A death in one nation left a name lying down, and the dead person's clan sent its young men to take a captive from the next nation to raise the name. Every captive taken was a name laid down on the other side, and the other side came back for its own. The rememberers say that the raids went on so long that the women planted their fields with men standing guard, and that a village moved more often for fear than for firewood.

The Grove began at a sugar camp. [Kiontsarosta]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, whose name means Boils the Sap, was a clan mother of the Falls People, and in one thaw her village's young men were readying a mourning war against the Rapids People downriver. She sent them back to the sap. In their place she sent her brother's son, [Siwenkatiro]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Sweet Words, unarmed, carrying a kettle of that spring's sugar to the Rapids People's sugar camp. He set it down before their clan mothers and offered it for their dead. The rememberers say he spoke from the morning the sap ran until the evening the leaves came out, and that the Rapids clan mothers took the sugar at the end of it. That autumn the two nations held one Feast of Bones together and laid their dead in one pit. The Maple People still say it plainly: a nation cannot make war on a nation whose dead lie in its own grave.

Other nations joined one by one, each with its own sugar and its own feast, until the Grove held eight: the Falls People, the Rapids People, the Copper People of the western lakes, the Dawn People of the eastern shore, the River People of the southwest, the Pine People of the north, the Birch People of the western forest and the Beaver People of the inland marshes. A ninth nation, the Island People, did not join, and what the Grove did to them is told below. The rememberers count eleven generations from the first sugar.

## The Open Word

**Good is what is done in the open: the word said before the council, the promise kept, the pot shared with whoever walks in. Evil is what is done in secret: the whisper, the private bargain, the food hidden from the house, and the harm worked by someone who will not show their face.** The Maple People hold that a thing said before everyone can be answered, weighed and agreed to, and that a thing said in secret cannot. A secret is not wicked in itself, but it is always suspected.

The worst evil they know is witchcraft, which they understand as harm done in secret: a sickness sent, a curse spoken under the breath, a hunter's luck stolen out of envy. A witch is the person who hides, and a person who hides too much is asked why. The same reasoning makes their politics. A king decides alone, and so the Maple People hold that a king is a witch with a council's power, and they say so to the One Fire's Suns at every council of the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]].

Grief sits between the two. A death is no one's fault, but the grief it leaves (_otsenta_) is dangerous, because grief held too long turns bitter and bitterness turns to killing. The Maple People set grief down on purpose: with words spoken over the mourners, with gifts, and with sugar. To settle a death between clans is to sweeten it (_siwate_), and the word covers everything from a cake of maple sugar to a payment of furs, beads and copper large enough to empty a longhouse.

## Being Listened To

Success is being listened to. A man whose word is asked for at his longhouse fire, a woman whose opinion the clan mother seeks before she decides, and a speaker whose council waits for him to finish have all succeeded. Standing comes through the clan and its names. Everyone born or adopted into a clan is clan kin (_atiakwahen_). A man or woman past the prime of work and war is an elder (_kahsta_), whose counsel the clan asks before it acts. The clan mothers are chosen from the women elders, and the speakers are named by them.

Failure is being talked about rather than listened to. A man who boasts, a woman who hoards, and anyone caught in a lie lose standing in the slowest and most public way: the village stops waiting for them to finish. At the bottom are those without a name (_tsahsiren_), whose clans have taken their names back. A nameless person has no longhouse, no fire, no claim on anyone's pot and no one to avenge him.

The Maple People value most the open word, the agreement everyone has reached, the shared pot, the clan and its names, and the peace of the Grove. They value least secrecy, haste, hoarding and command. No one among them may tell another grown person what to do, and a One Fire noble who gives an order in a Maple village is answered with silence and, if he goes on, with laughter.

## The Man Admired

[Skahniotiaro]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Elk clan is a speaker of the Birch People in the western forest, and the western villages name him when they say what a man should be. He was born among the Grass People and taken in a mourning war at nine. An Elk clan mother whose son had drowned that spring adopted him, raised her son's name onto him, and he became her son in full: he hunted for her longhouse, cleared her daughters' fields, and grew up speaking two tongues. Six years ago she named him her clan's speaker.

The western villages admire three things in him, and all three are the open word in practice. He keeps nothing: every hide he brings home goes to the longhouse, and he is the poorest man in his village and the best fed. He carries the council's word without adding to it, and he has never been caught saying more than was agreed. And he has twice carried a compact pipe to his birth people, standing in their camp as a Maple speaker and talking in their own tongue, and twice brought back a truce. His cost is that no one, including Skahniotiaro, can say which people he would stand with if a truce failed. Some in his village still watch him, after thirty years.

## The Woman Admired

[Tsiorhensiwen]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Loon clan is the clan mother of [Tsiorhenten]{.name data-type="place/settlement"}, the Dawn Place, a village of the Dawn People on the eastern shore. Last summer a boat of men came out of the sea, men of a kind no one in the Grove has a name for, and fell on her village. They were fought off the shore, but they burned the longhouses nearest the water, killed six of her people and carried four away over the sea.

The village's young men demanded a mourning war to raise the six names. There was no one to take captives from. The men from the water had gone back into the sea, and the only people within reach were the hunting bands of the northern woods, who had done the Dawn People no harm. Tsiorhensiwen refused the war. She raised two of the six names onto children born that winter and left the other four lying down until there was a better answer. The four who were taken she did not count as dead, and she has kept their names, neither lying down nor given, in a way no clan mother of the Grove has had to before. Then she moved the village inland, a year early, out of sight of the sea.

The women of the Dawn People name her when they say what a woman should be. Skahniotiaro keeps the open word in the council, and she keeps the clan's names, which are the clan itself, and she would not spend them on a war against the wrong people. Her cost is plain. Her longhouses are short of hands, the young men call her old and cold, and the Dawn People's council has not stopped arguing about her.

## What the Rememberers Keep

The Maple People write nothing down, and what holds them together is spoken, strung or carried.

- **The Founding** is the first thing a rememberer learns and the first thing recited at every great council: Siwenkatiro's speech at the Rapids People's sugar camp, which no rememberer has ever finished in less than a day.
- **The memory belts** (_ohniakaro_) are belts of white and purple shell beads strung in rows. Each holds one agreement of the Grove or the compact. A rememberer reads it by its patterns, a white road for a path kept open, a purple diamond for each nation that agreed, and recites the agreement from memory.
- **The names** are a clan's true property. Each clan holds a stock of names, lent to one living person at a time and given again when that person dies. The speakers' titles are names too, and the oldest of them have been carried by forty men.
- **The copper of office** (_tsikenha katiro_) is a plate of hammered copper a speaker wears at his throat while he speaks for his clan. When a clan mother takes back a speaker's title she takes back the copper with it.
- **The kettle** is in every longhouse and is the oldest of their figures. A council is a boiling (_rostahsa_), and the great council sits at the Kettle (_ostakaten_), whichever village holds it.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is the pot and the fire. The first spoonful of every meal goes into the fire for the dead of the house, who are still near and still hungry. The corn soup (_ohsterio_) is always on, and anyone who walks into a longhouse is fed from it before anyone asks what they want. A woman spends her day in the fields with her sisters and her daughters, hoeing the hills where corn, beans and squash grow together; a man spends his away from the village, hunting, fishing, trading by canoe or clearing the ground for the next village site.

Dreams are the other daily practice. The Maple People hold that a dream (_kahsentsia_) is the soul (_ohnionsaro_) saying what it wants, and a want unmet makes the soul sick and then the body. A person who wakes from a strong dream tells it at the fire, and the household does what it can to give the dreamer what the dream asked: a food, a visit, a name, a gift. A dream nobody can read goes to the healers (_ehniatehen_), the society of those who were once sick and were healed, who read it and answer it.

## The Round of the Year

The clan mothers and the ceremony keepers (_otekwahen_) name the days of the rites, and the year turns on the maples and the corn.

- **The Sap Feast** (_kiontsa ohnerakwa_) comes at the thaw, when the clan mothers tap the first maple. The first sap boiled goes to the elders and into the fire for the dead. It is for beginning the year, and every council of the Grove opens in the weeks after it, when the sugar is made.
- **The Planting** follows, when the clan mothers bless the seed and the women plant the hills together. It is for the fields.
- **The Berry Feast** comes with the first strawberries. The healers lead it, and every household drinks berry water. It is for health, and for thanking the healers.
- **The Green Corn** comes when the first ears are ready. The clan mothers name the children born since the Long Night, quarrels are set down, and debts between longhouses are paid. It is for the names and for clearing the year's grudges.
- **The Long Night** falls at midwinter and lasts for days. People go from longhouse to longhouse telling their dreams in riddles, and the village tries to guess each dream and give the dreamer what it asked. The clan mothers name the children born since the Green Corn. It is for answering the souls and starting the new year clear.
- **The Feast of Bones** (_kahiotsi ohnerakwa_) comes once in a village's life, when it moves, and is told below. It is for setting the dead free.

## Gifts, Talk and the Taking of Names

Reward comes as regard, gifts and names. A child who learns well is praised at the fire. A hunter who keeps nothing back is given gifts at the feasts. A man or woman the clan honours may have a great name raised onto them, the name of a famous speaker or a remembered clan mother, and carries its standing for life. The highest reward is the copper of a speaker, and after it a place in a rememberer's recitation: a person whose deed is woven into a belt is spoken of at every great council for as long as the belt lasts.

Punishment runs from talk to death, in this order. The first is talk: the village speaks about the offender and stops waiting for him to finish. The second is the clan mother's warning, given twice in private and once before the whole clan. The third is payment: a wrong between clans is sweetened, and a killing within the Grove is paid with ten belts' worth of furs, beads and copper for a man and fifteen for a woman, because a woman's death takes the children she would have carried. The fourth is the loss of office: a speaker who disobeys his clan mother loses his title and his copper. The fifth is the loss of one's name. A person whose clan takes back his name is nameless, and must leave or live on charity no one owes him. The last is death at the hands of one's own clan, for a proven witch or a traitor to the Grove; the clan kills its own so that no other clan has to, and no feud follows.

## The Mothers Name Both

The Maple People have two kinds of authority and one source for both. The clan mothers (_kontiesa_) name the speakers (_katirohen_), who sit in council and govern, and they name the ceremony keepers (_otekwahen_), men and women who keep the rites of the year. A clan mother can take either title back.

A quarrel within a longhouse goes to its eldest woman, and a quarrel within a clan to its clan mother. A quarrel between clans goes to the village council, where the speakers of both clans negotiate the sweetening. A quarrel between villages goes to the nation's council, and a quarrel between nations to the great council of the Grove at the Kettle. Every council decides by agreement: nothing is done until everyone present has spoken, been answered and agreed, and a hard question can take days. The rememberers (_sakwentahen_) keep the records, in memory and in the belts. Nobody collects anything. Each longhouse feeds its own guests, the host village feeds a council, and the Grove has no storehouse and no tribute.

When the council and the ceremony keepers disagree, over whether a feast must be held in a hard year or whether a war can begin before a rite is finished, the clan mothers decide, because they named both.

## Where the Dead Wait

The dead stay near the village until it moves. A person who dies is buried in a grave outside the palisade, or laid on a scaffold if the ground is frozen, and the soul stays close: it eats from the fire, it speaks in the dreams of its kin, and it can be hurt by neglect. When the village moves, every ten to twenty years, the dead move too. At the Feast of Bones (_kahiotsi ohnerakwa_) the women of each clan dig up their dead, clean the bones, wrap them in the family's best beaver robes, and lay them, with copper, beads and furs, in one great pit (_kahiotsiten_) at the old site. Then the souls are free, and they walk west along the river of stars (_tsikahse ohiatse_) to the village of the dead, where they live in longhouses as they did before.

That belief settles an ordinary day. A family spends its best furs, its copper and its beads on its dead, and it does so all at once, every ten or twenty years, so that no longhouse holds much for long. A chief of the Grove may be buried with a copper bell that crossed the southern desert before he was born, because his family put the best thing it had into the pit. Feuds are settled before a Feast of Bones, because the dead of every family in the village are laid in one grave together, and enemies' bones lie touching. And a village is one family in the end, which is the lesson of the Grove's founding.

## Raising the Names

The living reach the dead through the fire, the dreams and the names. A household feeds its dead at every meal until the Feast of Bones. A dream sent by a dead kinsman is answered like any other dream, and the healers read the hard ones; a household pays them in tobacco, sugar or a fur, according to the work.

The names matter most. A dead person's name lies down until it is raised onto someone new, and until then the clan is a person short and the dead person's place at the fire is empty. A clan mother raises a name onto a child at the next naming feast, or onto an adopted captive, who takes the dead person's place in the house with the name: a captive may become a son, a brother or a husband in a single afternoon. A clan with too many names lying down after a sickness or a war asks for a mourning war (_otsenta riakse_), and its young men go out to take captives to raise them. The clan mothers decide each captive's fate. A captive they adopt is kin in full. A captive they refuse is put to death by the family whose dead he was meant to replace.

## What a Person Owes

Ask one of the Maple People what a person owes, and the answer comes in order: to say what you mean in the open, before the people it concerns; to feed whoever walks into your longhouse; to bring every kill and every harvest to your mother's house, or your wife's; to clear the land and build the houses if you are a man, and to plant the fields and keep the seed if you are a woman; to avenge or replace your clan's dead; to keep the peace of the Grove and sweeten a killing, never repay it; and to go to council when your clan sends you.

In return a person is owed a place at a fire, a share of the pot, a name from the clan, a voice in council through the clan's speaker, revenge or replacement if killed, a place in the pit at the Feast of Bones, and one thing more: that no one will ever give them an order.

## The Weak

The longhouse is the first and nearly the last resort. A hungry person in a standing longhouse shames the whole house, so in practice there is no one destitute while a clan stands. A widow keeps her house, her fields and her children, because they were never her husband's. A widower goes back to his mother's or his sister's longhouse. An orphan belongs to its mother's clan, and the clan mother sets it at a sister's fire without being asked. These are duties, not kindnesses, and a clan mother who neglects one is talked about at every fire in the village.

A stranger who walks into a longhouse is fed once, without question, and then asked what he wants. A stranger who carries a compact pipe is owed food, shelter and a guide to the next village. The duty stops at the edge of the clan. A captive the clan mothers have not yet adopted and a person whose name has been taken back are owed nothing, and in a bad winter they are the ones who go hungry.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot hunt or hoe is kept at the fire and given work that suits them. A lame hunter makes nets and bows and bends the frames of canoes. An old woman who cannot hoe keeps the seed corn, minds the children and teaches the girls the clan's names. A blind elder learns the belts, because a belt is read by the fingers as well as the eyes, and many of the Grove's rememberers are blind. A man who cannot do any of this is still fed, because the pot is the longhouse's and not his.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defence

Shelter is the longhouse, and past it the clan. A longhouse that burns is divided among the clan's other houses in the village until the men raise a new one, which takes a summer.

Teaching belongs to the clan. A boy is taught to hunt, fight and speak by his mother's brother, and a girl to plant and keep a house by her grandmothers, who also teach her the clan's names and whose they were. A child who listens well may be given to a rememberer to learn the belts, a training of many years. Defence is the palisade, double in the border villages, and every man of the village behind it. A war leader (_rotsikwen_) raises a party for a raid and leads it only while it is out. A nation goes to war when its council agrees, and the whole Grove only when the great council agrees, which it has done four times in eleven generations.

## The Longhouse and the Clan

The household is a longhouse: a bark house up to two hundred feet long, home to one line of women, their husbands and their children, with a fire down the middle for every two families and the clan's animal carved over the door. A man marries into his wife's longhouse and hunts for it, but his clan stays his mother's, and his authority is over his sister's children, not his own. A woman who wants to end a marriage sets her husband's things outside the door. Every clan is found in every nation of the Grove, so an Otter woman of the Copper People and an Otter man of the Dawn People are kin and may not marry, though they live a month's paddle apart.

The age ladder is plain. An infant rides on a cradleboard and has no name until the next naming feast, when the clan mother gives it a child's name from the names lying down. A child fetches water and firewood, runs messages and plays the stick-ball game. At youth a boy goes alone into the forest to fast until he dreams, and comes back to an adult name and his first hunt; a girl takes her adult name at the first planting after her first blood. Young men are away more than they are home, hunting, trading and raiding; young women plant, and choose husbands with their mothers' advice. Adults keep the longhouse and the fields, and the men a clan mother names speak in council. Elders advise, and the eldest women become clan mothers. The dying tell their last dreams and say who should carry their names. The dead wait outside the palisade for the Feast of Bones.

## The Grafting and the Titles

The sin the Maple People committed is what their rememberers call the Grafting and the descendants of its victims call the Breaking. Seven generations ago the Island People lived on the [Lake of Islands]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, between the Pine People and the Falls People, and they refused the Grove three times. Their young men raided the Pine People's villages. The great council agreed, after a winter of talk, to bring the Island People in. Over three winters the Grove's war parties burned the island villages and killed the men who fought. The clan mothers divided the women and children among the eight nations, so that no two Island sisters lived in the same longhouse and no Island village was ever built again. Their names were laid down and Grove names raised onto them, and their tongue survives in a few words for lake fish and island birds.

The last Island clan mother, [Kwentsatsiotie]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, was adopted into a Falls People longhouse of the Crane clan. She taught her granddaughters the Island songs at night, in secret, in a people that counts secrecy as the root of evil. Her descendants, most of the Crane clan of the Falls People, recite the Breaking at every Feast of Bones. The rememberers recite the Grafting at every great council. The belt that records it has one row the rememberers read as "joined" and the Crane clan mothers read as "broken", and the great council has never agreed which it says.

The blind spot is in the titles, and thoughtful Maple People argue over it. They despise hereditary rule, and every speaker's title in the Grove is a name owned by one line of one clan, filled only from that line's sons and nephews. The clan mother chooses the man, and can take his title back, and that is the whole of the difference from the One Fire, where a Sun's mother's line also decides who rules. Defenders answer that a Sun cannot be unseated and a speaker can be, and that the choice is the clan mother's, not the dead man's. Critics answer that the choice is always among the same few longhouses, and that a Crane woman of Island blood has never held a title that mattered.

A second argument runs alongside it. The Maple People condemn the One Fire for the attendants who die at a Sun's burial. The One Fire's delegates answer at the Red Quarry by asking what becomes of the captives the Grove's clan mothers decide not to adopt. Some Maple People have no answer to that, and say so.

## The Men from the Water

The newest question in the Grove came out of the eastern sea. Twenty-one summers ago the Dawn People watched a strange boat stand off their shore and turn away. Last summer a boat came again and fell on Tsiorhenten. The council argues whether the two boats carried the same men, and nobody in the Grove has a name for them. Meanwhile the four people they carried off are somewhere over the sea, and the Maple People know better than anyone what happens to a captive: he is adopted, or he is not.

The Dawn People have asked the Grove for help, and the great council is still boiling it down. The Grove has asked the Red Stone Compact for help in turn, and the Grass People, who have never seen the sea, have asked why they should give it. Two other troubles press at the same time. In the north, hunting bands driven out of the far woods by newcomers from the ice are moving into the Pine People's country, and the Pine People argue over whether to adopt them, trade with them or drive them off. In the southwest the River People's villages push down the valleys of [[place-hanalxilanrgn|Ha'nal Shi'lan]], and the One Fire's war parties burn them. The Maple People are the compact's diplomats and its memory, and they say that the compact exists for exactly this. The other nations note that the Maple People say so most loudly when the danger is theirs.
