**The Oath at the Scales**, _zhepulu semboling mbedoling_, is a tale of [[place-kaljekor|Kaljekor]], the city of the Golden Flame, and of [The Market of Flames]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, the yearly trade festival at which oaths sworn on [The Scales of Kaljekor]{.name data-type="miscgear"} are held unbreakable. The Scales are a gilded balance in the temple-marketplace, used to weigh offerings and seal trade oaths, and a merchant who swears falsely on them is said to be ruined within a year and a day. Every Okharin knows the saying. The tale asks the question the saying leaves open, which is what counts as swearing falsely, and it answers it the way Kaljekor answers most questions, by weighing.

The [Flameweavers]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"} keep the records of the oaths sworn on the Scales, and their clerks tell this tale every year on the night before the Market opens, in the colonnade under the beam, to the apprentices of [[affiliation-crwntrdskhrs|the Crown Trades]] who will stand at the market for the first time in the morning. It is a comic tale, and the apprentices laugh in the right places, and by the end of it they have learned how an oath is sworn at Kaljekor, what the Flameweavers write down, and the saying every trader at the Market of Flames says before a weighing.

The teller here is **Ruko**, an old clerk of the Flameweavers, who has told it to forty years of apprentices and has stopped pretending that the salt-trader in it is not the hero. The herdswoman in it is a [[affiliation-talumengtrb|Tálumeng]] widow, and the Tálumeng tell the same tale with her as its hero, to the same end. Ruko gives the oath formula in Okharik, as it is sworn at the beam.

## The Telling

Sit down where you can see the beam. No, not on the step, the step is for the priests. Behind the pillar. That is where I sat, forty years ago, and the clerk who told it to me told it from where I am sitting now.

Look at it. It is a balance. That is all it is. Two pans, a beam, a post, and the gold on the beam, which is real gold, and you will be told that it is real gold more times than you want tomorrow, by people who want you to trust them. The Servants of Gold weigh the offerings on it in the morning before the market opens. After that it is the market's, and anyone with a bargain to seal can bring it to the beam and swear on it, and a Flameweaver writes it down. That is our part. We write it down. A year and a day later we look at what we wrote, and we look at the man who swore it, and we write that down too.

You know the saying. A man who swears falsely on the Scales is ruined within a year and a day. You have heard it since you were small. What nobody told you when you were small is that the Flameweavers have the records, and the records go back further than the pyramid's newest stair, and every false oath in them is followed, a year and a day later, by a line in the same hand that says what became of the man. I have read a great many of those lines. They are short. "Gone to the coast." "His house sold to the temple." "His sons trading under their mother's name." "Dead in the river." Read them and you will not swear falsely. Most of you will not read them. So I tell you this instead, which is the case of **Nkamu the salt-trader** and **Ngomi of the Tálumeng**, and what the Scales did with them.

Nkamu was a trader of this city. His house is still standing, on the street of the salt-sellers behind the gold-workers, and the family that lives in it now is no kin of his and does not like to be asked. He traded salt. You know where salt comes from. It comes from the coast and the pans, a long way off, on donkeys, in sacks, and it comes up the road to Kaljekor because everyone in Okharis needs it and nobody in the grazing country has it. A herd needs salt. A cow with no salt licks the sweat off the herdsman's arm and chews the leather off his sandals, and in the end it chews the rope it is tied with. So the herders come in to the Market of Flames for salt every year, and they pay for it in what they have, which is cattle.

Salt is sold by weight. You will sell a great many things by weight tomorrow. Remember what follows.

Nkamu had a trick, and he was not the first to have it, and he was not the last, and some of you will be offered it before the market closes. On the night before he took his salt to the beam, he opened the sacks in his yard and sprinkled them with water out of a jar, not much, the way a woman sprinkles a floor before she sweeps it. He did it in the yard because the yard was cool. By the morning the salt had drunk the water, and a sack that had weighed sixty measures at the coast weighed sixty-eight at the beam, and nobody looking at it could tell. Salt does not look wet. It looks like salt. It clumps a little, and Nkamu said that was the sea air.

You see it. Eight measures of water, sold as salt. The buyer takes it home, and in the dry of the grazing country it gives the water back to the air, and in a month he has sixty measures of salt that he paid sixty-eight for, and he does not know where the eight went. He thinks the cattle ate it. Nkamu sold water at the price of salt for nine years, and he grew a second story on his house with it.

Now hear the oath. This is the part you are here for.

At the beam a buyer and a seller come together, with the goods, and one of the Servants of Gold stands at the post. The goods go in one pan, and the weights go in the other, the city's weights, the ones with the flame cut in them, until the beam is level. The Servant says the weight aloud. Then the seller puts his right hand flat on the beam, on the gold, and says the oath, and the buyer puts his hand on the beam beside the seller's and says it back, and a Flameweaver writes it. The oath is always the same. It goes like this. Say it with me, you will need it in the morning.

"On the beam, before the Flame. This weighs what the beam says. It is yours for what I am given. The Flame is between us."

In the old tongue the priests use, it is shorter: _mbathel mbedolu, mbanor nkaru. tebu mbedom ntayi mbedolu zhepom. tebu thumom ndemin mbon ntayi ngem ndalom. nkaru thumom mbethenel ngemi._

Hear what it says. _This weighs what the beam says._ It does not say _this is salt_. It does not say _this is dry_. It says the thing weighs what the beam says it weighs, and when Nkamu put his hand on the gold over sixty-eight measures of wet salt, that was true. The beam said sixty-eight. The sack weighed sixty-eight. He swore it with a clear face nine years running, and nine years running a year and a day went by, and the Flameweavers looked in the records and wrote, beside his name, "Trading." So he was not ruined. So, said Nkamu, he had not sworn falsely. So the Flame did not mind wet salt.

You will hear men argue the same thing at the market tomorrow. Remember Ngomi.

Ngomi of the Tálumeng was a widow with a herd. Her husband had died the year before, of a fall, and she had kept his herd together, which a widow is not always let do, because she had sons too young to take it and brothers-in-law who wanted it. She kept it by counting it at the gate every evening in front of the brothers-in-law, so they could see it was all there. The Tálumeng still tell how she did it. That is not this tale.

That year she came in to the Market of Flames for salt. She came with a heifer to pay with, a good red heifer, two years old, which she had picked out of the herd because it was the one beast she could spare that would fetch the salt she needed. She came to Nkamu because Nkamu's stall was nearest the gate, which is where the herders come in, and he knew it, which is why his stall was there.

Now. Some of you are from the grazing country. You know what I am going to say. The rest of you, listen.

The morning Ngomi brought the heifer in, she did not come straight to the market. She came by the river. The road from the grazing comes into Kaljekor along the river for the last part of the way, and there is a place below the walls where the bank is low and the cattle can get down to the water. She took the heifer down there and let it drink. Then she walked it up and down on the bank a while in the sun, to make it thirsty, and took it down and let it drink again. She did that from first light until the market horn. You know how much a heifer drinks. By the time she brought it through the gate, its sides were out like a water-skin and it walked as if it were carrying a child.

And cattle at the Market of Flames are weighed. Not on the Scales, there is no pan big enough. They are weighed on the cattle-beam outside the east gate, the big one with the ropes, against the city's stone weights. The cattle-beam is not gold, but the oath on it is the same oath, and a Flameweaver writes that one too, and the records keep it on the same leaf as the Scales.

So this is what happened. Ngomi brought her heifer to Nkamu's stall and asked his price for salt. He said so many measures of salt for so much weight of cattle, which is how the trade goes, a weight for a weight. She said that was fair. They took the heifer to the cattle-beam at the east gate, and it weighed what it weighed, which was a great deal, because a great deal of it was river. They took the salt to the Scales, and it weighed what it weighed, which was a great deal, because some of it was the jar in Nkamu's yard. The weights matched. Nkamu put his hand on the gold and swore. Ngomi put her hand beside his and swore it back. And a Flameweaver wrote it down, both oaths, on one leaf, and it was the clerk who taught me, a young man then, who wrote it.

He told me he looked at the heifer and looked at the salt and knew. He had been a herdsman's boy. He knew a watered beast when he saw one. And he had been a clerk at the beam three years, and he knew wet salt. He said he wrote both oaths and said nothing, because it is not a clerk's business to say anything at the beam. It is a clerk's business to write.

Ngomi took the salt home to the grazing. Nkamu took the heifer to his yard.

In his yard the heifer pissed. You have seen it. A heifer that has drunk the river all morning pisses half the river out again before noon, and goes on doing it all afternoon, and Nkamu's boy stood in the yard and watched it come out of her, and went to his father, and said, "Father, the heifer is getting smaller."

Nkamu came out and looked at it. By evening it was a good red heifer two years old of the size a good red heifer two years old is, and that was perhaps four-fifths of what he had paid for at the cattle-beam. He had his men weigh it on his own small beam, which you do not need to know he had. Four-fifths. One fifth of the salt he had sold, he had sold for river.

And out on the grazing, a month later, Ngomi opened the sacks for the cattle, and the salt had dried in the heat, and she weighed it on nothing, because herders have no beam, but she had carried those sacks on her donkey all the way home and she knew what they had weighed, and she knew they were lighter. About one part in eight, she said, when she told it. She was not as exact as Nkamu. She did not need to be.

So each of them had cheated the other. Each of them had sworn on the beam an oath that was true. _This weighs what the beam says._ The salt weighed what the beam said. The heifer weighed what the cattle-beam said. And each of them had been cheated of the very thing they had cheated with: Nkamu had paid for water and Ngomi had paid for water.

Now Nkamu was angry. You would think he would laugh, being the man he was, and after a while he did. But that first night, standing in his yard with a heifer that was the wrong size, he was angry. He had been cheated in his own city, at his own stall, by a herdswoman with mud on her feet. And he did the thing a man of Kaljekor does when he is angry. He went to the Flameweavers and asked to see the record.

The clerk who taught me showed it to him. There it was, both oaths, on one leaf, in the clerk's own hand, with the weights. Nkamu read it, because he could read, and he put his finger on Ngomi's name and said, "This oath was false."

The clerk said, "It says the beast weighed what the beam said. The beam said it. The beast weighed it."

Nkamu said, "The beast was full of water."

And the clerk, who was young then and a herdsman's boy, said, "So was your salt."

That is the line the apprentices laugh at. You may laugh. Nkamu did not. He looked at the clerk a long time, and then he said, "Who else knows?"

The clerk said, "Everyone who has looked at the record. It is a record."

That was true, and that was the trouble. Because the clerk had not said anything at the beam, and would not have, but a record is a record, and the Flameweavers read their own records, and so do the Servants of Gold, and so, if they pay the clerk's fee for a reading, do the traders. And the clerk had written, under the two oaths, in the small hand we use for what is seen and not sworn, one line. It was a clerk's habit then and it is still. You will see me do it tomorrow. He had written, "The beast was watered. The salt was damp."

By the end of the Market of Flames, everyone in Kaljekor who traded salt or cattle had paid the fee and read that line.

Now hear what Kaljekor did with it, because this is the part that is a Kaljekor tale and not a herders' tale.

Kaljekor waited.

A false oath on the Scales ruins a man within a year and a day. Everyone knows it. And here were two oaths on one leaf, and one line under them saying both had cheated, and a question nobody could answer: were the oaths false? Each one said the thing weighed what the beam said, and each thing had. So perhaps the oaths were true, and nobody would be ruined. Or perhaps the Flame did not care what the words said, and weighed what was meant, and then both oaths were false, and both would be ruined. Or perhaps, and this was the view of most of the salt-sellers' street, a herdswoman's oath at the cattle-beam was one thing and a citizen's oath at the gold beam was another, and only the herdswoman would be ruined, because the cattle-beam is not gold.

So Kaljekor did what Kaljekor does. It wagered.

You think I am joking. Ask any old trader. That year there was more wagered at Kaljekor on the fortunes of Nkamu the salt-trader and Ngomi of the Tálumeng than on the price of iron. There were three wagers, one on each view. You could wager that both would be ruined, or that neither would, or that the herdswoman would and the trader would not. Nobody would take a wager that the trader would be ruined and the herdswoman would not, so that wager was not offered. The moneylenders of the Crown Trades held the stakes. They kept a separate account for it, and it is in their books, and that is the only account in their books that is written in the name of a cow.

Then the year began, and everyone watched.

They watched Nkamu, which was easy, because he lived on the salt-sellers' street. He did not change his trade. He still sold salt, and he still sold it at the stall by the gate, and the story goes that he stopped sprinkling it. The salt-sellers' street watched his yard at night to see. They said they did not see a jar. He sold dry salt that year, for the first time in nine years, and he sold less of it by weight, because it weighed less, and he grumbled, and he did not go hungry. He was not ruined in the first month. He was not ruined in the second.

They watched Ngomi too, which was harder, because she was out on the grazing with the Tálumeng. So the traders who went out to the grazing to buy hides asked after her, and the herders who came in to sell asked about Nkamu, and the news went back and forth on the road all year like a shuttle on a loom. The Tálumeng thought the whole thing was very funny. They knew about the watered heifer before the city did, because Ngomi had told them on the way home. A herder does not think it a sin to water a beast before it is weighed. A herder thinks it is a sin to sell a beast that is sick, or to sell a cow and keep the calf, or to boast about the herd. Watering is just something everyone does, and anyone buying cattle is supposed to know it, the way anyone buying salt from the city is supposed to know about the jar. The Tálumeng wagered too, on the grazing, in goats. They wagered that the city man would be ruined, because he was a city man.

Ngomi was not ruined in the first month. She was not ruined in the second. Her herd calved well. Her brothers-in-law gave up trying to take it. She married her eldest daughter into a lineage with a hill grazing, and a hill grazing came into the family with the daughter's husband's cattle.

The year went round. At midsummer the fires went out and were lit again, and Nkamu was not ruined. At midwinter the Night of Ashes came and the dead were fed, and Ngomi was not ruined. The rains came and went. People who had wagered that both would be ruined began to look at the ones who had wagered that neither would, and the ones who had wagered that only the herdswoman would began to look at their stakes.

And then it came near the Market of Flames again, and a year and a day from the oath fell on the morning the market opened. That had been noticed. Everyone had counted it. So everyone in Kaljekor who had a stake, and most who did not, came to the beam that morning to see.

Nkamu came. He came up from the salt-sellers' street in his good linen with his sons behind him, and he stood by the post with his arms folded, alive and not ruined, and looked at the crowd as if he had brought it.

And Ngomi came. That is the part people do not expect when they hear it the first time. She did not have to come. She could have stayed out on the grazing and let the city find out a year and a day had gone by without her. But she came in to the Market of Flames, as she would have anyway, for salt, and she came with a heifer. Another red heifer, two years old. The crowd saw it come through the east gate and made a noise like a herd.

She came up to the Scales leading it, with the mud of the road on her feet, and stopped in front of Nkamu, and the two of them looked at each other for a while.

He said, "Is it watered?"

She said, "Is your salt wet?"

He said, "No."

She said, "Then no."

And the crowd laughed, the whole crowd, the traders and the herders and the Servants of Gold at the post, and the eldest of the Flameweavers, who had come down from the colonnade to see it like everyone else. When it was quiet again, the eldest of the Flameweavers held up his hand and said that a year and a day had passed on the oaths of Nkamu the salt-trader and Ngomi of the Tálumeng, and that the Flameweavers would now write what had become of them, as the Flameweavers write what becomes of every oath, and he asked the clerk to bring the leaf.

The clerk was the one who had written it. He brought it, and the eldest read it out, both oaths, and the weights, and then the small line underneath, _the beast was watered, the salt was damp_, and the crowd laughed again.

Then the eldest of the Flameweavers asked the two of them a question, each in turn. This is the judgment. Listen to it.

He asked Nkamu, "When you sold the salt, what did you give?"

Nkamu said, "Sixty-eight measures by the beam."

The eldest said, "Of what?"

Nkamu said, "Of salt."

The eldest said, "How much of it was salt?"

And Nkamu was a clever man and an honest one in his way, standing there in front of the whole city, so he said, "About sixty."

The eldest asked Ngomi, "When you paid for the salt, what did you give?"

Ngomi said, "A heifer. Her weight by the cattle-beam."

The eldest said, "How much of her was heifer?"

Ngomi said, "About four parts in five. The rest was the river. The river is not mine to sell. I am sorry to the river."

The crowd did not laugh at that. The herders in it did not, anyway.

Then the eldest of the Flameweavers turned to the crowd and said this, and it is in the record in his words, and I have read it.

"The oath says, _the Flame is between us_. It does not say the beam is between us. The beam weighs what is put on it. The Flame weighs what is given. Nkamu put sixty-eight on the beam and gave sixty. Ngomi put a heifer and a river on the beam and gave a heifer. Each gave less than the beam said. That is a false oath, and the Flame is between a false oath and the bread of the man who swears it.

"But the Flame is between the two of them. It holds them level. It does not hold each of them level against the beam. It holds the one against the other. Nkamu gave about seven parts in eight of what he swore. Ngomi gave about four parts in five of what she swore. Those are near enough the same that I will not stand here and argue the difference in front of the Servants of Gold. Each was short to the other by about what the other was short to them. The beam between them was level. The Flame had nothing to take from either of them, because each had already paid the other back in the same coin.

"So neither is ruined. Write it."

And the clerk wrote it. It is the line beside their names in the record. "Neither is ruined. They cheated each other level."

That is the judgment. That is why the wager on neither paid, and the moneylenders paid it out of the cow account, and some of the salt-sellers' street did not speak to the moneylenders for a year.

But that is not the end of the tale, and this next part is the reason we tell it to apprentices, and not only for the laughing.

When the judgment was given, Nkamu stood there by the post a while longer. Then he unfolded his arms and went up to Ngomi and the red heifer. He said, in front of everyone, "I need a buyer at the gate who knows a watered beast when she sees one."

She said, "You need a buyer at the gate who knows wet salt."

He said, "Both."

And so they swore again, there at the beam, that morning, a new oath, which is the one that is in the record on the next leaf, in the same clerk's hand. Ngomi of the Tálumeng would bring the herders of her lineage and her daughter's lineage to Nkamu's stall at every Market of Flames for as long as both of them lived. Nkamu would sell them dry salt, weighed on the gold beam the morning it was sold and not the night before. And the herders' cattle would come to the cattle-beam at the east gate thirsty, walked in from the grazing by the dry road, and not by the river. The salt weighed dry, and the cattle weighed thirsty.

They both put their right hands on the gold, and they swore it, the whole oath, the one you said with me, _on the beam, before the Flame_. And then, because it was the Market of Flames and an oath sworn at the Market of Flames is held unbreakable, they kept it. For twenty-two years, until Nkamu died, the herders of two Tálumeng lineages bought their salt at his stall by the gate, and paid in thirsty cattle, and the Flameweavers wrote it every year, the same oath, the same names, and a year and a day after every one of them, the clerk wrote "Trading."

That is in the record too. Twenty-two leaves, the same line on each. I have read them all. It is the longest run of the same line in the records of the Scales, and the oldest clerks show it to the young ones when the young ones think that a clerk's work is reading about men drowned in the river.

When Nkamu died his sons kept the stall, and the oath, and Ngomi's sons kept it on the other side, and their sons after them, and then the families went different ways, as families do, and the stall by the gate belongs to someone else now. But the saying stayed at the Market. You will hear it tomorrow. Every trader at the Market of Flames says it to the other before a weighing, and you will say it too, and now you know where it comes from.

"Dry salt. Thirsty cow."

Say it. Again. That is right.

It means: what we put on the beam is what we give. It means: I know your tricks and you know mine, and neither of us will use them today, at this beam, because the Flame is between us and holds us level. And it means, though nobody says this part aloud: the herders and the city need each other, and have always cheated each other, and have never been able to do without each other, and the beam is where they settle it.

Now. You will be offered the jar tomorrow. Some trader you have never met will take you aside before the horn and say there is a way to make a sack heavier overnight, and that the oath says only that it weighs what the beam says. He will be right about the oath. He will not tell you about the leaf, or the small line, or the clerk who knows wet salt when he sees it, or the herdswoman on the grazing who has a river of her own.

You will be at the beam. I will be at the leaf. Go to sleep.
