**The Answer from the Bones**, _zhepulu ntayi ngorun zhepom_, is told in households of the [[affiliation-shokumatrb|Shókuma]] on [The Night of Ashes]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, the midwinter festival at which families burn offerings to their dead and shamans appease the restless, and the [[lore-zohira|zohira]] are said to walk openly among the living. It is told by the eldest of a household while the offerings burn. It is not told at every hearth. A household tells it when it has a dead person of its own whom no temple receives, and it tells it at the cairn, because that is where such a person is fed.

Its subject is a woman of the Shókuma who declined the Flame and was put out of it. A person who takes up a foreign god is an eccentric in the tribal country and close to a traitor in the city-states, where the [[place-vuthraka|Vuthraka]] priesthood treats refusal of the Flame as touching the defense of Okharis. The person put out of the Flame is a **shurem**, "ash": no temple receives her, she is given no ashmark at a passage, and her fire is not reckoned descended from the First Ember. The telling says what was done to her plainly. It does not say whether it was right. Its other subject is [[lore-orclbonesspr|the Oracle of Bones]], which the dead woman's brother, a shaman, went to with one question. Everyone who has reached the Oracle agrees that it answers exactly the question asked, which is not the same as the question the asker meant, and the telling turns on that.

The household here is the **Óbamba** lineage of the Shókuma, and the teller is **Nyomi of the Óbamba**, its eldest woman, at the river cairn below the village on the Night of Ashes, to her grandchildren and her grandchildren's children. The telling names the way to the Oracle no more than the shaman did. Where the temple's account and the Bone-Reader's differ, Nyomi gives both, and gives the household's practice after them.

## The Telling

Put the millet cake on the fire. Not that one, that one is your grandfather's, he liked them burned. The small one. Put it on the edge of the fire where it will catch slowly. That one is hers.

You have fed your grandfather already, at the hearth, and your great-grandmother, and the two who died small, and your uncle who went in the river. You fed them at the hearth because their fire came from the temple and their names were said to the new flame when the hearth was lit again after they died. That is where they are fed. They are fed at the hearth, by the fire that took them up.

She is fed here, at the cairn, by the river. Every year you ask why, and every year I say when you are older. You are older. Sit down on the stone. This is the answer from the bones, and it is long, and the cake will take a long time to burn.

Her name was **Ndori of the Óbamba**. She was the sister of **Ngathu of the Óbamba**, who was the shaman of this lineage in the time of my grandmother's grandmother. He took the Hunt as a young man and came back from it, and the bone at his throat was the bone of the thing he had hunted, and he was a teacher of the spirits for the rest of his life. He went to the cairns for the lineage. He told the storm before the season what the lineage meant to plant. He spoke for the lineage at the river. Remember him. He is the one who went.

Ndori was younger than he was. She was the one in the lineage who wanted to see what was down the river. You know where the river goes. It goes north and east out of the forest, twisting, and it gets wider, and in the end it goes into the country of the Khelathi, where they have dug the land into squares and the river does what they tell it. The Shókuma have always traded down the river, honey and wax for cloth and pots. Ndori went down with the honey boats when she was a girl, and came back, and went again, and the third time she did not come back for four years.

When she came back, she had married a boatman of the river people downstream, a man who was half Khelathi and half nobody's, and he had died, and she had two children by him, and she had his god.

I do not know the god's name. Nobody in this lineage has said it since. It was a god of the river people, a god of boats and of the far bank, and it had a small image, carved out of black stone, a seated man with his hands on his knees, the size of your hand. She kept it in a niche in the wall of her hut, where the lamp goes. That is the first thing. Where the three-wick lamp goes, she put the stone man. She did not light the lamp. She put a little oil in a dish in front of the image and lit that, one wick, and on the day of the god, which was not one of our days, she put bread in front of it.

The lineage did not like it. You will understand that. But listen: the lineage did not do anything about it. A woman who prays to a downriver god in the forest country is a strange woman. She is not a wicked one. Ngathu went to see her about it and came back and said, "She has not left the spirits. She cannot leave the spirits. She goes to the river cairn the same as anyone, she announces herself at the ford, she gave first milk to the fertility cairn when her second was born downriver and she was too far away to give it, so she gave it when she came home. She is of the spirits as you are. She has declined the Flame. That is a different thing."

He was right. You know he was right. The spirits are what being Okharin is. Nobody joins them and nobody leaves. The Flame is the faith of the cities and the temple and the priests, and it is professed, and what is professed can be professed otherwise. In the forest that is all it is. A woman of this lineage who does not light the three wicks is a woman who does not light the three wicks.

But the Shókuma country lies toward the south, and the city nearest the south is Vuthraka, which is the city of the red fire, and it guards the frontier.

What happened is this. It was the year of the Rekindling, as every year is, and at midsummer the fires were put out and the ember was carried out from Zarhanis to the cities and from the cities to the villages, and in the forest country it comes from Vuthraka. The Soldiers of Fire bring it, with a priest, to each village, and the village lights its hearths from it again, hearth by hearth, so that the realm is one fire.

They came to this village, the soldiers and the priest, and the hearths were lit, and the priest went from hut to hut with the ember to see each hearth lit from it, which is what the priest does. And he came to Ndori's hut, and her hearth was already burning.

She had not put out her hearth. You understand. She had not put it out at midsummer, because she did not keep the Rekindling, so she had not needed to light it again. She had kept it burning all through the night when every other fire in Okharis was out. And the priest came to the door with the ember, and found it burning. The fire in it was not from the ember. It was a fire that had not been put out.

So it was not reckoned. You know the hymn. A fire not lit from the ember is not reckoned. It warms the hands, and it is ash.

The priest asked her to put it out and light it from the ember. Ngathu was there. He said afterward the priest asked her civilly, the first time, the way you ask a strange old woman to do a small thing. She said no. The priest asked her again, and he saw the niche in the wall, and the stone man in it with the little dish in front of him, and he asked her a third time, and his voice was different. She said no. She said, "My fire is my own fire. I have kept it. It has never gone out since I came home. I did not get it from you."

The priest said, "Every fire in Okharis is from the ember."

She said, "This one is from my hands."

That is what she said. My grandmother said it to me in those words. "This one is from my hands." Remember it.

Then the priest stood up out of the doorway, and the Soldiers of Fire went in.

I will tell you what they did and I will not tell you whether it was right. That is not mine to say, at this cairn, on this night. Your uncles say it was right and your aunts say it was wrong and the cities say it was the law. This is what was done.

They put her fire out with water from the river, which is a thing that is never done, because water is the river's and fire is the Flame's and they are kept apart, and they did it so that everyone would see it done. They took the stone man out of the niche and broke it on the hearthstone. The priest struck her name from the temple roll. He struck her children's names too, because she was their mother and they were too small to say otherwise. He said in the doorway, in front of the village, the words the priests say, which I am not going to say here. And then she was ash.

You know what ash is. No temple gives her grain in a bad year. No court hears her if she is wronged. No ashmark is laid on her at a passage. Her children get no ashmark at their first passage. Her hearth, if she lights another, is not reckoned, and nobody may light a lamp from it. When she dies, no priest lights her household's hearth again from the temple and says her name to the new flame. Her name is not taken up. The priest at the door said so, and the Soldiers of Fire stood behind him, and the whole village heard.

And she was still of the Óbamba. That is the thing. The priest could put her out of the Flame. He could not put her out of the lineage, because the lineage is not his, and he could not put her out of the spirits, because the spirits are not his either. So she lived. She lived in her own hut, with a new fire she lit from her hands with a fire-stick, which was not reckoned, and her children, and the lineage gave her milk and millet from its own stores, because the lineage gives its own. Nobody in the village spoke against it. Some in the village did not speak to her either. That is how it is.

Ngathu went to her every day. He was a shaman, and the shamans are not on the priests' ladder at all. A High Pyremant outranks every priest in Okharis and does not outrank a shaman, and a shaman goes where his work takes him. He brought her honey. He sat with her children. He did not say anything about the god.

Then there was a bad year. The rains failed in the south two years running, which they do not do often, and the river went down to its stones, and the millet died in the plots. The village went to Vuthraka for grain, because in a bad year that is what the cities are for: the granary-keepers hold the stores the realm lives on, and the temple gives them out to the faithful by the roll. The village took its roll to Vuthraka and came home with grain.

Ndori's name was not on the roll.

The lineage fed her from its own stores. It did. But its own stores were what was left after a year when nothing grew, and there were children in the lineage whose names were on the roll and who were thin, and she would not take from them. She took less than she was given. She gave her children what she was given. She went into the forest for roots. She was not young anymore. In the second year of it, at the end of the dry season, she got a fever and died of it in four days.

Ngathu was with her. He said she was cold at the end, though it was hot.

So now listen to what happens to the dead, because this is where the answer comes.

When one of us dies, two things are done, and you have seen them both done for your grandfather. The temple's rite is done. A priest comes. The hearth is put out, and lit again from the temple, and the dead one's name is said to the new flame, and the new flame takes it up, and the dead one goes up the road of light to the fire he came out of. Zarhanis keeps that road. That is the temple's account and it is a good one.

And the Bone-Reader comes. The Bone-Reader comes after the body has been in the ground its season, or before it goes in, as the lineage prefers, and reads what the Oracle of Bones has written in it. The Oracle is the zohira of death. It writes in every body what it discloses, and the Bone-Reader reads it, and says it to the family. That is the Bone-Reader's account and it is a good one too.

The two accounts are not the same. The priest says he went up in the fire. The Bone-Reader says what is written in his bones. You asked me once, when your grandfather died, which one is true, and I said you were being rude. You were. Your grandfather is where the Flame takes him and also what the bones say. Every widow in Okharis knows that, and every widow has both rites done, and does not lie awake over it.

But for Ndori there was no temple rite. No priest would come. The hearth in her hut was not reckoned, so there was nothing to put out and light again from the temple, and her name was not said to any new flame, and nothing took it up. The priest who had struck her said, when Ngathu went to Vuthraka and asked, that she was ash, and that ash does not go up. Ash stays where it falls.

So there was only the Bone-Reader.

Ngathu brought one up from the next village, an old woman who had read for the lineage before. She read Ndori's bones, before she went into the ground, in the hut, by the light of Ndori's fire that was not reckoned. Ngathu sat with her. She read a long time. Then she said what the bones said, and Ngathu told it, and it has come down to me.

The bones said that Ndori had borne three children and lost one. They said she had carried heavy loads when she was young, on her back, up from the boats. They said she had been hungry for two years. They said she had been cold at the end. And they said that the hand that had written all of that was the hand the Oracle of Bones always writes in.

The Bone-Reader said that last part twice. She said, "It is the same hand. It is the hand it writes in all of you. There is nothing wrong with the hand."

Do you understand what that meant? The priest said Ndori was ash. The bones were written in the Oracle's own hand, the same as anyone's. The Oracle is a zohira, and the zohira are the Flame's own servants, and the Oracle had written her as it writes everyone. So the Oracle had not put her out. Only the priest had.

That should have been enough. For most of us, it would have been. The Bone-Reader's account is a good one. But Ngathu was a man of the Flame as well as of the spirits. He lit his three wicks. He went to the Rekindling. He believed the temple's account of the road of light, the same as you do, and he could not bear that his sister was not on it.

He wanted to know whether she was in the Flame. The Bone-Reader could not tell him that, because the bones say what the Oracle writes and not where the dead are. The priest had told him she was not, and he did not believe the priest, or he did not want to. And there was only one who could tell him.

So he went to the Oracle.

You know what that is. It is not done often, and it is not done lightly, and not everyone who does it comes back. The Oracle of Bones is seated in the mountains of the spirits, the Yanda Zohiring, where the tribes hold the gods live. That much everyone knows. Where in the mountains, nobody agrees. The shamans who say they have reached it tell different ways, and different places, and none of them match, and the tradition stopped trying to make them match a long time ago. Ngathu never told anyone the way he went. Not his wife, not his sons, not the lineage. I do not know it. Do not ask me again. If you ever need to go, which I hope you never will, you will find your own way, or you will not.

He went at the end of the rains after she died. He took a horse and a bag of millet and honey and his bone and his blade, and he went south and then east toward the mountains, and the lineage did not see him for most of a year.

What happened on the way he did not tell. He told only three things about it. He said that he left the horse at a river it would not cross, and crossed alone, announcing himself, and the river let him. He said that he was hungry for many days in a place with no game and no water but snow, and that he ate the last of the honey there and was not sorry. And he said that when he came to the place he was going, he knew it, because there were bones on the ground and none of them were broken.

That is all. He did not say what the Oracle looks like. He did not say whether it speaks or writes or how. Nobody who has gone tells that, and nobody asks.

He told only this. He said that he had come there with one question, and he had held it in his head all the way, through the river and the snow, and turned it over every night by the fire so that he would have it exact. Because everyone knows what the Oracle does. It answers exactly the question asked. Not the question meant. He knew that before he set out. Every shaman knows it. So he had spent the whole way making his question exact, so that what he asked would be what he meant.

And when he came to it, he asked his question.

He asked it the way it is asked, in few words. I will say it to you as he said it, and then I will say it plainly.

"_ndori thumom mbo nkaru zhembe?_"

Is Ndori in the Flame?

And the Oracle answered him.

"_ndekhe._"

No.

That is the answer from the bones. One word.

He came home. It took him the rest of the year. He came home on foot, without the horse, which he never found again, and with his left hand that would not close all the way, from the snow, and he came up from the river to the village at the beginning of the rains with his hair gone grey, and he went to his hut and sat by his hearth, which was reckoned, and did not say anything for three days.

On the fourth day he went to his sister's hut. Her children were living with his wife by then, so the hut was empty. Her fire had gone out when she died, the fire she lit from her hands. He sat down in the cold hut and stayed there all day. In the evening he came out and called the lineage together, and he told them what he had asked and what he had been answered. Then he said what he thought it meant.

He said, "I asked whether she was in the Flame. It said no. I made my question as exact as I could make it, and it was exact, and the answer was exact. She is not in the Flame. The priest was right."

And the lineage was quiet, because he had gone all that way and come back old to bring them that.

And then his wife spoke. This is my grandmother's grandmother's mother, so she is yours too. Her name was **Mbula of the Óbamba**, and she had come into the Óbamba from the Yéshiro, the tanners, and she was not a shaman and had never been anywhere. She said:

"Of course she is not in the Flame. She declined the Flame. She told the priest so at her door, and he heard her, and the whole village heard her. She said, _this fire is from my hands_. The Oracle has told you what she told you herself. You went all the way to the mountains and nearly died in the snow to ask a question your sister had already answered at her own door."

Ngathu said, "Then where is she?"

And Mbula said, "That is not what you asked."

It is not. You see it. He asked whether she was in the Flame, and he meant, _is she lost?_ He meant, _is she somewhere, is she cared for, has anything taken her up?_ He meant all of that, and he asked about the Flame, because he was a man of the Flame and could not think of any other place for her to be. He made his question exact, and he made it exactly wrong. The Oracle answered exactly what he asked. It did not answer what he meant. It never does.

Mbula said, "You did not ask whether she was with the spirits."

He said, "Everyone is with the spirits. Nobody leaves them."

She said, "Then why did you not ask that?"

And he did not have an answer, and after a while he laughed. My grandmother said he laughed, there in front of the lineage, with his bad hand in his lap, the first time since he came home. He said, "Because I already knew it."

So that is what the lineage did. You can see it here. The priests would not take her up, and the Flame was not hers, because she had declined it herself, at her own door, and the Oracle had said so. But the spirits were hers. Nobody leaves the spirits. The Bone-Reader had said that her bones were written in the same hand as everyone's. And the cairn is the spirits' place. The river cairn is Yombel Ngoriring's, who has the same rules at every ford and never asks which god you pray to, only that you say your name when you cross.

So the lineage feeds her here. Every Night of Ashes since Ngathu came home, the Óbamba have fed Ndori at the river cairn and not at the hearth. A small millet cake on a fire that is lit from the hearth and carried down here in a pot, because we have no other kind of fire to bring. Some of the lineage say that makes it the Flame's fire, and that we should light it from our hands, the way she did. Some say it does not matter, because the fire is only carrying the cake. We have argued about that for as long as we have done it, and we still bring it down in the pot.

Some of the lineage say Ngathu should have gone back and asked the other question. He did not. You do not go to the Oracle twice. He said that once you have your answer, it is yours, and the next one would be someone else's. He lived another twenty years and did his work at the cairns, and he did not go to the mountains again.

And the children. Her children were struck off the roll with her, and that does not come undone. The temple does not undo it. The elder one, a girl, married into the Kirofa, and the Kirofa took her without the ashmark, and her children have the ashmark, because she had them marked at the shrine of the Kirofa village where the priest did not know or did not ask. So there are people on the Kirofa banks tonight who are fed at a hearth and lit from the temple and are her grandchildren's grandchildren. They do not know it. I am not going to tell them.

The younger one, a boy, would not take the ashmark when he was old enough to ask for it. He went down the river, the same as his mother, and did not come back.

That is the answer from the bones. The cake is burned now, see, it has gone black and broken. That is right. That means she has had it.

Put the ash from it in the river. With your hand. Say your name when you put it in, because it is the river's. Say hers after.

_ndem en mebom, ndori._

May you eat.

Now come up to the hearth. Your grandfather's cake is burning too, and he never liked to eat alone.
