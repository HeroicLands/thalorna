**The Ember on the Road**, _zhepulu nkarking mbathel yenor_, is a telling of the **Ngáleza**, a lineage of the [[affiliation-magaratrb|Magara]], the horse-breeding tribe of the plains. It belongs to [The Rekindling]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, the midsummer festival at which every temple fire in Okharis is put out and lit again from embers carried from the First Ember at [[place-zarhanis|Zarhanis]], so that the realm is one fire again. Most of the realm takes the new fire from the nearest temple. A lineage out on the far grazing at midsummer sends its own carrier to the city for it and sits without fire until the carrier comes home, and the telling is about one such carrier, a girl, and what the ember cost on the way back.

The Ngáleza tell it every year on the first day of the dry-season move, when the lineage drives its horses and cattle past a valley it no longer grazes. The valley is the **Valley of the Two Baobabs**, and it belongs to the **Sángweri**, a Magara lineage the Ngáleza have been at feud with for longer than the telling goes back. A clan that yields a grazing right once has yielded it, and everyone remembers. The Ngáleza yielded this one, and the telling says why. Its teller is whoever leads the move; the version here is the one **Rito of the Ngáleza** tells, at the mouth of the valley, from horseback, to the young riders of the lineage on their first move.

The telling marks where the Ngáleza and the Sángweri tell it differently, and leaves the difference standing. It gives the words said at a ford in Okharik, as every rider of the lineage says them.

## The Telling

Rein in. All of you, here, at the mouth of it, where you can see the trees. Let the herd go by. The herdsmen have it. The horses know the way better than you.

Look up the valley. Two baobabs, one on each side of the stream bed, as old as anything in the Magara country, and between them the best dry-season grass from here to the hills, and water under the sand even at the end of the heat if you know where to dig. Look at it well. You will never graze a horse in it. It is the Sángweri's.

It was ours. We grazed it in the dry season, every dry season, from before anyone counted, and the Sángweri grazed the hills to the north. The right was ours and the whole Magara knew it. Now the right is theirs, and the whole Magara knows that too. Every year on the first day of the move, the Ngáleza ride past the mouth of it and tell why. This is your year to hear it. Next year it will be your year to tell it to someone younger, maybe, or the year after. Pay attention.

This was in the time of **Omengo of the Ngáleza**, who led the lineage, and the feud with the Sángweri was old already then. You know there is blood between us and them. It is in the Keeper of the Feud's record at the moot, and it is not paid. There was one Ngáleza man dead by the Sángweri when this happened, and two Sángweri dead by us, and cattle owed both ways, and that year nobody of either lineage spoke to anybody of the other, and at the festivals the two lineages camped on opposite sides of the city.

That year at midsummer the lineage was far out on the eastern grazing, with the horses. The rains had been late, and the grass was late, and Omengo did not want to take the herd in to the city for the Rekindling and lose the late grass. So the lineage stayed out with the herd, and Omengo sent carriers to Zarhanis to bring the new fire back.

You know how that goes. At midsummer every fire in Okharis is put out. Every hearth, every forge, every lamp, the herd-camp fires, all of them, and for a night there is no fire in the realm but the First Ember under the pyramid at Zarhanis. In the morning the ember is carried out, and the realm is lit from it again, hearth after hearth. A lineage that is in the city takes its new fire at the temple and carries it home. A lineage out on the far grazing puts out its fires on the night, the same as everyone, and sends someone to the city for the new fire, and sits in the dark until it comes.

You have sat in the dark for it. Two nights, three. You know what it is like. No milk warmed, no meat cooked, no lamp in the hut, and the herd restless all night because the herdsmen have no fire to keep the lions off with, and everybody sitting up with a spear. That is what the lineage was doing, all the days the carriers were on the road. Remember that while I tell the rest. Every day the carriers took was a night in the dark for the whole lineage out on the grass.

Omengo sent two. He sent his sister's son **Jondo of the Ngáleza**, who was nineteen and had his blade and thought well of himself, and he sent his granddaughter **Tenzi of the Ngáleza**, who was fourteen and the best rider in the lineage, and they took a spare horse each and two fire-pots.

You have seen the fire-pots. Clay, with a lid with holes in it, and a strap. You pack the bottom with dry dung and charcoal from the Dóngaru, and you put the ember in the middle of it, and you close it, and the ember eats slowly through the charcoal and the dung, and you feed it through the lid every half a day. A good carrier can bring an ember four days in a fire-pot without it going out. Five, if the charcoal is good. It was four days' ride from the eastern grazing to Zarhanis, riding hard, and four back.

They rode hard. They got to Zarhanis on the morning of the Rekindling, which was close, and there was a crowd at the temple court like the whole realm, because it was, and they stood in it all day with their pots and their horses until the priests came out from the pyramid with the ember from the First Ember, and lit the great fire in the court from it, and then the carriers of every lineage in Okharis came up to the great fire one after another and took their embers from it. Tenzi and Jondo took theirs late in the day. Tenzi said afterward that she put her ember in the pot with a pair of tongs one of the priests gave her, and the priest looked at her and said, "Far?" and she said, "Four days," and he said, "Feed it at noon and at dark, and do not let it get wet."

She said that was all he said. She said she thought at the time it was a stupid thing to say, because everyone knows that.

They rode out of Zarhanis that night with the two pots, one on each of their saddles, and rode east.

The first day was good. The second day was good. On the third day they came to the river.

You know the river. It is the one we cross on the move in the rains, when it is a river. At midsummer it is low, most years. That year it was not low, because the rains had run late, which is why the grass was late, which is why the lineage was out on the grazing in the first place, so you see how one thing goes into another. The river was up. Not a flood, but up, brown, fast, to a horse's chest at the ford.

You know what is done at a ford. Say it with me. You will say it at the ford tomorrow.

"_okharé, ne thalé. ngem thumom tenzi, ngorathen ngáleza. ngem ngenom ngembet._"

You say your own name, not Tenzi's. One of the people, hear me: peace. I am so-and-so, from the Ngáleza. I go across. The river forbids crossing in silence, and her rules are the same at every ford in the realm, which is her mercy, because nobody can say they were not told.

Tenzi said it. She stopped her horse at the edge of the water and said it out loud and went in.

Jondo did not say it.

Some say he forgot. Some say he was showing off, and wanted to get across first and laugh at her for stopping. In the Sángweri telling, because they tell this too, he did not say it because he was a Ngáleza and the Ngáleza never learned manners. That is what they say. We say he forgot. Either way he went into the water in silence, at a canter, ahead of her, and the river took his footing.

She took it in the middle, where the current was strongest, and the horse went down sideways into the water with Jondo still on it, and the spare horse pulled loose, and Jondo came up holding the saddle with one hand and the horse's mane with the other, and he got the horse up and got to the far bank, and the spare horse got there after him. And when he stood on the far bank in the mud with the water coming off him, the fire-pot on his saddle was full of river.

Yombel Ngoriring takes what she takes and does not apologize. He had been given the same warning as everyone.

So now there was one ember, in Tenzi's pot. And there was a day and a half still to ride.

Jondo did not say anything to her, and she did not say anything to him, which is the right thing in both cases. They rode on. At noon she fed the ember. At dusk she fed it again. They rode on in the dark, because the lineage was in the dark.

That night the storm came.

You know Gorombe does not answer during a storm. He is told before the season what the lineage means to build and plant and travel, so that what he breaks he breaks knowing. Omengo had told him, at the cairn on the high ground before the rains, that the lineage would be on the eastern grazing that season. He had not told him the lineage would send two carriers to Zarhanis at midsummer, because he had not known it then. So it was not the storm's fault. The storm had not been told.

It came out of the south in the middle of the night, with no warning but the wind, and it came at the tail of a late season, when the storms are heaviest. They were on the open grass, with nothing to shelter under. The rain came down so hard that the horses turned their tails to it and stood and would not go on.

And you know what the priest said. _Do not let it get wet._

Tenzi got down off her horse. She took the fire-pot off the saddle and sat down in the grass with her back to the wind and put the pot in her lap and bent over it. She took off her cloak and put it over her head and over the pot, so that she and the pot were under it together, like a hut. She held it like that, with the rain coming down on the cloak and through it and down her back, and the water running past her in the grass like a stream. She could feel the heat of the pot in her lap through her clothes. She said she could feel it getting less.

The storm went on all night. Jondo stood with the horses. Some time in the night, she said, she took the lid off the pot under the cloak to look, and the ember was red at the bottom of the charcoal, very small, and the charcoal round it was wet. She blew on it. It came up a little and went down. She did not have anything dry to feed it. Everything she had was wet. She took the dry inside of the dung cake from the bottom of the pot and broke it up and fed it, and it came up a little and went down.

She fed it the hair of her head. She told that afterward, and Jondo said it was true, because she was bald on one side in the morning. She pulled it out in pinches, from under the cloak, where it was still dry, and fed it to the ember, a pinch at a time. Hair burns quickly and stinks and does not last. It kept the ember for a while.

By the end of the night it was nearly out.

And when the rain thinned toward dawn, and they could see a little way across the grass, they saw a fire.

Not far. Across the grass to the north, under a stand of thorn, a herd camp, with a fire in it, burning, a real fire, with smoke going up into the rain, and herdsmen sitting round it, and horses and cattle beyond. A lineage that had sent its own carrier and had its carrier home already. A lineage with its new fire.

Jondo knew the horses. He said, "Sángweri."

Now you see where this is going. You have seen it since I said there was blood between us. Listen anyway.

Tenzi sat in the grass with the pot in her lap. The ember was a speck of red the size of a millet seed. She knew it would not see the sun come up. And there across the grass was the new fire, lit from the First Ember at Zarhanis the same as hers, the same fire, two days old, burning in the camp of a lineage her lineage had blood with, and she knew that if she went to that fire and asked for a coal, she would get one. Because you do not refuse fire at the Rekindling. Nobody does. The whole festival is for making the realm one fire again. A lineage that refused fire to another lineage at the Rekindling would be talked about at every festival in Okharis for a hundred years.

And she knew that if she took it, it was a gift. And what is given is counted.

You know this. You have been told it at the fold gate since you could stand. A gift is not nothing. A gift is a debt the other way. A man who drinks at the gate is inside the giving, and what is given to him is counted by the fire. A coal from the Sángweri fire would be a gift from the Sángweri to the Ngáleza, given in front of their herdsmen, at the Rekindling, and carried home to light every hearth in our camp. The Ngáleza would owe the Sángweri their fire. Their whole fire for the year. Every hearth in our camp, every pot of milk, every lamp, would be the Sángweri's gift until it was paid back. And there was blood between us that was not paid back either.

That is what she was sitting in the wet grass thinking, with the ember going out in her lap.

Jondo said, "Let it go out. We will ride back to Zarhanis. Four days there. Four back."

She said, "Eight more nights in the dark."

He said, "Eight nights in the dark is better than owing the Sángweri our fire."

You will hear people say he was right. Some of the lineage say it to this day, here, at this valley, when the telling is done. Eight nights in the dark and our own fire, and no debt. Our fire from our own carriers' hands, the way it should be. He was not wrong about the debt.

Tenzi said, "It is not our fire or their fire. It is the fire."

And she got up, with the pot, and walked across the grass toward the Sángweri camp in the rain.

That is what she said. Say it. _It is not our fire or their fire. It is the fire._ Some of the old people say that is what the Rekindling means and some say it is a thing a girl says when she is cold and wet and has been up all night. Both, maybe.

She walked into the Sángweri camp at dawn with her hair pulled out on one side and her cloak running water and a dead fire-pot in her hands, and the Sángweri herdsmen stood up round their fire with their spears, because they knew whose horses those were behind her. And she said the greeting, _okharé, ne thalé_ (one of the people, hear me: peace), and she said her name and her lineage, and then she held out the pot and said, "My ember is going out. I ask for fire."

The head of that camp was a man named **Ufiro of the Sángweri**. Remember the name. The Sángweri remember it.

He looked at her for a long time. He looked at Jondo, who had come up behind her with the horses and had his hand nowhere near his blade, to his credit. He looked at the pot.

Then he said, "Where are you carrying it?"

She said, "To the Ngáleza camp on the eastern grazing. They have been in the dark since the night of the Rekindling."

He said, "How many days?"

She said, "Six, tonight."

And Ufiro of the Sángweri took the tongs from beside his own fire and took a coal out of it, a good coal, as big as a fist, and put it in her fire-pot on top of the dying ember, and packed it with dry charcoal from his own store, and put the lid on, and gave it back to her. He did it in front of all his herdsmen. He said, "There is fire." That was all he said.

She said, "The Ngáleza thank the Sángweri for their fire," which is what is said, and he said nothing to that, because he knew as well as she did what she had just said, and so did all his herdsmen.

She did not sit at his fire. She did not eat. Neither of them would have wanted that. She went back to her horse and tied the pot on and rode east with Jondo, and the coal from the Sángweri fire went with her, packed down on top of the ember from Zarhanis, and by noon they were the same fire and you could not have told them apart.

The Sángweri tell that part differently. In their telling Ufiro said a great deal more than "There is fire," and some of it was about the Ngáleza and fords. We do not tell their part. We tell ours.

She rode into our camp at dusk on the fourth day from Zarhanis. The whole lineage was sitting in the dark, with spears. They heard the horses and stood up. She got down and walked into the camp with the pot, and went to Omengo, because he was the head of the lineage and the new fire is given to the head first.

He held out his hands for it. And she did not give it to him.

She said, "Before you take it, you have to know whose it is."

And she told him, there in the dark, in front of the whole lineage, all of it. The ford, and Jondo's pot full of river. The storm, and her hair. The ember the size of a millet seed. The Sángweri camp in the thorn, and Ufiro, and the coal. She told it exactly. She did not leave out that Jondo had said to let it go out, and she did not leave out that she had said it was the fire, and she did not leave out that Ufiro had given it in front of all his herdsmen.

Then she held out the pot again.

Omengo stood there in the dark with his hands out. He did not take it for a long time. My grandmother's grandmother's mother was there, a small girl, and she said afterward that she thought he was going to knock it out of Tenzi's hands and send to Zarhanis again, and that half the lineage thought so too, and half of them wanted him to.

You must understand what was in the pot. The ember from Zarhanis was in it, from the First Ember, carried by our own carrier, the right way. And the Sángweri coal was in it, given by a lineage we had blood with. And you could not take one out of the other. They were one fire. If he took the pot, he took both. If he lit the camp from it, every hearth of the Ngáleza for that year was lit by the Sángweri's gift. And if he did not take it, the lineage sat eight more nights in the dark, with the lions round the herd, and a girl's hair pulled out for nothing.

He took it.

He took it out of her hands and turned round and knelt down at his own hearth, and opened the pot, and lit his hearth from it, and the fire came up, and the whole lineage made a sound. Then he stood up and said, "Light your hearths." And they came one after another and lit their hearths from his, and in a little while the camp was lit, and the milk was warming, and the herdsmen went out to the herd with torches, and that night nobody sat up with a spear.

And in the morning Omengo said to the lineage, "We owe the Sángweri our fire. We will pay it at the moot."

Now you see why we are sitting here.

At the festival at Zarhanis that year, at the moot, when the lineages of the Magara sat before the elders and the priests to hear the year's disputes, Omengo stood up. The Sángweri were there, on the other side, and Ufiro was with them. And Omengo said, in front of the moot and the Keeper of the Feud and the whole Magara, that at the Rekindling the Sángweri had given fire to the Ngáleza, in the storm, when the Ngáleza ember was going out, and that every hearth of the Ngáleza had been lit that year from the Sángweri's gift, and that the Ngáleza would pay for it.

The moot was quiet. Because everyone there knew that there was blood between those two lineages, and everyone knew that a debt of fire at the Rekindling is not a small debt, and nobody knew what a lineage pays for its whole fire.

Ufiro stood up and said, "There is blood owed between the Ngáleza and the Sángweri. Is this a payment against the blood?"

And Omengo said, "No. The blood is the blood. This is the fire."

That is the second thing you must remember. He did not mix them. He could have. He could have said, we owe you fire and you owe us a man, so let the one pay for the other, and the moot might have let him. He did not. The blood stays in the Keeper's record. It is still there. Nobody has paid it.

Ufiro said, "Then what do the Ngáleza pay for their fire?"

And Omengo said, "The Valley of the Two Baobabs. The dry-season right. From this year."

You can hear the moot now, if you have ever been to one. Every elder of the Magara knew that valley. It was the best dry-season grazing between the river and the hills. A lineage that yields a grazing right once has yielded it, and everyone remembers. And Omengo yielded it, at the moot, in front of everyone, without being asked, for one coal out of a fire in the rain.

The Sángweri telling says Ufiro took it because it was owed. Our telling says Ufiro stood there a long time and then said, "The Sángweri accept the valley." Both are true, probably. He took it. It was owed.

So the Keeper of the Feud wrote it down. Not in the blood record. In the other record, the record of grazing. _The Valley of the Two Baobabs, the dry-season right, from the Ngáleza to the Sángweri, for the fire at the Rekindling._ It is there now. You can ask the Keeper to read it to you at the moot. I have.

That is why the Sángweri graze it and we ride past it.

Some of you are angry. I can see it. I was angry, the first time I heard it, on my first move, sitting on a horse where you are sitting. I said to the man who told it to me that Omengo was a fool, and that a girl's pinch of hair was not worth the best grazing in the Magara country, and that Jondo was right, and we should have sat eight more nights in the dark and kept our valley.

The man who told it to me said, "Then you would have owed the dark."

I did not understand him for a long time. Here is what I think he meant.

The fire is not ours. That is what Tenzi said in the wet grass, and it is what the Rekindling is for. At midsummer every fire in Okharis goes out and is lit again from the one ember, so that every hearth in the realm is one fire, the Ngáleza's and the Sángweri's and the city's and the herdsman's on the far grazing, all of it. We have blood with the Sángweri, and we will have blood with them, I expect, when you are old. But we have one fire with them. We had it before Tenzi took the coal. The coal only made it so you could see it.

And the debt was real. That is the other half, and that is why Omengo paid. You do not get to say "it is all one fire" and walk away from a gift. A gift is counted. If he had taken the coal and said, it is all one fire, we owe nothing, every lineage in the Magara would have known the Ngáleza for people who take and do not pay, and they would have been right. So he paid. He paid more than it was worth, maybe. He paid in the open, at the moot, in front of the whole Magara, so that nobody could ever say the Ngáleza took the Sángweri's fire and did not pay for it.

They cannot say it. They say a great many other things about us, but not that.

And here is the last part, which is short. Tenzi lived to be old. She led the lineage herself, later, after Omengo and after his son. Her hair grew back grey on the side she pulled it out, and black on the other, and she wore it that way all her life and never covered it. And every year at the Rekindling, as long as she lived, she rode to Zarhanis herself for the ember, and she would not let anyone else carry it. And every year on the way back she stopped at the Sángweri camp, wherever it was that year, and said the greeting, and held out her pot, and said, "My ember is going out. I ask for fire." It was not going out. Everyone knew it was not going out. And every year the Sángweri gave her a coal, in front of their herdsmen, and every year she said, "The Ngáleza thank the Sángweri for their fire," and every year she rode home and lit the camp from a pot with both fires in it.

She did not pay for those coals. She had paid. The valley was paid. The Sángweri did not ask, because they knew what she was doing. She was showing them, every year, that it was one fire. And she was showing us.

Nobody does it now. When she died, the lineage stopped. Some say it should have gone on. Some say it was hers.

That is the ember on the road. Look at the trees once more. Now ride on. The herd is half a day ahead of us and you are going to have to catch it.

And tomorrow, at the ford, say your name.
