**The Guest-Price**, _zhepulu zharing mbeneling_, is a household telling of the **Mbárazi**, a lineage of the [[affiliation-talumengtrb|Táluméng]] whose wet-season grazing lies on the eastern side of the tribe's country, where the grass gives out into dry land. It tells the herders' side of the embassy that [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Au'Khelathu]] sent west with an army behind it, in the age when the sun-house reached for the temple-cities. The Khelathi record of that venture, [[lore-templetrib|the Tribute of the Temple Cities]], says the temple-cities paid the Gar-Au's due for a generation. The temple-cities remember the same goods as the guest-price of a long road. The Mbárazi telling agrees with the temple-cities about the name and disagrees with them about who paid it.

A Mbárazi elder tells it the first time the lineage's young stand at the fold gate with the milk for a stranger. A stranger on the road in Okharis is handed soured milk, cold from the shade, and the herders stand by to see whether he drinks. A child who has learned the herd learns this last, because it is the one duty at the fold that is owed to someone outside the lineage. The telling gives the young the reason in the form of a story, and the story is about strangers who drank and owed and never came back to pay what they owed.

The version here is the one **Ndárima of the Mbárazi** tells. She is the lineage's eldest woman and the one who holds its grazing rights in her head, and she tells it at the fold at dusk, after the evening count, with the cattle lowing behind her and the young ones sitting on the thorn fence. Other Mbárazi elders tell it to the same end. It quotes the fold-gate formula in Okharik, as every Mbárazi says it, and it gives the [[place-kaljekor|city's]] account of the matter where the city's priest gave it, without arguing with him.

## The Telling

Sit on the fence where you can see the gate. The count is done and the herd is in, and there is light enough for this, and if it gets dark you know the way home.

You stood at the gate today with the milk. You did not spill it, and the man drank, and he went on up the road with his donkey and his cloth and said the greeting. That was well done. It is a small thing, and you have watched your mothers do it since you could stand, and you think it is small. I am going to tell you why it is not small. It is the guest-price, and once it was paid in cattle.

This was long ago, in the time of my mother's mother's mother's mother, and further back than that, but that is as far as I can count it. The Mbárazi were in these valleys, the same valleys, in the same seasons. In the rains we were out on the long grass to the east, where the grazing ends and the dry country begins, and in the heat we came in toward the river and the village. That is the right our grandmothers held, and it is the right we hold. You know the valleys. I will name them for you another night.

The head of the lineage then was **Jamuro of the Mbárazi**. He was a man with a big herd and a short temper, and he counted his cattle at the gate every evening with his lips moving and never said the number aloud, which is the right way. His wife was **Oruna of the Mbárazi**, who had come to him from another lineage of the Táluméng with a grazing in the hills behind her, and she was cleverer than he was, which he knew. Remember her. The telling is mostly hers.

That year, at the end of the rains, a herdsman came in from the far grazing at a run, which a herdsman does not do. He said there were men on the dry country, coming west, and that he had seen their dust for two days before he saw them.

Jamuro said, "Raiders."

The herdsman said no. He said raiders come in the night and these came in the day, and raiders come few and these were more than the Mbárazi and the next lineage together, and raiders do not carry water and these carried it. Every second man of them had a skin of water on his back, and they had donkeys loaded with water, and at every place they camped they dug.

Now you know what the dry country is. It is the country east of the long grass where the stream beds are stones and the wells are a day apart, if you know where they are, and the people who know where they are do not tell. Nobody comes across it in a body. A man comes across it alone with a donkey, or in a small company of traders who know the wells. These came in a body. They came with an army, and the army carried its water and dug its own wells as it came, and built a little wall round each well it dug, and left men at each one.

The Khelathi still have those walls. Some of them stand out there now, on the dry country, broken, and the companies of the Marches use them for their own business. You have heard of them. That is what they were for. They were for this.

Jamuro sent the young men to bring the herds in close to the fold, and he sent a boy to the next lineage, and he sat down by the gate with his blade across his knees. Oruna went into the hut and came out with the milk.

He said, "What is that for?"

She said, "For the gate."

He said, "Those are not strangers on the road. That is an army."

She said, "An army is a great many strangers on the road. I have made a great deal of milk."

And she had. She had set the milk to sour three days before, when the herdsman's dust was only dust, in every vessel the hut had, and in the shade under the thorn, so that it would be cold. That is the first thing to remember about Oruna. She had made the milk before anyone told her there would be guests, because there would be, and an army that has been given milk is a different army from one that has not.

The army came to the edge of the long grass on the second day and stopped, and three men came on from it toward the fold. Two were soldiers, and they walked behind. The one in front was dressed in white linen, finer than anything the Zhíkande weave, and he had a staff of office and a gold collar. He was the envoy of the Gar-Au of Au'Khelathu. Behind him came a fourth man who was not a soldier and not an envoy, a little man with a board under his arm and a reed behind his ear and a pot of black on a cord round his neck. He was the scribe. Remember him too.

They came to the gate, and Jamuro stood up with his blade in his hand and did not raise it, and Oruna came to the gate beside him with a bowl of milk in her two hands.

She said what is said at the gate, which you said today: "_okharé, ne thalé_."

The envoy did not understand her. He had a man for that. The man who had it was one of the soldiers, a man of the Marches who had our tongue badly and theirs well, and he said in their tongue that she had greeted the envoy as one of the people. The envoy did not like that, because he was not one of the people. He was one of his own.

Then Oruna held out the bowl.

The envoy looked at it. It was sour milk in a wooden bowl, cold, with the cream gone thick on it, and he was a man who drank wine out of a cup with a gold lip. He did not know what it was. He thought it was food offered by a poor woman, which he could take or leave. He did not know that the whole of the Mbárazi lineage was standing behind the fold fence with its herd behind it, watching to see whether he drank.

And you know what it means if he does not drink. A man who will not drink at the gate is not a guest, and a man who is not a guest is not owed. He is a stranger on the road, and what is owed to a stranger on the road is nothing, and everything on our grazing is ours to defend against him. An army that will not drink at the gate is raiders, however much water it carries.

The envoy did not drink. He looked at the bowl, and he looked at Jamuro's blade, and he looked at the soldier who had our tongue, and he said something in his own tongue that the soldier did not put into ours.

The scribe drank.

He stepped round the envoy, the little man with the board, and he took the bowl out of Oruna's hands with both of his own, which is how it is taken, and he drank the whole of it standing, with his throat going, and gave the bowl back to her empty. He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. Then he said something to the envoy in their tongue, fast and low.

The soldier told it afterward, to a Táluméng herdsman, at one of those walled wells, for the price of a cow's leg. What the scribe said was this: "I have read of these people. If you do not drink they will kill you, and if you drink they will feed us. Drink."

So the envoy drank. Oruna had brought out a second bowl for him. He drank it with his eyes shut, which is not how it is drunk, and he gave the bowl back to her full of what he had not been able to finish. And the soldiers drank, because the envoy had, and when the herders behind the fence saw the soldiers drink, they put down their spears.

Now they were guests. You know what that means as well as I do, but you are going to hear it said, because it is the guest-price and that is what we are talking about. A guest is inside the giving. Whatever he is given is counted by the fire and not by the man who gives it, and what the guest does after is counted by the fire as well. A guest owes the house that fed him. He owes it the same as it owes him.

That is what our grandmothers knew and the envoy did not. He thought he had drunk a bowl of sour milk to keep a herdsman's blade down. He had made the whole army behind him the Mbárazi's guests.

They camped that night at the edge of the long grass. In the morning the envoy sent the soldier with our tongue to the fold to say what the army wanted. It wanted water from our wells for itself and its donkeys, which it had a right to ask as guests. It wanted grazing for the donkeys for three days, which it had a right to ask. And it wanted to know the road to the temple-cities, and the wells along it, and the number of the herds of the Táluméng, which was not the business of guests at all.

Jamuro was angry about the last, and he wanted to send the soldier back with nothing. Oruna said to him, "Give them the water. Give them the grazing. Give them the road. They will find it anyway with that many men. Do not give them the number of the herds." And then she said, "And give them a feast."

He said, "With what?"

She said, "With what a feast is given with."

So it was done that way. The Mbárazi watered the army at the lineage's wells, which took most of a day and left the wells low. They gave its donkeys the grazing on the near valley, which was the valley the calves were to have that month. They sent a herdsman to walk the road west with the army as far as the river, so that it would not cut across the next lineage's grazing and start a fight with them. And on the third night they gave the feast.

I am going to tell you exactly what was given, because Oruna counted it, and she said the count to her daughter, and her daughter said it to hers, and it has come down to me in the same words.

Eleven head of cattle. Four were barren cows, which is right, because you feed a guest from what is not going to calve. Three were young bulls that would not have been kept for breeding. Two were steers. Two were good cows in milk, which is more than is right, and Oruna gave them anyway, because she wanted the army to know what it was being given. All the milk of the herd for three days, soured and fresh. Sorghum from the plot, as much as the plot had, which was not much. Honey from the Shókuma that Jamuro had traded for at the last festival and had been saving. Salt.

Eleven head of cattle, out of a herd of something under three hundred. I will not tell you the herd's number. Oruna did not say it either, and I have it from my mother that she did not, and she was right not to. But you can count. Eleven head is the price of a marriage. It is the price of a man's life between two lineages, settled before the Keeper of the Feud. It was a great thing to give. It was meant to be.

The feast was given in front of the fold, in the open, by the light of three fires, with the army sitting on the grass and the Mbárazi serving them, and every lineage of the Táluméng within a day's walk came to see it. They came because they had heard. They stood at the edge of the firelight and counted. That is what a feast is for. You know this. A herd walks, and it cannot be hidden, and so a household is open-handed in public and close-handed in the hut, and what it gives at a feast is its account, given out loud, for the neighbors to count. The Mbárazi gave their account that night in front of the whole Táluméng, and the Táluméng counted it, and the count is still known. That is the second thing to remember.

The envoy ate. He ate the beef, which was good beef, and the honey, and he drank the milk, which he was getting used to, and toward the end he stood up and made a speech in his tongue, and the soldier put it into ours. He thanked the Mbárazi for their tribute to the Gar-Au of Au'Khelathu.

The soldier said _tribute_ in their tongue, because ours has no word for it. The people at the fires thought it was a Khelathi word for _gift_. Oruna knew. She had heard it said by a trader at the market at Kaljekor, about what the river towns of Au'Khelathu pay their king, and she knew it meant a thing that is owed and is paid because it must be. She did not say anything. She looked at the scribe.

The scribe was sitting at the edge of the firelight with his board on his knees and his reed out, and he had been sitting like that all evening. He was writing. Every time a cow was led into the light to be killed, he wrote. When the honey came, he wrote. When the salt came, he wrote. He was writing down the feast.

Oruna went and stood over him. The little man looked up at her and smiled, because she had given him milk, and he showed her the board. She could not read it. None of us could read, then or now. But she could see that there were marks on it in rows, and that the rows were the same length as the count, and that at the top of the rows there was a mark bigger than the others, with a little sun drawn over it.

She asked him, through the soldier, what he had written.

The soldier told it at the walled well, the same night he told the rest. The scribe said: "I have written that the herders of the western road brought to the Gar-Au's envoy eleven head of cattle, with milk, grain, honey and salt, as their due. That is what is written. When I go home it will be read in the archive at the city of the sun, and it will be the first entry under this road, and every entry after it will be counted from it."

Oruna said, "It is not a due. It is a feast."

The scribe said, "Mistress, I know that. You know that. The archive does not know that. The archive has a column for what comes in from the west, and the column is headed _due_, and there is no column for a feast. I am sorry. I liked the milk."

And Oruna laughed. My mother said she laughed. She laughed at the little man with his board and said, "Write it, then. Your archive is not a fire."

That is all there is about the scribe, except one thing, and I will tell it at the end.

The army went on west in the morning, with the Mbárazi herdsman walking ahead of it as far as the river. It went on to the temple-cities. What happened there, the priests tell, and I will tell you what they tell, because I heard it from one of them myself, at Kaljekor, when I was a young woman at my first Market of Flames.

The priests say the envoy came to the city and was received at the pyramid as a guest of the Flame. They say the city weighed out gold for him on the Scales, in front of the whole market, a weight of gold that was the guest-gift of the city to the Gar-Au, and the envoy went home with it, and with linen and iron and pyrestone besides. They say the envoys came again, every year after that for a generation, and every year the city received them, and weighed out gold, and sent them home with gifts, and that the gifts were the guest-price of a long road. And they say that the Khelathi wrote it down as tribute, as the Khelathi write everything down as something it is not, and that the city did not argue, because the Khelathi were a long way off and paid well for iron.

That is what the priest told me. He was a Servant of Gold, a fat man and a kind one, and he was telling it to a market crowd to show how the city had dealt well with a great power. He did not mention the Mbárazi. He did not mention the eleven head of cattle, or the wells, or the grazing the calves did not have that month. He did not mention that the army came across the dry country by the eastern road and over our grass, and was fed by us, and watered by us, and walked to the river by a man of ours, before it ever saw his pyramid.

I asked him. I was young and I asked him in front of the crowd. I said, "Who fed the army before it came to the city?"

He said, "The herders of the eastern grazing, I suppose. It was their honor to do it."

I said, "It was eleven head of cattle."

He said, "Then it was eleven head of honor," and the crowd laughed, and he went on with his telling.

Now listen to this part, because it is the part we argue about at the moot, and you will sit in the moot one day.

The year after the embassy, Jamuro went to the moot at Zarhanis with the elders of the Táluméng. He stood up in front of the city's priests and said that the Mbárazi had fed the army of the Gar-Au on the city's business, eleven head and the wells and the calves' grazing, and that the city had sent the army home with gold and had been thanked for it, and that the city owed the Mbárazi eleven head. He said it in front of every lineage of the Táluméng, because they had all counted the feast and could say the count with him.

The priests heard him. That is what a moot is for. They said he spoke well, and that the city honored the Mbárazi for their hospitality, which was the hospitality of all Okharis. They said the cattle had been given as a guest-gift, freely, by the custom of the gate, and that a guest-gift is not a debt, and that the city could not pay for what the Mbárazi had given freely without making it a sale, and dishonoring the gift.

And Jamuro could not answer that, because it was true. He had given it freely. You cannot give a feast and send in the price afterward. That is a merchant's feast, and the whole Táluméng would have laughed at him.

So the city was right, and the Mbárazi were eleven head short, and the grievance went into the moot and was heard and was absorbed, the way the grievances of the clans go into the moot and are heard and are absorbed. Some of the elders say that is the moot working. Some say it is the moot doing what the cities built it to do. You will hear both said, and you will say one of them yourself one day, and I will not tell you which I say.

But Oruna had the answer. She was not at the moot, because she was a wife and not the head of the lineage. When Jamuro came home and told her, she said: "The city is right. It is not the city's debt. The city did not drink at our gate. The army drank."

He said, "The army is gone back across the dry country."

She said, "It will come again. They have built walls round the wells. You do not build walls round a road you mean to walk once."

And it came again. Every year, at the end of the rains, the envoys came across the dry country by the walled wells, with soldiers and a scribe, and carriers to bring the city's gifts home. Not an army now, a company. And every year they came to the edge of the long grass and over the Mbárazi grazing, because it was the road the first army had walked and the road the walls were on. And every year Oruna met them at the gate with the milk.

She met them herself, every year until she was too old to carry the bowl, and then her daughter met them. And every year the envoy, whoever he was, drank, because the first scribe had written it down that the herders of the western road give milk at the gate and must be answered, and every envoy after read it before he came. So every year they were guests. And every year the Mbárazi fed them, not eleven head, because that is a feast and a feast is given once, but a cow, and milk, and the grazing for the donkeys, and the herdsman to walk them to the river.

And every year, when they had drunk, Oruna said the words that are said at the gate after the drinking, which you did not say today because the man with the donkey was only going up the road. These are the words. Say them with me. "_uzhelom‼ ndem thumom mbo zharu._" Drink. You are inside the giving.

They did not understand it. Nobody put it into their tongue. But it was said, every year, in front of the herders standing at the fence, and the fire heard it, and the fire counted. Every cow the Mbárazi gave at that gate was counted by the fire, and what the guests owed for it was counted too, every year, for a generation.

And then they stopped coming.

One year at the end of the rains the dust did not come up on the dry country. The herdsmen watched for it and it did not come, and the next year it did not come, and after that nobody watched. The priests say the Gar-Au's house changed, in their country, and the new house did not want a road it had to keep walls on, and called its soldiers home from the wells, and left the walls standing out there with nobody in them. The priests say that the city's gifts stopped, and that some of the last of them went no further than the walls, because nobody came from the east to collect them, and that they are still out there somewhere along the walls. Maybe. The priests know the priests' part.

Here is our part. They did not come back to pay what they owed.

They drank at our gate for a generation. Every one of them drank, and was a guest, and was fed, and what they owed was counted by the fire, every year. And they went home across the dry country owing it, and they never came back to pay it, and they never will, because their house is closed and their walls are empty. The guest-price was paid to the city, in gold, on the Scales. The guest-price was paid at our gate, in cattle. The city's was paid back to it. Ours was not.

That is why it is called the guest-price, here. In the city they say it is the price the city paid to its guests for the long road. Here we say it is the price of a guest, and a guest has not paid it until he has paid back the house that fed him. They have not paid. They owe it still. The fire has it counted.

You will ask why we go on giving the milk, then, if this is what comes of it. Every child asks. I asked my mother, on this fence. I will tell you what she told me.

We do not give the milk so that we will be paid. A household that gives in order to be paid back is a merchant, and a merchant swears on the Scales, and the Scales count. We give the milk because a man who drinks at the gate is inside the giving, and what he does after, the fire counts. The fire does not forget a count. The army drank at our gate, and it never came against us. It walked to the river behind our herdsman and went west and came back east and never once drove off a cow of ours or fouled a well of ours, in a generation of walking over our grass with soldiers. Not once. They were guests. They had drunk.

An army that has drunk at the gate is a different army from one that has not. Oruna knew that before anyone told her there would be an army. She made the milk three days before.

That is what is in the bowl when you hold it out. It is not much. It is sour milk, cold from the shade. It is the whole of the lineage standing behind the fence, and the herd behind the lineage, and the fire behind all of it, counting.

Now the one thing about the scribe, which I said I would tell at the end.

When the envoys stopped coming, there was one more year when somebody came. It was not a company. It was one old man on a donkey, alone, across the dry country, by the walled wells, which had nobody in them. He came to the edge of the long grass at the end of the rains and came on to the gate, and Oruna's daughter was at the gate by then, and she met him with the bowl.

He had a board under his arm. He was very old. He took the bowl in his two hands, the way it is taken, and drank the whole of it standing, and gave it back empty. Then he said, in our tongue, badly, which he had learned somewhere, "I liked the milk."

He did not stay. He did not say why he had come, and nobody asked him, because a guest is not asked. He stayed the night by the fold and was fed, and in the morning he gave Oruna's daughter something out of his bag and went back east across the dry country by the empty walls, and nobody saw him again.

What he gave her was a piece of board, flat, with marks on it in rows, eleven rows, and at the top of the rows a bigger mark with a little sun drawn over it, and the sun had been scraped across with a knife, so that it was not a sun anymore.

My mother had that board. It went into the ground with her when she died, because she asked for it, and so I have never seen it. She told me what was on it. Neither of us could read the marks. But she said the sun was scraped out, and she said she thought it meant he had taken the column off it. She said she thought it meant it was a feast again.

I do not know. Nobody in this lineage can read. Maybe it means he scraped out his king.

That is the guest-price. The light is gone. Go home by the fence, not across the fold, and do not wake the calves.

Tomorrow, if a man comes up the road, you will hold out the bowl.
