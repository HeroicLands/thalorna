The Kì'ìch'chik live in the pyramid cities of the heartland and the south, from the plateau of [[affiliation-ixilankul|Ish'ilankul]] and the limestone lowlands of [[affiliation-kankul|K'ankul]] to the terraced valleys of [[affiliation-ixbaltzaku|Ish'bal Tsa'ku]] and the last fortress-temples above the waste. They are many cities and one people: every city keeps its own Sun Lord and its own quarrels, and every one of them keeps the same count of days, serves the same ten gods through one [[affiliation-itzanpnthn|Itsani priesthood]], speaks [[skill-kiichek|Kì'ì'chek]], and believes the same thing about the world. The world is in debt. The sun rises because people pay for it, a household stands because its neighbors lent it fire, and a soul reaches the far side of death because its family paid for the words. A Kì'ìch'chik life is the work of paying what it owes on time, and of knowing exactly what that is.

## Where They Came From

The Kì'ìch'chik say that the world has been built and broken four times, and that the gods gave and only the gods gave in each of the four. In the fifth, [[lore-piqalkulqatdty|P'iqal Kul'qat]] took the maize of [[lore-kanixhakandty|K'anish Ha'kan]], broke it small on the stone, mixed it with water and divine blood and shaped people from it, and [[affiliation-kayikchalix|K'ayik Ch'alish]] brought the fire, and the people stood up and spoke. The serpent-keepers say the end of it aloud in [[lore-countofsuns|The Count of the Suns]]: _the people give back. This is the debt. It is ours._ The four dead suns had no one who could repay the gods, and the fifth has. That is the whole of Kì'ìch'chik history as the cities tell it: an age that lasts as long as its people keep up the payments.

Everything else they know of their past is a city's own account: which Sun Lord raised which stair, which captive went up it, which round of fifty-two years closed late. Those accounts are cut on stelae and knotted into cords, and they disagree with one another, because every city keeps the record that flatters it. The count of days is the one record no city disputes, because the priesthood keeps it for all of them.

## The Debt Is Everything

**Good is a debt paid in its time, to the one it is owed to. Evil is a debt refused, hidden, or left for someone else to pay.** The Kì'ìch'chik make no distinction in kind between owing a neighbor three sticks of firewood and owing the sun a captive. Both are _ch'imuk_, and both are kept the same way: said aloud when they are made, remembered by everyone who heard, and paid back with something better than was lent.

So manners run on the words _that is owed_. A woman who lends her neighbor wood in the doors says it as she hands the wood over, so that it is said and nobody has to say it again. A guest who drinks the bitter bean drink at a host's hearth owes that host his welcome in return. A gift given before it is asked for is a kindness, and it is also a debt laid on the one who takes it. The lowest person in a street is the one who lends nothing, because nothing is owed to him and nobody owes him a kindness in return.

Wrongs are judged by the same measure. Theft is a debt the victim never agreed to, and the thief pays it twice over or is sold to pay it. Murder takes from a household a person whose labor it was owed, and the killer's household pays the dead person's in cloth and cacao for as long as the judge sets. The worst wrongs touch the sun's ledger. A warrior who kills a man he could have taken has spent on the ground a working the city was owed, and to delay a runner of the Speaking Road is to stop the whole realm's account in its tracks; the second is punished on the pyramid.

## Standing in the City

Success is a long name and a clear account. A commoner's name has two elements, and a person who rises earns a third: a knight raised for his captives, a priest at ordination, a noble at birth. The full name is the only one knotted into the [[skill-ktzlcrdscrpt|census cords]], and the first element alone is the house name, used by kin, by equals and by those above, and never by anyone below. A person's standing is plain from which name the street uses: _Ch'akul_ to his household, _Ch'akul Ts'ultel Ish'bal Tsul_, with the title after it, to everyone beneath him.

Failure is to be struck off. Every free person (_ts'omalil_) is on a ward's roll, with a field from the ward's common land and a share of its labor, its tribute and its levy. A household that cannot pay its tribute sells its labor, and then a member of itself, into slavery for the debt, and buys him back when it can. A person whose crime the ward refuses to answer for is struck from the roll and becomes a _Ts'ikalbal_, one put outside, with no field, no ward temple and no shelter that anyone owes him, and any household may take him as a slave.

The Kì'ìch'chik value most a debt paid early, an exact count, a captive taken alive, maize, cacao and jade, and a steady hand at the moment of the blood. They value least the hoard, waste, a kill where a capture was possible, and a person who keeps no account and so can never be paid or asked to pay. They prize being on time above almost everything. A runner who was never once late is praised at his funeral for that before anything else.

## The Man Admired

[Ishkul Puq'em Tsul]{.name data-type="being/npc"} is the master of youths of a stone-cutters' ward in [[place-kultaqan|Kul'taq'an]], and the wards of the plateau name him when they say what a man should be. He was born a commoner with two names, and he took seven captives in the hunts and killed none of the men he faced, and the ward gave him his third name, _Tsul_, strength, when the Sun Lord raised him to knight. Every one of his captives came out to fight him. He has never taken a man from a field, and he says so to the boys of the ward's house of youths every year, on the first day of the war month, before they go out with the levy for the first time.

The plateau admires three things in him. He takes rather than kills, so every man he has met has gone into the city's account and none has gone into the ground. He keeps his word to his captives: at the stair he has called each one by his house name, which the vow between equals allows and which few captors bother to do. And he keeps the boys alive. More of his ward's youths come home from their first campaign than from any other ward in the city. His cost is plain to everyone. A knee broken in his fourth hunt makes him walk like an old man at fifty, the field the Sun Lord gave him goes back to the Sun Lord when he dies, and his sons are commoners with two names.

## The Woman Admired

[Nelush Ts'alam K'ushi]{.name data-type="being/npc"} is a water-priestess of [[affiliation-xaqikhanal|Shaq'ik Ha'nal]] and the midwife of the Street of the Kilns in [[place-kankul2|K'ankul]], and the lowlands name her when they say what a woman should be. She has brought most of the street into the world. She gives each child its water-marking at the hour of its birth, whatever the day, and gives it its name, and walks to the temple the same morning so that the cord-keepers knot the child into the census under the hour she saw. She has never once been wrong about an hour, and the Reckoner's scribes take her word over the family's.

Her admiration is not a smaller form of Ishkul Puq'em Tsul's. He fills the city's account with captives; she fills the city itself. The lowlands hold that a woman who dies giving birth has gone into the sun's ledger on the side of what is given, the same as a captive, and Nelush Ts'alam K'ushi carries such a woman to the temple herself and makes the priests write her down there. When a sun-priest tells her a child must wait for an open day, she holds the door open for him and tells him to explain it to the mother. Her cost is that she is never off duty. She has not slept a whole night in the dry months for thirty years, and the street's young mothers are more afraid of her than of the priests.

## What the Cities Keep

The Kì'ìch'chik write and knot a great deal, and almost all of it is an account.

- **The census cords** (_k'otsel_) carry every household of a ward, every person in it by full name, and what each owes in tribute, labor and levy. A cord is tied and read only by the priestly scribes, and a ward head who disputes his ward's count must take it to the temple.
- **The blood-ledger** is the Warden of the Debt's register in bark paper, entering each gift of blood against the celestial event it was meant to answer. At the close of every month he announces whether the city stands in surplus or in deficit, and the whole city hears it.
- **The list of open days** is what the Reckoner of Conjunctions issues to the city: the days on which a household may sow, build, marry or set out. Nothing important is begun without it.
- **The funerary codices** hold the chambers of [[place-chaltzumaq|Ch'al Ts'umaq]], each trial and each deception in its order, and the death-walkers learn them by heart.
- **The stelae** stand in the plazas and record the reigns, the raising of stairs and the captives given at the calendar's turns, in the [[skill-chlxplmscrpt|ceremonial hand]].
- **A household's own tales** are its property, told the same way every year in the doors. A family that tells a neighbor's tale as its own owes the neighbor for it.

## The Day Kept

Faith on an ordinary day is a small payment and a question asked of the count. Each morning the senior woman of the house drops a pinch of ground maize and a little copal on the hearth stones before anyone eats. Each household gives a few drops of blood at the ward temple on the days the count opens to blood, an earlobe pricked with a thorn by the adults and a fingertip by the children once they are old enough to be named in the levy, and the priest enters the gift. Before anything is begun, a household asks the solar priests for an open day and pays for it. Every Kì'ìch'chik person is expected to make the pilgrimage to [[place-tzomalchalix|Ts'omal Ch'alish]] at least once in a lifetime, to breathe the vapors of creation and stand in the shadow of the Sacred Mountain, and a family saves for years to send its members.

A person in trouble calls on the god whose business the trouble is: [[lore-puqilchaqundty|P'uqil Ch'akun]] for late rain, [[lore-xaqikhanaldty|Shaq'ik Ha'nal]] for a birth, [[lore-tzuqilixbaldty|Ts'uqil Ish'bal]] for a death. Nobody chooses a god to follow, and nobody belongs to one god's temple rather than another's.

## The Round of the Year

The year runs from winter solstice to winter solstice, eighteen months of twenty days and then the five doors, and [[lore-itsaniclndr|the calendar note]] gives the months in order. Five festivals matter to everyone.

- **The rekindling** opens the year at the winter solstice. The Sun Lord climbs the pyramid and draws his own blood into the eternal flame, and every household lights its hearth again from the temple fire. It is for beginning the year in the sun's debt rather than out of it.
- **The summer solstice** falls in the month of sunlight, and the captives taken in the hunts die on the pyramid's summit at noon. It is for paying the year's great installment.
- **The harvest festivals** of the golden flower month are the most lavish of the year. The temples of [[affiliation-kanixhakan|K'anish Ha'kan]] open their granaries, feed the whole city, slaves included, and receive the Sun Lord as a supplicant. It is for the maize giving back what it was given, and the city eats its fill once a year.
- **The Calling of the Dead** comes at each turning of the moon's count, when the ward gathers at the mouth of its sacred pool through the night, brings food it will not eat, and says aloud the full name of everyone lost since the last turning. The children are brought so that they grow up unafraid of the mouth of the world, and it ends at dawn with a meal and a good deal of maize beer. It is for sending the dead on.
- **The doors** (_shots'emal_) end the year: five days outside the months, when nothing is begun, the kilns go cold, and every household sits up in turns to keep its fire alive, borrowing wood from its neighbors where it must and telling its own tales, as in [[lore-doornights|The Nights of the Doors]]. It is for keeping the boundary between the worlds shut.

Every fifty-two years the two counts meet and the doors end in the [[lore-itsaniclndr#round|Living Flame]]: every fire in the city is put out, the city keeps silence for a day and a night, and at sunrise on the solstice the fire-priests draw a new flame from the sun with obsidian mirrors, and runners carry it to every hearth. A person sees one Living Flame in a lifetime, or two if they are lucky, and every grandmother tells the one she saw.

## Rewards and Punishments

Reward is a name, a dress and a field. A child who learns well is put forward for the temple school. A youth who takes his first captive is given the dress that his captive earns him, and each captive after it adds to the dress, so a warrior's standing can be read across a plaza. A man raised to knight takes a third name, eats in the lord's hall and holds a field for life. A commoner who serves a ward well is chosen by its elders as its head. The highest reward a household can be given is a place for its dead in the wall of the temple's pool chamber, close to the water.

Punishment runs from a fine to the loss of the passage below. The first is payment: the wrongdoer's household pays the wronged one in cloth, cacao or labor, for as long as the judge sets. The second is labor on the temple platforms and the causeways, in the sight of the street. The third is sale into slavery, for theft or for an unpaid debt, from which a slave may buy himself free. The fourth is to be put outside the ward. The fifth is death on the pyramid, for the gravest crimes against the sun's account. The last, and the one the Kì'ìch'chik fear more than death, is exclusion from the funerary rites: a soul sent into Ch'al Ts'umaq with no recitation, which the priests pass only on traitors to the faith, on the Serpent Awakeners, and on a person who has harmed a runner.

## Two Hands on One City

Civil authority and religious authority are two institutions, and they are rivals who cannot do without each other. Each city has its Sun Lord (_Ts'enmah_), who leads its armies and its captive-hunts, takes tribute from the towns under him and rules through his lords, his judges and his stewards, and each city has its high priest (_Ch'alish Tq'an_), who answers to the hierarchy of the whole faith and to no Sun Lord. No Sun Lord rules without the priesthood's sanction, and the priesthood can withdraw it. The most powerful Sun Lords have a voice in who is made high priest in their cities. Neither has ever won the argument outright.

Cases are tried by the lord's judges, who sit in the lord's hall from dawn to dusk in a noble court and a commoners' court, and a judge who takes a gift from a party is put out of office. Merchants are judged by their own guild's court. The temples try crimes against the sun's account and against a runner. The records are the priesthood's: the census cords, the blood-ledger and the list of days are all kept by priestly scribes, and no Sun Lord receives word from a distant province except through the priests who run the [[affiliation-chumishlan|Ch'um Ish'lan]], the Speaking Road. The Sun Lord's stewards collect the tribute in maize, cloth, cacao and feathers. The priesthood collects its own tithe on every load that moves along the white roads.

When the two answers conflict, the count decides. A Sun Lord may order a war, a marriage or a new stair, and only the Reckoner can open a day for it. A Sun Lord who wants a war the priesthood does not want finds that no day in the season is open for it, and a high priest who wants a war the Sun Lord will not fight can open every day in the war month and wait.

## The Passage Below

A person who dies goes down through [[place-chaltzumaq|Ch'al Ts'umaq]], the labyrinth beneath every sinkhole, cave and sacred spring, a chamber at a time. Each chamber holds a trial or a deception, and a soul that knows them in order passes through and comes out on the far side of the night, reborn. A soul that is turned aside becomes one of the _ts'uqilob_, lost in the dark. The death-walkers (_ish'bal'ob_) of [[affiliation-tzuqilixbal|Ts'uqil Ish'bal]] know the chambers, and they sell the knowledge: the Speaker of the Trials sits with the body through the night and names each chamber and each deception, while the family listens and does not speak. A family pays for accuracy. A full recitation costs more than most households earn in a year, and a short one is honest about what it leaves out.

That belief governs an ordinary day more than any law. A household puts by for its funerals before it saves for anything else, and most wards keep a burial society, its members paying into a common store against the day one of them dies, with the death-walkers keeping the rolls. A dying person calls in everyone he owes and everyone who owes him, and settles each debt aloud, because the households teach that a debt carried below is the deception best fitted to turn a soul aside: a voice in the dark that calls a person by his full name and asks to be paid. Inheritance follows the same settlement. A household's field-right passes to whoever keeps working the field, and its debts pass with it. The dead go to the temple's pool chamber, given to the black water where it is deep and set in niches cut in the walls where it is not, and in the owl country they are laid out on platforms in the sacred groves. A traveler who dies on the white road is the road shrine keeper's charge, and the keeper says [[lore-roadrecitation|the road recitation]] over him for nothing.

## The Living and the Dead

The living reach the dead through the recitation, at the death, and through the Calling of the Dead at every turning of the moon, when the dead are named and sent on. The dead reach the living in dreams. A dream of the dead is taken seriously and taken to a dream-keeper of [[affiliation-naliktzuqal|Nal'ik Ts'uqal]], who sends the dreamer on to the death-walkers rather than read it alone, and a dream of an unpaid debt is the commonest of them. The death-walkers charge for a second, shorter recitation for a soul that appears to be lost, and families pay it.

A third way back is forbidden. The [[affiliation-tzumaqkalanil|Serpent Awakeners]] of the deep jungle can draw the newly dead out of the labyrinth before their trials are done, and they do it for anyone who asks. The soul comes back damaged, and the damage cannot be undone. The priesthood hunts the Awakeners and denies them the rites, and every few years a grieving household in some city goes to them anyway.

## What a Person Owes

The Kì'ìch'chik state what a person owes in order: blood to the sun on the days the count opens to it; labor, tribute and service in the levy to the ward; the hearth kept alive in the doors to the household; what was lent, said aloud and paid back better, to every neighbor; and a recitation for every one of the household's dead. A noble owes the city captives and his blood at the calendar's turns. A Sun Lord owes the whole city a year that the ledger can pay for.

In return a person is owed what the ward and the temple give. A free person is owed a field from the ward's common land for as long as the household works it, a place on the roll and in the census, a share of the harvest festival's food, an open day for the things that need one, the levy's protection, a place in the burial society's store, and a recitation at death. A slave is owed food, the harvest festival's meal, the right to buy himself free, and free children.

## The Weak

The ward is the first resort and the temple the last. A widow keeps her husband's field-right for as long as she and her children work it, and the ward's levy owes her a man's share of labor in the planting and the harvest if she has no son old enough. An orphan (_nalshubal_) is placed by the ward head with a household of its roll, often the mother's kin, and keeps its own name and its parents' field-right until it is old enough to work it. A household with no wood in the doors goes down its street, and every house gives it a stick. A household that has lost everything sells its labor and then its members, and the law that a slave may buy himself free is the street's assurance that this is not forever.

The temple's charity is a duty and not a kindness. The granaries of K'anish Ha'kan feed the whole city at the harvest and ration it in a lean year, and the priests who keep them count what they give out as part of the city's account. A stranger in a city has no ward and is owed nothing by any household, and is fed and housed by the road shrines of [[affiliation-xilanixlan|Shilan Ish'lan]], which ask nothing of a traveler and keep a count of who has passed.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot work the field or march with the levy is kept by the household, and the ward finds work that its roll needs done. A warrior lamed in the hunts trains the youths, as Ishkul Puq'em Tsul does. A woman whose hands are too stiff to grind maize keeps the household's tales and its count of what it is owed. A blind person learns recitations by ear, and the road shrines and the death-walkers' schools take the blind as students more readily than the sighted, because the work is to remember. A household whose earner is sick for a season borrows against the next harvest from its neighbors, says what it owes, and pays it back when he is well.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household and then the ward. Teaching is the household's work until the fifteenth year: a child learns its work from its parents and its tales from its grandparents. From the fifteenth year a ward's boys sleep in its house of youths under the master of youths, work the ward's labor and train for the levy and the captive-hunts. A girl learns her work at home and from the women of her street, and a girl or a boy with a head for figures is brought to the temple steps at the new year in the hope of a place in the temple school, where the nobles' children are taught alongside them. Defense is the ward's levy, which marches under its quarter lord.

The Kì'ìch'chik fight in steel, with blades and iron-headed spears, and the richest warriors wear a steel breastplate. The armor of feathers and leather that records a warrior's captives is worn in procession and at the rites. A city's wars against its neighbors are fought for captives and for roads, and the hunts are bound by rules the cities agree between them: who may be taken, in which month, and how a captive is kept.

## The Household

The household (_sh'ulum_) is a courtyard of rooms around one hearth and the extended family that shares it: a couple, their married sons and their families, their unmarried daughters, and any orphan or widowed kinswoman the ward has placed there. Membership comes from the father's side for the field-right and the ward roll and from either side for the name. Households that share a founding ancestor are a clan (_nalash ts'omesh_), which holds its fields together in the south, where the kin group is the unit that works the labor turn, and shares a burial society in the cities.

Marriage is made on an open day, which the families pay the solar priests to find. The groom's family gives the bride's family cloth and cacao, and the bride's family gives the couple a hearth-stone from its own fire, so that the new hearth is lit from the old.

The age ladder is plain. A child is born, given its water-marking and its name by the midwife at the hour of its birth, and knotted into the census the same day. It works beside its parents from the time it can carry water. At fifteen a boy goes to the house of youths and a girl takes her place at the grinding stone, and either may take the temple school if the temple will have them. An adult marries, works the field, marches, pays and is paid. The old (_sh'umlibal_) choose the ward head and say what the ward's customs are, and an elder who has paid off every debt of his household is honored as nobody else is. The dying settle their accounts aloud. The dead go down, and the household pays for their passage.

## The Taking of Farmers and the Uncounted

The sin the Kì'ìch'chik admit is the taking of farmers. The captive-hunt is for warriors who come out to be taken, and a captive is worth what he gave in the fight. A city in deficit, with the solstice close and the ledger short, sends its levy out of season to take the people of a rival's fields instead: farmers, potters, women at the river. The temples enter such captives in the ledger at a fraction of a warrior's worth and count a captor who takes them as a man who has spent a working badly, and every city accuses its rival of doing it. The cities know which of them have done it in living memory, and the Living Flame that closes each round of fifty-two years is preceded, in every city, by a season of hunts that break the rules.

The blind spot is the one the ledger cannot see. The Warden of the Debt announces the city's blood every month, to the drop. Nobody announces the households that sold themselves in a hungry year while the festival granaries stood full, because the granaries are the god's and the hunger is the household's. The [[affiliation-pikob|Owl Keepers]] say that a city which counts its blood and not its hunger is keeping the wrong account, and that the Fifth Age is owed people as much as it is owed blood. The priests of K'anish Ha'kan answer that the granaries feed the whole city at the harvest, and that a granary emptied in the hungry season is a festival the god does not get. Ishkul Puq'em Tsul has refused to take farmers all his life, and Nelush Ts'alam K'ushi has refused to let the priests leave a dead mother out of the ledger, and neither of them has changed the count.
