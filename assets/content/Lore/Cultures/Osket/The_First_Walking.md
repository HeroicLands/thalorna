**The First Walking**, _Achalk pultom_, is the [[lore-osketclt|Osket]] account of how the first road over [[place-graznmntns|the Grazian wall]] was found and held. It is a _rulshar_, store-talk: the prose a hearth-warden tells at the fire through the closed months, when the doors of the passes are shut, the village lives on its common store, and the hearth has the whole winter to fill. Store-talk is plain and exact, and its teller says how she knows each part of it, as the tongue obliges her to: what she has seen, what she was told and by whom, and what can be read from the road.

The telling below is [Kharsel]{.name data-type="being/npc"}'s, hearth-warden of [Randug]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}, a [[place-suryadvar|Suryadvar]] hearth of [[place-oskhelt|Oskhelt]], given in the deep of the closed months with a lowland guest at the fire, a man the door had let in on the last day of the open season and the snow had kept. Store-talk told with a stranger at the hearth is told as a stranger may hear it. The forces that live in the range go unnamed in it, and at the col the telling goes over without a word about what is there; that part is told at Randug when the door has nobody inside it. The hearths of [[place-tsokhar|Tsokhar]] tell the walking as a walking of [[place-meghadvar|Meghadvar]], and Kharsel gives their account beside her own without choosing between them.

## The Telling

The door is shut against the snow and open to whoever reaches it, and you reached it, so you eat from the store until the thaw. You have eaten. Now you hear what the store hears in the closed months, which is the talk. Sit where the smoke goes up and not in your face. You will be told what was done and where a thing began. You will not be told what the road tells us, because you are a guest and not a daughter, and that is the same for every guest this hearth has had, and there have been a great many.

This is the first walking. I was told it by my mother at this fire, in the winters, and she was told it by hers, and so on back. That is how I know it, and I say so first, so you know what you are hearing. Where I have seen a thing myself I will tell you that too. Where a thing can be read from the road I will tell you that, and you can go and read it in the open months, if you have the boots for it.

Before there was a road, the wall was the mountain's.

That is the first thing, and I was told it, and so was everybody. The mountain was there and the ones who live in it were there. Who they are is not said to a guest, and I do not say it. The ice was where it is, and the snow came and went as it does, and nothing crossed. There was the low country on this side, warm and wet, which you have come up out of, and on the other side there was the low country of the far edge, dry and hot, and between them there was the wall, and the wall was nobody's road, because nobody had walked it.

We were here already. We were on the high pastures with the yaks, under the wall and not over it. The yaks were here before us, my mother said, and we came up after the yaks, because the yaks go where the grass is and we go where the yaks are. That is told, and I believe it, because I have seen where a yak will go for grass, and it will go anywhere. The hearths were hearths then and not roads. A hearth was a house and a herd and a high pasture, and that was all a hearth was, and it was enough while the grass held.

Then there was a winter that did not end. I was told this. It went on into the time of the thaw and through it, and the snow lay past the knee on the high pastures into the time when the calves should have been dropping, and the calves were dropped into the snow and died there, and the yaks began to eat the roofs. The low country on this side was no good to us. We had nothing the low country wanted and it had nothing it would give for nothing. The store went down. A hearth whose store goes down in a winter that does not end is a hearth that is going to stop.

There was a woman at a hearth then whose name was [Ghuqom]{.name data-type="being/npc"}. You know the word, or you do not, so I will tell you: _ghuqom_ is the stillness before snow, the hour when the air goes flat and the birds stop and the dogs lie down, and it is felt with the back of the neck before it is seen. She was given it because she was born in that hour. Names are not inherited up here. A name is a word. Hers was a word for watching.

She watched the peak. She did that all that winter, every morning at first light, from the door, while the others watched the store. She watched what the cloud did on the summit and not what the sky did, because she had seen, she said, that the sky over the low country and the sky over the wall were one sky and told you nothing, and the cloud on the peak was the peak's own and told you what the peak would do. That is the first morning word, and it is the oldest thing in the store-talk, and it is still said: read the peak and not the sky. My mother said it to me before I could walk and I have said it to every child at this fire. It was said to you at the foot of the road, I expect, as a thing to do. Now you know it has an age. It still has no reason that you will hear.

At the end of that winter Ghuqom saw the cloud lift off the summit for three mornings together and not come back, and she put on her boots.

I will tell you what she took, because the telling gives it, every time, the same: a rope of yak hair, a bag of roasted barley, a skin of butter, a knife, and a cord of hide with nothing on it. She took no load and no beast. She went out of the door in the dark before first light, alone, and she went up.

Here the telling goes stage by stage, the way a road is said. I will not give you the stages by their names. You have walked some of them with your own caravan, and those names are not given to a caravan.

The first day she went up from the last of the pasture to a water that comes out from under the stones, and slept beside it. The second day she went up a slope of loose stone, and every step slid back half a step, and she was a whole day on a slope a yak would cross in a morning in the open months. The third day she came to a shelf, a long shelf, a ledge on the side of the mountain with the drop on the one hand and the rock on the other, and the snow on the shelf was past her knee, and she went along it. The fourth day she was still on the shelf. When a stage is a day's walk between shelters, the long shelf is two stages, and that is because Ghuqom was two days on it, and the shelf has not got any shorter since. That much you can read from the road. I have walked the long shelf. It is two days.

At every place where she slept she tied a knot in the cord. One knot for the water. One knot for the stones. Two knots for the shelf, close together, because it was one shelf and two days. Her knots were the first cord. That is told, and I tell it as told, and I have never seen her cord, because nobody has. A cord is read by its keeper, and when its keeper dies, if she has not given it, it is not read again.

On the fifth day she came out above the shelf into a high place where there was no water and nothing to make a shelter of except loose stones, and the hail came, the way it comes up there, out of a clear sky in the afternoon, hard enough to cut, and she lay down against the slope with her bag over her head and let it come. When it had gone she piled stones. She built a wall of stones, low, the height of her knee, and lay behind it that night. That wall is the first shelter on any road over the wall, and it is still standing, and it is higher now, because everyone who has slept behind it since has put a stone on it in the morning. I have put a stone on it. You may put one on it in the spring. You may not take one off.

The sixth day she came up into the steps, where the slope goes up in shelves of rock one above the other like the floors of a house, and she went up them on her hands. The seventh day she came to the col.

_The teller is silent while the fire is mended._

She came down.

That is what is said to a guest, and it is all that is said. What was on the col is not told at this hearth while you are at it, and you will not ask, because you have eaten from the store and a guest who has eaten does not ask what the store has not offered. I will tell you that she came down by the way she had gone up, and she tied no knot on the col, and the count of a road says the col as the col and nobody stops there. That is all.

The going down was worse. It is worse now. I have walked a road, and I tell you so: the going down is when a person stops. She came down the steps backward, with her face to the rock. She came down past the wall of stones and slept behind it again. She came along the shelf, and on the shelf the cold took her feet.

I was told this part plainly, and I tell it plainly. She lay two nights on the long shelf in the snow, because the snow came again, and when she stood in the morning she could not feel the ground. She walked on feet she could not feel for two days, along the shelf and down the stones to the water, and on the last day she crawled. Her brother found her at the water. He had gone up as far as the water every morning since she went, because that was as far as he had seen her go and he did not know the way any further. He carried her down to the hearth on his back.

His name was [Tsum]{.name data-type="being/npc"}. That is the word for the col, the saddle at the top of a road. At this hearth we say he was named for the col after the first walking, because he walked to it after her, more times than anybody. At Tsokhar they say he had the name from his birth, and the col was named for him, because he was the first man to stand on one. I was told it both ways. I tell it both ways. It does not matter to the road which came first. It matters to Tsokhar, a little.

Ghuqom's feet went black, and the women of the hearth took the black off, and she lived. She never walked a road again. She sat in the door of the hearth with her feet wrapped and read the peak every morning, and she had the cord in her lap.

That is the division, and you have seen it, though you did not know you were seeing it. The knowledge of the road is a woman's and the walking of it is a man's. It was not made that way because it was better. It was made that way because the first one who knew the road could not walk it any more, and the one who could walk it did not know it, so she told him and he went. Every guide-mother since has the knowledge and the cord and the morning, and every man of a road walks what she opens and stays where she closes. That is not a ceremony. Caravan-masters offer gold to a road-holder for a morning his guide-mother has shut, every season, and nothing comes of it.

The next open season, when the cloud lifted off the peak and stayed off, Ghuqom said the first morning word that was ever said to a man about a road. It is the word that is said now. She said, "The road is open," and he went.

He went up with the cord she had tied. Not in his hand; she kept it. In his head. Before he went she said the cord to him, knot by knot, the way the count is said at the closing, stage and stage: the water, the stones, the shelf and the shelf, the wall, the steps, the col. And he walked it. And he came back, and told her what he had seen, and if what he had seen was not what she had tied she untied the knot and tied it again. That is how a cord is kept. It is told, and it is also what my mother's mother did with the cord of this hearth, and my mother after her, and our cord-keeper now, and I have sat by them and watched.

Tsum walked the road to the col four times that season, and the fourth time he went over.

Now the far side. I was told it.

He went down the far side of the wall, which nobody had walked, by himself, with her cord in his head ending at the col and nothing after it. He tied the far side himself, in his head, stage by stage, as she would want it tied, and he came down out of the ice into stones and out of the stones into dust, and at the bottom of the dust there were the towns of the far edge, where the people wrap their faces against the dust and the water comes out of the ground and is fought over. He went into a town. The telling does not say which, because the towns have changed and the road has not. He had nothing to trade but the rope and the knife, and nobody in the town could understand him, and he could understand nobody, and he stood in the street and was looked at.

There he saw a thing, and the telling gives it every time, and it is why we are what we are. He saw that there was salt in that town, white salt, in heaps, worth nothing because there was so much of it, and he saw that there was no barley. And he had come out of the low country on our side, where there was barley in heaps and the salt was carried up the rivers from a long way off and cost what silver costs.

He came back over. He told her. She tied it.

That is told, and the rest is the trade, which you know, because you are the trade. I will tell you only the first of it.

The second winter after the first walking a man came up out of the low country on our side, a lowlander, in the closed months, in the snow, alone. Nobody comes up in the closed months. He came anyway. He had heard, the telling says, that there was a woman under the wall who knew a way over it, and he wanted to go over, and he did not know you cannot go over in the closed months, because he was a lowlander, and he came up through the snow to find her, and he reached the door of Ghuqom's hearth with his hands frozen to his staff.

The store was short. It was always short in those years. It was a store for one hearth in a bad winter, and the yaks were thin, and there was nothing in it for anybody who was not of the blood.

Ghuqom was in the door. She looked at his boots. She looked at his hands. She looked at the sky behind him. In that order. You know the order, because it was done to you on the steps of this village the day you came up, by a woman with a cord on her wrist, and you thought she was a strange woman. She was not a strange woman. She was doing what Ghuqom did.

Then Ghuqom moved aside from the door and pointed him to the fire, and he was fed from the short store, and he was housed until the thaw, and his hands were saved, most of them. Nobody asked him what he could pay. She did not ask his name. The telling does not give his name. Nobody knows it. I was told that she said, "Whoever reaches the door is fed," and that nobody at the hearth said anything against it, though there was not enough, and that every hearth on the wall has said it since and kept it, in every winter, in the short ones hardest. You have eaten from this store tonight, and the store is short tonight, and I have said it, and you are eating. That you can see.

In the spring he went down. In the next open season he came back, up out of the low country, with eleven others and with mules, and barley on the mules, and he asked to be taken over.

_The teller is silent a moment._

Tsum took him over. Tsum took them all over, with Ghuqom's morning word every day and her cord in his head and his own on the far side, and the mules went down into the dust with the barley on them, and the lowlander went into the town where the salt was, and Tsum stood in the street beside him while he traded, and then they came back over with the salt.

At the foot of the road, at the last of the pasture, where the road ends and the low country begins, Ghuqom was waiting, carried down on a yak to meet them. She looked at what the mules carried. She looked at it for a long time, the telling says, the whole of a morning, every load, and the lowlander waited, not knowing what for.

Then she said, "Half of that is the road's."

The lowlander said that it was his salt, and his barley that had bought it, and his mules, and that half was a great deal.

She said, "Half of what lands at the far end." And she said nothing else, though he talked until noon.

He paid it. Not at the start, when he had his barley and had seen nothing; at the end, when he had his salt and had seen what the road left him. That is how the fee is taken now, at the far end and not before. You have been told that already by a fee-reckoner in the open, in one breath, as a rule. Now you know where the breath came from. It came from her, and she did not give the lowlander any reason, and the fee-reckoners do not give you one.

And he came back the next year with more mules. Lowlanders do.

That is how a hearth became a road. Before the first walking a hearth was a house and a herd and a pasture. After it a hearth was a house and a herd and a pasture and one road, and the road is the part that matters, because it is what the far end pays half for. The hearths that walked roads after Ghuqom's each held the road they walked, by descent, and held no other. Ghuqom's daughters held hers. That is told. Which road it was, I have told you: at this hearth we say it was the one that comes up under this village, the great one, and at Tsokhar they say it was the storm-door in the west. I was told it both ways. I have walked only ours.

Now the dead. I was told this, and it is also the count, which is said at every closing, and you will not hear the count of this hearth, because it is said for the hearth.

In the third season after the first walking, the cloud lifted off the peak at first light, and Ghuqom said the road was open, and Tsum went up with a party, and on the steps a man stopped. The stone he stood on came away, at noon, under a clear sky, and he went with it. He was a man of the hearth. His name was [Golkash]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, which is the word for scree, a slope of loose stone, and that is a name we still give and you have very likely heard it shouted on the road, because there are a great many Golkash.

When Tsum came down and told her, Ghuqom tied it. She had to. The cord is the road, and he was on the road, at the steps, where the road was, with his load and on that day, and she could not tie the steps again without tying him into them. So the first dead went into the first cord at the stage where he stopped, with what he carried and the day. And that is why the dead are in the cord. They are not in it to be remembered. They are in it because they are where the road is, and the road cannot be said without them.

I will tell you what she said when she tied him, because it is said still, at every closing. She said, "The morning was open. The stone did not ask the morning." And she tied the knot, and said the next morning when it came.

That is the thing a guide-mother does. If the morning was wrong, it is counted. Then the next morning is said. It is said to every daughter on the road. I was not a daughter for the road, I am a warden, but I was told it, and I have watched my sister say the morning on the day after a morning that was counted, at first light, in the same voice as the day before. That I have seen.

So the road is made of them. Every stage of every road over the wall has its dead, and the cord says where each of them stopped, and a guide who walks a stage walks past them, and that is how they are reached. Nobody is sent down to the low country to find them. Nobody says where they go. We say where they stopped. Where they go, I say what my mother said: they are on the road. Whoever walks it passes them.

_The teller mends the fire again._

There is one more thing in the walking, and it is short, and it is the one thing in the store-talk that is told without a story under it.

When Ghuqom was old, the telling says, her daughter asked her what the road was worth. She meant: what could be had for it. The lowlanders were coming every season by then, with more mules, and some of them had begun to ask, as lowlanders do, what it would cost to be told the way, so as not to need a guide.

Ghuqom said, "The road is not given for anything."

Her daughter asked why.

Ghuqom said it again.

That is all of it. A man who gives a road for something is out of every door on the wall. He is not fed, he is not housed, and he is not taken over, not by any hearth, ever. He is Roadless. That is the one door that is shut, and you know now that it is shut by the oldest word on the wall but one. I will not tell you how a Roadless man is dealt with. I do not tell my own children, at this fire, with the door full. You are the door's, and this is as far as the door goes.

So that is the first walking. The woman who read the stillness walked to the col and back and the walking took her feet. Her brother walked what she opened. The first knot was a water and the first dead was a scree. The first stranger was fed from a short store. The first half was paid at the far end. And the road is not given.

When a hearth loses its cord, because its keeper died before she gave it, the hearth walks its road again. It goes up with no load and no party, slowly, from the last of the pasture to the far end and back, stage by stage, and looks, the way Ghuqom looked, and ties what it finds, and the cord begins again from the new knots. It is a hard season. It costs a season's fee and sometimes it costs more than that. My grandmother's sister did it, for a hearth of the central roads, and I was told she said that it was the only time in her life she walked a road the way the first one was walked, and that she would not have missed it. I have not done it. I hope I do not.

That is the walking. The door is shut against the snow and open to whoever reaches it. The store is open to you until the thaw. Sleep near the fire. In the morning the snow-watcher will read the peak, and she will not tell you what she reads, and now you know why she will not tell you, which is that nobody has ever told anybody, since the first morning.
