**The Third at the Door**, _Achilk shomöl_, is store-talk of [[place-tsokhar|Tsokhar]], told at the fire in the closed months, and it is told there more than most because it happened there, at a hearth that still stands. It is a telling of the door, the one rule of the [[lore-osketclt|Osket]] that lowlanders find entirely legible: whoever reaches a door is fed and housed, without payment and without exception, except the Roadless. Its warden keeps the rule through a winter when the store will not stretch, against a lowlander who has eaten at her door for years and against her own blood, and what breaks the night open is done by the one person at the hearth the rule does not bind.

The telling below is [Belkhat]{.name data-type="being/npc"}'s, of the hearth of [Burnakh]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} at Tsokhar, who was a child at that hearth in that winter and tells it to the hearth's own children. It is told among the hearth with no stranger at the fire. Belkhat says, as the tongue requires, what she saw herself, what her grandmother the warden told her, and what she does not know, and the hearth's children are taught to hear the three apart. The third at the door is not named in it. The hearth that tells it does not say his name at the door or anywhere else.

## The Telling

You know the rule of the door, because you were born inside one. Whoever reaches the door is fed. There is one man who is not, and you know which. Tonight is the telling of the winter my grandmother kept both halves of that, in the same week, at this door, and I was in this room, so some of it I saw and I will say which. The rest she told me, and she is in the count now, so you have it from me as told. Sit down.

It was a short year. I saw this part. Two caravans were due over the storm-door that season and one came, because the cloud would not come off the peak, and the guide-mother of this hearth, who was my grandmother's sister, held the road for eleven mornings together in the second month while the caravan-master sat in our yard and shouted. She did not explain it to him. He went back down to the low country with his loads and did not cross, and the half of what lands at the far end did not land, because nothing went over. So there was the share of one caravan that year, at a hearth that lives off its yaks anyway, and you know what one caravan is worth. It buys grain from the drovers. It does not buy much.

Then the snow came early, and stayed. You have seen a winter like that once, the year your father brought the yaks into the house. That was a soft one beside it.

My grandmother was the hearth-warden. Her name was [Yulmum]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, which is deep snow, lying past the knee, and she said she was given it because she came in the winter and took a long time to go. She kept the store. It is the room at the back, where it still is, and in those years it had one door and one key and the key was round her neck on a cord. She went in every morning and looked, and came out, and said nothing, and that was how we knew how the store was. If she said nothing, it was short. That winter she said nothing every morning from the closing to the thaw.

The first to reach the door was the drover.

You know who I mean, because he is still coming. He is old now and comes up with one mule instead of six, but you have seen him: the lowlander with the red cloth round his head who brings the last grain up out of the marches before the closing and is paid for it in coin, which is how the grain is bought here, and is asked nothing, which is how the drovers are dealt with here. And then he does not go down. He reaches the door instead. Every year. He sells the grain to the hearth, he takes the coin, and the snow comes, and he is at the door, and he is fed from the store he has just been paid for filling, until the thaw, and then he goes down fat and buys more grain with our coin and brings it up again.

I was eight that year, and I had worked this out, the way a child works a thing out, all at once, in the middle of the meal. I said it out loud. I said, "He is eating the grain we bought from him."

My grandmother said, "Whoever reaches the door is fed."

I said that he had reached it on purpose.

She said, "Whoever reaches the door is fed." And then she said, "Feed the one who has eaten here before. Feed him again." That is a morning word, she said, and I was too young to have been taught it, and now I had been. That I heard. I was eight and I remember the sound of the spoon she hit the pot with.

The drover did not understand a word of it. He does not speak more than the road-talk, twenty words, and the road-talk has no word for any of it. He ate. He was a big man then and he ate like a big man. He slept by the fire where you are sitting and snored. He was not a bad guest. He mended things. He carried water. He told stories in his own tongue that nobody understood and laughed at them himself, and after a while the little ones laughed too because he laughed. I did not laugh. I watched the store door.

The second to reach the door were our own.

In the deep of the closed months the snow came down on the high end of the village for three days without stopping, and on the third night the roof of the hearth of [Shomber]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} came in under it. I did not see that. I heard it, everybody heard it, it was like the steps coming down. They got out. Four of them, a man and his wife and his mother and a baby, and they came down through the snow to our door in the dark, because ours was nearest, and my grandmother opened it.

Hearth-kin are not the door. They are owed the shelter of a hearth anyway. But hearth-kin eat from the store as well as a guest does, and the store was short before they came. Now there were eleven of us at the fire and the drover, and the store was the same store.

So she measured. I saw this. She stopped putting the pot on the fire and letting it be eaten from. She put the barley in her hand instead, one hand for each person at the fire, roasted, into each person's bowl, with the butter tea, morning and evening. One hand. The same hand to the drover as to the baby's mother, and to me, and to herself. I watched her hand go into the sack and come out, every morning and every evening, and I watched it come out the same, and I was hungry enough to count the grains of what she gave the drover and check them against mine, and I did, twice, and they were the same. Her own hand she gave herself last, and it was smaller. That I saw too, because I was watching for it the way I was watching for the drover's. I told nobody. A child of eight who knows that the warden is eating less than her own hand is a child who does not sleep well.

The men killed a yak in the middle of the closed months, which you do not do. You know why you do not do it. It was done.

Then the third came.

_Belkhat waits until the children are quiet._

It was the worst night of that winter. The storm had been on the village two days and the wind was the kind that comes over the col in the west and down the valley and takes the turf off roofs. Nobody went out. The beasts were in under the floor and we could hear them moving. The shutters were barred and the door was barred. And in the night there was somebody at the door.

I did not see who it was. I was told. The door was not opened.

You must understand that nobody comes to a door on a night like that. Not from Tsokhar, and not from anywhere. The drover was asleep. My grandmother was not, because she never was. She went to the door and put her face to the crack of it and asked who was there, and the one outside said his name.

I heard him say it. I did not hear what it was, because of the wind, and I have never asked. I heard her stop. I heard her stand at the door a long time without moving, with her hand flat on the bar and not lifting it. I heard her go back to the fire and sit down in her place with her back to the door. Then I knew who it was, by what she did, and so did everybody awake at the hearth, and nobody said anything. That is read, not seen. You can read a thing like that. You will.

He was Roadless.

I am not going to tell you whose blood he was. You know whose, or you will be told when you hold a share of this hearth, and that is not tonight. I will tell you that he had been of this hearth, and that two years before that winter he had taken a party over the storm-door with the road open on the morning, and on the cold shelf going down he had gone a way the cord did not say, a shorter way, to save a day, because the party was paying by the day; and the shelf had moved, the way it does in a warm year, and the party stopped there. Four of them. Lowlanders. He came down. They did not. A party lost through carelessness: you know what that is. It is the same as selling the road. He was put out of every hearth. He had not been seen since, by anyone of ours. And now he was outside our door in the worst storm of the worst winter, and he had said his name through the crack.

My grandmother did not open the door. That is the rule. She did not say his name, then or ever after, at the door or in the house or in the count. That is the rule too. He is not in the count of this hearth, because he did not stop on our road; he is not anywhere in any cord of ours. That is what being Roadless is. He knocked again. She did not get up. I saw her face, in the firelight, with her back to the door, and I am not going to tell you what it looked like, because she never did.

Here is the part I saw, all of it, with my own eyes, and I say it as seen.

The drover woke up. The knocking woke him. He sat up by the fire and looked at the door, and looked at my grandmother sitting with her back to it, and looked at the rest of us, all awake, all not looking at anything. He said something in his own tongue. Nobody answered. He said something in the road-talk, the word for door and the word for man, with his hands. Nobody answered that either.

He got up. He took his own pack from the corner where he kept it, the pack he brought up the road with him every year with his own food in it, his lowland food that he had not eaten all winter because he had been eating ours. He took his blanket off the floor. He went to the door and lifted the bar.

My grandmother did not stop him. That I saw. She did not turn round. She did not say a word. He opened the door and the storm came into the room and the fire lay down flat, and he went out into it with his pack and his blanket, and pulled the door to behind him, and we barred it.

He was out there all night.

What he did out there I did not see. I was told nothing of it, by anybody, because nobody knew; and the drover has never said, not in his tongue and not in the road-talk, and nobody has ever asked him. I can tell you what was read in the morning, because I read it. When the storm went off at first light my grandmother unbarred the door. The drover was sitting against the wall beside it, wrapped in nothing, with his hands in his armpits, alive, and his face was grey. There was nobody else. The blanket was gone and the pack was gone. In the snow at the foot of the wall there was a hollow, the size of two men sitting close, with the drift built up round it on the wind side where somebody had piled it with his hands. From the hollow there was one track, going down the valley toward the marches. It was a man's track and it was walking, not crawling. That is read. You can read a track.

My grandmother looked at the drover. She looked at his boots. She looked at his hands. She looked at the sky behind him. In that order.

Then she moved aside from the door and pointed him in to the fire, and he came in, and she put him by it and put the pot on, and gave him a hand of barley in his bowl, the same hand, and he ate it.

Nobody said anything to him. Not that morning and not after. He did not say anything either. He went down at the thaw. He came back up the next autumn with the last of the grain, and was paid for it in coin, and asked nothing, and the snow came, and he reached the door, and was fed. He has reached it every winter since, and he is fed, and you have seen him fed, and you have heard me let him be.

That is the telling. Now I will tell you the one thing in it that is mine.

After that winter I never said again that he was eating the grain we bought from him. I never counted his barley against mine again. I do not know what changed. I was not told.

My grandmother kept the rule. She did not open the door to the Roadless, and she did not say his name, and she never once spoke of that night to anybody, not to the hearth and not to me, except to tell me, years after, when I was a warden myself and had the key round my neck, the part I have told you about the cold shelf, so that I would know what had been at the door. Then she said, "Whoever reaches the door is fed," and nothing else. I have thought since that she was telling me she had kept it. I have thought since that she was telling me the drover had. Both are inferred. I say them as inferred.

The store lasted. It lasted to the thaw and two days past it, and then the drovers came up out of the marches. That much I saw. Nobody at this hearth stopped that winter, and that is something I say at the closing every year when I count the store, which is not the count of the dead, because the dead are on the road and the store is in the house, but it is a count. The house of Shomber is standing again, on the same ground, with a roof of new slate. The yak we killed was the grey one. My grandmother is in the count of this hearth's road, on the scree above the first water, where she stopped, an old woman going up to see the road one more time against the word of her own sister, who had told her no. That is another telling.

The drover is still coming up. When he comes this year, and he will, feed him. Feed him again.
