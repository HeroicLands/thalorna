On the first of the twelve nights the lamp in the hof's doorway is kept lit, and the boys sent to trim the wicks learn that everything else done that week is for the same reason. Behind them the hof is blazing, every sconce and brazier lit, and across the water the jarl's hall has opened its doors for the feast.

Jól is the twelve-night midwinter season around the solstice, when hofs keep their lights burning and halls open their feasts. The dead are invited to the table, and the living keep vigil against the long dark.

## The Twelve Nights

Every faith has a place in the season. [[lore-odvardty|Ódvar]] is prominent. [[lore-thrunvalddty|Thrúnvald]]'s priests bless the hearth, [[lore-solrundty|Sólrún]]'s priestesses conduct the rites for the coming year's loves and marriages, and the skalds sing the cycle of hero-sagas from the first night to the last.

## The Jól-Ride

The first thing a household tells a guest who arrives in the Jól nights, before the bench and before the cup, is to keep the lamp lit in the doorway, and the reason is the **Jól-Ride**. On the longest nights of the season, while the hall fires are kept up against the dark, the dead who were never given their grave-ale ride abroad: drowned sailors whose bodies the sea kept, the fallen nobody buried, kin who died with nobody to mourn them. A night of hard wind is a riding night, and a household that hears the wind rise at Jól does not need telling what it is.

The riders have a leader. [[affiliation-nahild|Náhild]]'s hidden clergy say the one at the head is one of their own Návargr, and claim to know which; the other hofs refuse to say anything about the rider at the head at all.

What the Ride seeks is a hall that has left no lamp in its doorway and no sheaf on its roof-peak, and anyone caught out of doors. Someone the Ride takes is found frozen at dawn, or comes back years later with no memory of the time between. So the custom is plain: a lamp in the door, a sheaf on the roof, the names of the household's dead spoken aloud at the Jól table, and nobody travels alone in the riding nights. The classic mistake, the one every child is warned of, is answering a voice that calls your name from outside.

Why the dead ride is disputed three ways. The hofs teach that the riders are the dead denied their _hrúmsminni_ (grave-ale), which is why the rite at the howe matters as much as it does; [[lore-nrdlndsfnrl|The Pyre, the Ship and the Howe]] sets out that rite. Old women at the hearth say the riders are the giants' last servants, still looking for the gate to the void their masters were shut behind. The skalds say the Ride is Ódvar's host, out counting who will stand at [[lore-aldarlok|Aldarlok]].

## The Ninth Night

A seeker may keep the howe-wake on the ninth night, sitting awake on a burial mound or at a crossroads, with a völva's customary leave. A private answer from the dead must still be supported by living evidence before anyone acts on it.
