**The Dinner of the Silent Passage** is a dialogue of the schools of [[affiliation-thyrenae|Thyrenae]], and the College of Ethics reads it with its students on the first night of [[lore-selmorosdty|Sélmoros]]'s Silent Passage, when the temples close to the living while the priests perform the rites for the year's dead. A student narrates it to a friend who was not there. The copies do not agree on the student's name, and the College does not choose among them.

The dinner is at the house of **Dókhis**, an old teacher of ethics, in the years after [[lore-heladharbors|the War of the Harbors]]. His guests are **Thálimos**, a ship-master of [[affiliation-pelagora|Pelagora]] bound to him as a friend in the old way, who fought as a marine in that war, and **Sphrâdîa**, a companion of the [[lore-heladsilverveil|Silver Veil]] who came to Thyrenae with him. The talk begins with the poets and their claim that glory is the second life of the dead, and Dókhis argues against it. He then tells the story of **Ghígnis**, a potter's son of the Thyrenaean levy who fell in the first battle of that war and was carried home by the herald among the dead, and who sat up on his bier when his mother said his name. Dókhis offers it as a likely story and not as a proof, and the dialogue ends on an objection that he concedes and does not answer.

Nothing in the cities' practice has the dead answer the living, and the dialogue's own speakers say so: the story is offered as an argument, and the College reads it as one.

## The Asking

You ask me what was said at the house of Dókhis on the second night of the Passage. I will tell you, for I was there, and I wrote it down the next morning before the temples opened, while I still had the words in the order they were said. You know how the city is on those nights. The temple doors are shut, and the priests are inside with the year's names, and nobody goes to the agora because there is nothing to argue about that the dead have not already settled. So the old men stay at home and the young men go to the old men, and that is how I came to be at Dókhis's table.

He had two guests besides me. One was Thálimos the Pelagoran, who brings oil into our harbor twice in a summer and has eaten at Dókhis's table every time for twenty years, for they are bound by salt before the gods, and it is a friendship that has outlasted a war between their cities. The other was the companion Sphrâdîa, of the Silver Veil, who had come up from the harbor with him. She said nothing for a long time and ate very little and listened, as they do.

It was Sphrâdîa who began it. When the cups had gone round twice she asked Dókhis what the priests do behind the shut doors.

"They say the names," said Dókhis. "Every citizen who died in the year, from the archon to the oldest beggar on the roll, by his name and his father's name and his clan. The priests say them and the city does not hear them, which is the point. It is the one thing in Thyrenae that is not done in public."

"In Pelagora they read them in the agora," said Thálimos. "From the speaker's stone, in front of everyone, and anyone may stand up and say that a name was left out."

"I know it," said Dókhis. "And in Pelagora a name has been left out on purpose, and a man stood up and said so, and the assembly voted on whether the dead man should be named, and voted no. I was there. I was your guest."

"He had sold grain to the legions," said Thálimos.

"Then the assembly voted on whether he was dead," said Dókhis, "and found that he was not. I do not think the assembly can do that. That is my whole quarrel with your city, and it is a small one."

Sphrâdîa asked what would become of a man whose name no one said.

## The Poets

"Ask the poets," said Dókhis. "They will tell you. A man whose name no one says is nothing, and a man whose name everyone says lives on. That is the whole of the poets' account of the dead. Ýthros burned his keel so that the heralds of seven cities would say his name at an altar, and the song says it as though it were the end of the matter. Glory is the second life. The name on the archon's list, the stone in the sanctuary, the speech the schoolboys learn. You have heard it."

"I have heard the Thyrenaean version," said Thálimos. "In ours, Ýthros is a man who burned his own ship to light the way for a better sailor who did not need it."

"I know your version. It does not matter which ship came first. What matters is what both songs believe, and they believe the same thing. They believe that a man lives after his death in the mouths of other men, and for as long as the mouths go on, and no longer. Do you believe it?"

"I believe the songs," said Thálimos. "I am a Pelagoran. We believe what we sing."

"Then answer me this. Suppose a man does a wrong, and does it in the dark, and no one knows. He poisons his brother for the terraces, and the doctor says it was the fever. He goes on to be strategos and wins a battle and endows a gymnasium with a good teacher, and when he dies the city puts up a stone and says his name every year in the agora. He lives on, by the poets' account. He lives on gloriously. And the brother, whose name was said once, at his rites, and never again, is nothing. Is that just?"

"No," said Thálimos. "But it is what happens."

"It is what happens among the living. I am asking about the dead. The poets say that what happens among the living is all that happens to the dead. If they are right, the man who hides his wrong well is better off than the man who never did one. And if that is so, then everything we say in this city about the wrong done in concealment is a pretty thing we say to children. We say that the hidden wrong is the worst wrong. We say the magistrate who leaves before the auditor has confessed. If the poets are right, he has not confessed anything. He has got away."

"The auditor catches him," said Thálimos.

"Sometimes. The auditor reads the accounts that were written. He does not read the ones that were not."

Then Sphrâdîa spoke, for the first time at any length. She said that the Silver Veil teaches its daughters that everything said at a dinner is remembered by someone, and that nothing done in a city is truly done in the dark, because there is always a slave in the room, or a child, or a woman behind the curtain, and the only question is whether that person will ever be asked.

"That is a hard comfort," said Dókhis. "It means that justice depends on whether the slave is asked. I want a better one. I want a world in which the brother who was poisoned is not nothing. And I cannot prove that there is one. So I will tell you a story."

"A poet's story?" said Thálimos.

"A likely story. I do not say it is true. I say that if it is not true, then something like it is, or else the poets are right and we are all fools for keeping the auditor. And I say that a man who lives as though it were true will live better than a man who lives as though the poets were. You may take it as an argument, which is what it is. It was told to me by a woman of this city whose brother it happened to, or so she said, and you may doubt that too."

## The Story of Ghígnis

"You remember the first battle of the war with your city, Thálimos. It was in the hills, in the first summer, where our levy and Kalydria's had marched on your hinterland, and your marines came up from the coast to meet them."

"I remember it," said Thálimos. "I was one of the marines."

"Then you will correct me where I am wrong. Our levy broke in the afternoon and went back over the ridge, and left its dead on the slope. It was the first time in eight and a half centuries that Helionite had killed Helionite in a pitched battle, and nobody on either side was sure what to do next. The next morning our herald walked out on the slope with his staff, alone, and asked for the dead, as the custom of the Games requires. And your strategos gave them up."

"He did not hesitate," said Thálimos. "I have heard it said that he did. He did not. He said: take them, and when it is our turn we will ask the same of you. And we carried them down to the herald's carts ourselves, because the herald had brought too few men. I carried four. I remember the fourth, because he was heavier than he should have been, and I thought he had a stone in his pack."

"He may have been Ghígnis," said Dókhis. "Ghígnis was a potter's son, and heavy in the shoulders, as potters' sons are. He had gone down with a spear in his side in the first rush, and lain on the slope all night with the dead, and in the morning he was cold and his eyes did not move, and the herald counted him with the others. They brought him home in the carts, two days on the road, and laid him in his father's courtyard, and his mother washed him and dressed him and the priests came to say the rites. And on the third day, when his mother stood at the head of the bier and said his name, as the rite has the mother say it, Ghígnis sat up.

"You can imagine what the courtyard did. I will not describe it. The priests went on with the rite, for they did not know what else to do, and his mother fainted, and his sister, who told me this, went for water. And when there was quiet again, Ghígnis asked for a drink, and then he asked for the priests to wait, and then he said: I was sent back to say what I saw. And he said it, and the priests heard it, and his sister heard it, and his sister told me many years after. This is what he said.

"He said that he had lain on the slope and felt the cold come up from the ground into him, and then he had stood up, and the slope was empty. The dead were there and he could see them, lying in their rows where they fell, and himself among them, but the living were gone, and the light was the grey light before dawn, and it did not change. And there was a road going down from the slope, and he went down it, because there was nowhere else to go.

"He said the road went down a long way, and that others were on it, going down, the dead of the battle, both cities, and some of them he knew. Nobody spoke. They went down until they came to a wall, a long wall of black stone, and a gate in it, and the gate was black, and there were vines on it, and the vines were made of the bones of hands, and they moved a little, as vines move in a wind, though there was no wind.

"And before the gate there was an open place paved with stone, and around it on three sides were steps, rising, as the steps rise around the agora of Pelagora where you hold your assembly, Thálimos, or around the theater at Kalydria. And on the steps were the dead. More dead than he could count, all the dead there had ever been, sitting in their cities, the way the citizens sit by their tribes in the assembly, and every city had its own steps. He saw ours, and he saw yours, and he saw Kalydria's and Korinthea's and the others, and beyond them steps of cities he did not know, and beyond those more steps, going up into the grey, so many that they were like the hillside of olives seen from the sea.

"And in the middle of the open place, before the gate, there was a stone. Not a high stone. A flat stone, a little raised, the size of the speaker's stone in the agora. And in the gate, in the shadow of it, there was someone standing. Ghígnis did not see his face. He said there was no face to see, or that there was a face and it would not let itself be looked at. He stood in the gate and said nothing. That is all Ghígnis said of him, and all he could say: that he said nothing, and that his silence was the loudest thing in the place.

"Then the dead from the road went one by one to the stone. And Ghígnis saw what they did there, and this is the heart of it, so listen.

"Each man went up on the stone and faced the steps, and spoke his life. All of it, aloud, from the beginning. Where he was born and to whom, and what he was taught, and what he did and did not do, every year of it, in front of all the dead of all the cities. And the dead of his own city listened, on their steps, as the assembly listens. And the Silent Judge listened in the gate, and said nothing.

"And while the man spoke, Ghígnis heard another voice. It came from behind him, from up the road, from the world, faint, the way a herald's voice comes across a valley. It was the voice of the priests of that man's city, saying his name in the rites. And when the man had spoken his whole life, and the voice from the world had said his name, he stepped down off the stone, and the dead of his city stood up on their steps and said: we heard. And he walked between them to the gate, and the Silent Judge stood aside, and he went through.

"That was how it went for most of them. Ghígnis watched a great many. A Pelagoran marine went up, one of yours, a young man, and spoke his life, and it was short, and there was a girl in it, and a debt at a tavern he had not paid, and he said so. And the voice came down the road with his name, and your city's dead stood up and said: we heard, and he went through. Ghígnis said that he seemed glad of it."

"What was his name?" asked Thálimos.

"Ghígnis did not know it. He did not know any of their names. That is part of the story. The names were said by the voice from the road, faintly, and by the man himself, and Ghígnis said that he forgot each one as soon as the man had gone through, as one forgets a dream. The names were not for him. They were for the man's own city."

## The Ones Who Could Not Speak

"But not all of them went through. Ghígnis saw a man go up on the stone, an old man, well-dressed, the kind of man who has a seat at the front of the theater. He began to speak his life, and he spoke well, as a man speaks who has spoken in public all his life. And he came to a certain year, and he stopped.

"His mouth was open. He was trying to speak. Ghígnis said that you could see him trying, the way you see a man trying to lift a stone that is too heavy, the cords standing in his neck. And no sound came. Whatever he had done in that year, he had done in the dark, and had never said, and now he could not say it. Not because he was forbidden. The Silent Judge forbade nothing. He could not say it because he had never said it, and a thing that has never been said aloud has no words yet, and the words have to be found, and he had spent his life not finding them.

"And he stood on the stone with his mouth open, and the voice from the road said his name, faintly, for his city was saying the rites for him like any other, and his city's dead sat on their steps and did not stand up, because they had not heard. They had not heard the whole of it. And the Silent Judge stood in the gate and did not move aside.

"Ghígnis asked one of the dead near him how long the old man would stand there, and the dead man said: until he says it. Some say it quickly. Some stand a long time. There are some, the dead man said, who have stood on that stone since before your city had walls. And Ghígnis looked again, and saw that it was not one stone. There were many, many flat stones in the open place, more than he had seen at first, and on many of them a man or a woman was standing with an open mouth, trying to lift the weight of what they had not said."

"That is the auditor's hold," said Thálimos.

"It is the auditor's hold," said Dókhis, "except that there is no auditor. The man holds himself. He may not leave the city until his accounts are cleared, and he is the only one who can clear them. And this is why I tell the story, Thálimos. The man who poisoned his brother stands on the stone, and the city says his name every year in the agora, and he cannot go through, because the city's saying his name is not enough. He has to say his life, and his life has the poison in it."

"And the brother?"

"The brother goes up on the stone and says his short life, and his city says his name once, and that is enough. Once is enough. The poets are wrong about that. It is not the number of times the name is said. It is that it is said by his own city in his own rites, and that he has said the rest himself."

"Then the rites matter," said Sphrâdîa, "and the glory does not."

"The rites matter. The glory is a pleasure for the living. I do not despise it. I would like a stone. But the stone does not walk you through the gate."

## The Plain

"There was more. Ghígnis said that after a while he looked past the steps, past the open place, to the side, where the wall of black stone ran on into the grey. And there was a plain there, a grey plain, with grey grass on it, and on the plain there were ranks of men standing.

"They were armed, and they stood in their ranks, facing one way, as men stand when they are drawn up and waiting. Two armies of them, he thought, from the look of their shields, though the shields were grey like everything else. They did not move. They were not on the steps and they were not at the stones. They stood on the plain, and they faced the gate, and waited.

"Ghígnis asked the dead man near him who they were, and the dead man did not know. So Ghígnis went to the edge of the open place, to the side, and there he found a herald. A herald of the old kind, with a staff, standing alone where the paving ends and the grey grass begins. Ghígnis said that there were heralds there, at the edges, a few, the heralds of the old wars of the cities, and that they stood where they could see both the steps and the plain, and that the dead let them be. And he asked the herald who the men on the plain were.

"The herald said: they are the dead of a field that no herald could cross. A levy and a legion, who died together in one afternoon, the levy of one of the seven cities. Their city has not said what it did there. It has not asked for them, and it has not said their names in any rite, and it has not said aloud what happened on that field. And so they cannot come to the stone, for the stone is for saying, and a man cannot say his life when the last day of it has not been said by anyone. They are waiting for an order. The herald said: they have been waiting a long time.

"And Ghígnis asked if the herald could not go out to them, since heralds cross every field. And the herald said: I have tried. I walk out on the grass, and the grass is long, and I walk all day, and at evening I am standing here again, where I started. No herald can cross that field, here or there. Only their own city can bring them in, by saying it."

Thálimos was quiet for a time. Then he said: "That is Korinthea."

"I did not say so," said Dókhis. "Ghígnis did not say so. The herald did not say so."

"It is an argument against Korinthea. You are telling me a story to say that Korinthea's assembly should vote the rite."

"It is an argument against every city that has a thing it has not said. Korinthea is only the one whose silence has a field. Does Pelagora have nothing it has not said? Does Thyrenae? We have spat on the pavement by your bronze copy since the day it was cast, Thálimos, and we have never once asked aloud in our own assembly what our own mages did in the war before your city treated. We say it was the warlords. We say they were not ours. Some of them were ours."

"Some of them were," said Thálimos. "That is true."

## The Return

"And then Ghígnis came to the stone himself. He had not meant to. He was in the line of the dead from the road, and the line moved, and he was at the front of it, and he went up on the flat stone and faced the steps, and he saw his city's dead on their steps, and among them, he said, his grandfather, whom he knew from the mask in the courtyard. And he opened his mouth to speak his life, and it was a short one, and he was not afraid of it.

"And the herald was beside him. The same herald, from the edge of the plain, or another, Ghígnis was not sure. And the herald put his staff across Ghígnis's chest, the way a herald's staff is put across the road to stop a cart. And he said: not yet. Your name is not said.

"And Ghígnis listened, and it was true. There was no voice coming down the road with his name. The priests of Thyrenae were saying the rites over him, but they had not come to the name yet, for in our rite the mother says the name last.

"The herald said: you have seen the steps and the stones and the plain. Go back up the road and say what you saw. Say it aloud, in your city, in front of witnesses. That is all that is asked of you, and then you may come down again, and I will be here, and the stone will be here. And Ghígnis turned and went up the road, against the dead coming down, and it was hard going, like walking up a stream. And he heard his mother's voice, faint, and then louder, saying his name. But she was not saying it as the rite says it. She was calling him, the way a mother calls a boy in from the terraces at dusk. And he sat up on the bier in the courtyard.

"That is the story. He said what he saw to the priests and to his sister, and to the courtyard, and to anyone who would listen, for the rest of his life, which was eleven years. He made pots. He married. He went with the levy again in the third summer, and came home. And when he died the second time, his sister said, he was quite calm about it, and asked them to say his name early in the rite and not to wait for their mother, who was dead by then, so that the herald would not have to stop him a second time."

## The Objection

Nobody said anything for a while. The lamp needed oil and I got up and gave it some, and when I sat down Thálimos was looking into his cup.

"I do not believe it happened," he said at last. "I believe I carried four men down the slope and one of them was heavy. But I have seen many dead, Dókhis, and none of them sat up, and none of them came back, and the cities have never had the dead answer the living. Your Ghígnis is a story."

"I said so," said Dókhis. "I said it was a likely story. Do you believe the poets' story better?"

"No. Yours is better. That is what I distrust about it. It is better than I would have made it."

Then Sphrâdîa set down her cup, and spoke, and what she said is the reason, I think, that I remember the night at all.

"I have a question about the steps," she said. "You said the dead sat on the steps by their cities, as the citizens sit in the assembly. And that a man goes up on the stone and speaks his life, and his city's dead stand up and say: we heard, and his city's priests say his name. And then he goes through."

"Yes."

"Where does a slave sit?"

Dókhis did not answer.

"A metic has no assembly," she said. "He paid his tax and stood the levy and died, and the city he lived in does not say his name in its rites, because he was not of it, and his own city is across the sea, and does not know he is dead. A freedman is barred from the assembly for life. Is he barred from the steps? A slave has no standing before the law except through his owner. When he goes up on the stone, who stands up and says we heard? Whose priests say his name? Ghígnis saw citizens. Did he see anyone else?"

"His sister said," said Dókhis slowly, "that he spoke of a door. A small door in the wall beside the great gate, and people going through it, people who were not on the steps. He did not see who they were, or what they said, or whether anyone heard them. He said that the Silent Judge looked at that door too."

"Then your story stops where your city stops," said Sphrâdîa. "It is very beautiful as far as the agora goes. And then there is a small door, and you do not know what is on the other side of it, and neither did Ghígnis. You have told us a story in which the dead are judged by an assembly, and the assembly is closed to half the people in this house tonight. To me, for a start. I am not a citizen of Thyrenae. I am not a citizen of anywhere that would sit me on a step."

Dókhis looked at her for a long time. Then he turned to me.

"Write that down," he said. "Exactly as she said it. It is the truest thing that has been said at this table tonight, and I have no answer to it, and I will not pretend to one. If the story is likely, it is likely in the way a citizen finds things likely, and that is a fault in it. Or it is a fault in the city that the story tells the truth about. I do not know which. Perhaps both."

"Perhaps the small door is the better door," said Thálimos.

"Perhaps," said Sphrâdîa. "Nobody has come back through it to say."

## The Morning

We talked until the lamp was out again, about other things, and then about this again, and nobody settled it. When the sky was grey Dókhis stood up and said that the temples would be open soon and that he meant to go and hear the names, though he had no dead that year, and that anyone who wished could come with him. Thálimos went with him, though it was not his city. Sphrâdîa went down to the harbor. I went home and wrote this, and I have written it as it was said, and I have not made Dókhis win.
