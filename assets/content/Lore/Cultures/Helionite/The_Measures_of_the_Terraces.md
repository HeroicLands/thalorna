**The Measures of the Terraces** is the poem of [Órasis]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a farmer of the interior highlands of [[place-helionis|Helionis]], and it is the oldest poem the schools read that has an author's name on it. It is in the long line of the rhapsodes, and in the highland speech that keeps older words for the olive terrace and the spring than the cities do; a Thirenaean schoolmaster teaching it stops at every second line to say what a word means. The highland villages recite it at the olive harvest, a verse to a basket, and the schools of the cities read it after the [Song of the Ships]{.name data-type="lore/literature"} as the second book a boy learns.

It is two poems joined at a seam. The first tells how the world came to be measured: before it was the [Unmeasured]{.name data-type="lore/cosmology"}, which had no length, no weight and no hour, and the Twelve of the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian pantheon]] held the first assembly in it, argued and voted with stones, and eleven stones were cast for measure. [[lore-karnavosdty|Karnavos]], the Wild Beast, threw his stone into the Unmeasured and it has not landed. The second poem is addressed to the poet's brother [Euthalos]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who took their father's terraces to law and lost them to the advocates, and it gives him the year of the terrace instead: the wall, the harvest, the press, the season when the sea may be sailed and the season when it may not.

The schools have argued with the first half since there have been schools. Its gods quarrel, bargain and cast stones like citizens, and the [College of Ethics]{.name data-type="affiliation"} at [[place-thyrenae2|Thirenae]] opens its first course by reading the assembly of the gods aloud and asking the students what is wrong with it. The second half no one argues with. The poem's account of the gods' assembly answers to the eastern rite's account of the gods' first terms, in which eleven gods seal the book of the world and the Wild Beast leaves his place empty, and each land holds the other's version a misreading of its own.
