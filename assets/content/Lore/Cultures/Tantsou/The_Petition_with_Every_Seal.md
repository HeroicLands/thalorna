**The Petition with Every Seal** is a hearth tale of the valley below [Tengmou]{.name data-type="place/settlement"}, told by the old women and now and then by the village [[lore-ancstrwrshp|Leigen]] on the night of the [Moon Festival]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} in Youjui, when the lanterns are lit along the terraces to shore up the boundary the long nights wear thin. It is the valley's favorite tale for that night, and it is told with one lantern set on the floor in the middle of the listeners, which nobody may let go out until the tale is finished. Children who know it say the last line with the teller.

Its hero is [Fourth Son of the Ditch-Keeper of the Tui Clan]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, near Tengmou, a [[lore-nouwen|Nouwen]] of a poor house, and it is set in a year of the [White Ape]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"}, the year animal under which an unconventional petition is likelier to succeed. The tale is a trickster tale, and the Tantsou laugh at it, and its trick is that the fourth son broke no rule at all. The tellers differ over how many of the ten bureaus they walk the petition through; the telling below is a Tengmou grandmother's, who gives five, as her own grandmother did.

: The Petition with Every Seal {#petition-seals}

Put the lantern down there, where all of you can see it. Not so near your knees. If it goes out before I finish, we start again from the beginning, and I am old and I will not enjoy it, and neither will you.

This happened in a White Ape year. It could not have happened in any other kind of year.

In the valley below Tengmou there was a house of the Tui Clan that kept the ditches. It was not a rich house. Its ancestors were good, careful people who had never had much to burn for each other, and so they had never been strong in heaven, and so the house had never been rich, and so it had never had much to burn. You know how that goes. Everybody in this room knows how that goes. The Ditch-Keeper had four sons, and the fourth son was the one this is about. A fourth son, you see. That tells you something already. Four belongs to nothing, and a fourth son has to make his own way into a sentence.

When the fourth son was grown, the Ditch-Keeper died, as people do, and the eldest son had a tablet carved for him and set on the shelf, and the household fed him at every dawn, rice when there was rice and millet when there was millet. For three years the father was fed and the father was quiet, which is what you want from a father. Then the ground shook in the Month of Festivals, the way it does, and the shelf jumped, and the Ditch-Keeper's tablet fell on the floor and cracked from top to bottom.

You know what that means. A tablet is an address. Crack it and the mail goes astray. The household went on setting the rice down in front of the cracked tablet, and burning the paper, and none of it reached him. And the father, wherever he was posted, sat at his little desk in heaven and nothing came to him, and he could not send anything down either, and the dreams stopped, and the rain went past their ditches to somebody else's.

The eldest son went to the temple in Tengmou and asked what was to be done. The temple clerk looked it up. A cracked tablet must be carved again, he said, and the new tablet must be registered to the same post, and that is a petition to the Archive of All Records countersigned by the Court of the Dead, with the household's seal, the clan's seal and the temple's seal on it, and the fee is three strings of cash.

Three strings of cash. You have seen one string. Imagine three.

The eldest son went home and counted and found he did not have three strings. The second son counted and did not have them. The third son counted and did not have them either. Then the fourth son counted, and he had one string, because he was a fourth son and had been saving it for something nobody knew about, and he said he would go.

Now the eldest son should have said no, because a fourth son with one string of cash is not a plan. But the dawn rice was going to waste and the rain was going past, and so he said, go on, then.

So the fourth son went down to the Tengmou market, where the scribes sit under their awnings by the temple gate with their brushes and their little pots of ink, and he went to the oldest of them, a scribe so old his brush had a groove worn in it where his finger went. And he said, how much to write a petition?

The old scribe said, which bureau?

The fourth son said, how much if I tell you the bureau, and how much if I do not?

The old scribe looked at him for a long time. He had been a scribe at that gate for fifty years and nobody had ever asked him that. He said, if you tell me the bureau, it is the fee for the petition and the fee for the address. If you do not tell me the bureau, it is only the fee for the petition, one string, because I am not writing an address.

Here, said the fourth son, and gave him the string.

And this is what he had written. Listen carefully, because it is the only part of this tale that matters, and the rest is only where the paper went.

He had the scribe write his household and his clan and his place, properly, in the right order. He had the scribe write that his father's tablet was cracked and his father's mail was going astray, and that the household asked for the new tablet to be entered at the old post, so that the father could be fed again. He had the scribe write the date, the year of the reign and the year of the Generation and the year of the White Ape, all correctly, because a fourth son is not a fool. And then at the top, where the bureau's name goes, he had the scribe write: _To whichever office this is not._

The old scribe wrote it. His brush did not shake. Then he put the brush down and said, do you know what you have done?

The fourth son said, I have filed a petition.

The old scribe said, you have. And he sealed it for him with the market seal, which is the seal you can buy for nothing, and the fourth son took it to the brazier at the Tengmou temple and burned it, and went home.

Now here is where the paper went.

It went up in the smoke, the way every paper goes, and it came out at the top of the smoke into the Bureau of Transformation, which keeps the fire and takes in everything that is burned. A clerk of the Fire Bureau caught it as it came through, with his long tongs, the way they catch everything, and he read the top of it. _To whichever office this is not._

Now, he could not keep it. It was not addressed to his office. And he could not throw it away, because a petition correctly filed and correctly dated must go somewhere, and he had no form for throwing a petition away. So he did what the procedure says to do with a paper that is not your office's. He stamped it with his seal and _not this office_ beside the seal, and he sent it on to the next office down the corridor. And as he stamped it, the edge of it was a little singed, because that is the Fire Bureau, and nothing leaves there quite as it came in.

One seal.

The next office was the Bureau of Night Counsel, which keeps the dreams. It was day in heaven, or what passes for day there, and the clerk of the dreams was asleep at his desk, because that is how the dreams get read. He read the petition in his sleep. He dreamed it, I should say. He dreamed that it was not addressed to him, which was true, and he stamped it in his sleep, _not this office_, and sent it on. His seal came out a little blurred, the way a thing does in a dream, but it was his seal.

Two seals.

The next office was the Bureau of Allocation, which keeps fortunes. Its clerk was a careful woman with a counting-frame on her desk and she did not stamp anything until she had looked at it from every side. She read the petition and saw that it was not hers. Then, because she was careful, she looked up the household of the Ditch-Keeper of the Tui Clan in her own books, to see whether it owed her bureau anything. It owed nothing. It was owed nothing either. It had never had enough to owe. She wrote that in the margin, _owes nothing, is owed nothing_, in a very small hand, and stamped it, _not this office_, and sent it on.

Three seals, and a note.

I will tell you two more. The rest go the same way. Your great-grandmother could tell all ten, and it took her until the lantern burned down, and she made us start again.

It came to the Court of the Dead. That is the office that keeps the death of every person, and its clerks wear white, and they do not laugh, and they do not look at you. A clerk of the Court of the Dead read the petition and saw it was not his. But it had a living man's name on it, the fourth son's, as the one who filed it, and the Court of the Dead keeps a file on every living man as well, waiting. So the clerk opened the fourth son's file and looked in it, and wrote one word, and closed it again. The word was _not yet_. Then he stamped the petition, _not this office_, and sent it on.

The fourth son never knew about that. I am telling you so that you know.

It came to the Bureau of Passage, which keeps the roads and the crossroads and every journey. A clerk of the Bureau of Passage read the petition and saw that it was not his, and he stamped it, _not this office_, and he sent it on, the way a road sends you on. And it went the way of things the Bureau of Passage sends, and that is to say it was lost for a year at a crossroads in heaven where four roads meet, which nobody had ever counted, because four belongs to no domain and nobody counts a crossing of four. A year it lay there. Then a clerk who was passing picked it up, because it had so many seals on it already that it looked important, and carried it to the next office.

So it went round. I will not take you through every door. The Bureau of Creation, where everything is made, made it a little thicker, because it had been sealed so often. The Bureau of Returns, where everything that rots is sent back, left a spot of green mould on the corner. The Bureau of Unraveling, which keeps the floods that renew the soil and is watched closer than any bureau in heaven, stamped it upside down, because that is how they stamp everything there and they are very exact about it. The Archive of All Records read it twice to be sure and wrote _we know_ in the margin. And every one of them stamped it _not this office_, and sent it on, because it was not their office, and they were all correct.

And when it had been round the whole of heaven, it came at last to the place where everything comes that does not belong to one bureau. You know where that is. It went up to the desk of the Jade Sovereign herself.

You have seen her in the temple paintings. She sits behind the biggest desk there is, with the papers stacked around her like a city wall, with the jade seal in one hand and the ledger in the other, and her face is not kind and not cruel, because she is working. Everything in heaven that nobody else can settle goes up to her, so her desk is never clear, and she does not look up from it. That is why you write to the right bureau, children. Because otherwise you are asking the Jade Sovereign to stop what she is doing, and you are a ditch-keeper's son.

A clerk brought her the fourth son's petition and laid it on the very top of the pile.

She looked at it. And it was not a paper like the others on her desk.

You know, because every temple clerk says so, that a small petition needs one seal and one bureau, and that a great one needs the seals of many. The great rites the Wanduo work, that turn a storm from a whole province, carry the seals of three bureaus, or five, and the countersignatures of their overseers, and that is why they are so hard to break. Now here was a paper from a ditch-keeper's house in the valley below Tengmou, written by a market scribe for one string of cash, and it carried the seal of every bureau in heaven but hers. Every one but hers. The Fire Bureau's, singed. The Night Counsel's, blurred. The Allocation's, with its note. The Court of the Dead's. The Bureau of Passage's, a year late. The Bureau of Unraveling's, upside down. All of them, each beside the words _not this office_.

There had never been such a paper on her desk. There was no working in the whole of heaven that carried so much authority. The rites that hold back the rivers carried less. You could have moved a mountain with that paper, if the paper had asked to move a mountain.

So the Jade Sovereign read what it asked.

It asked that one cracked tablet in a ditch-keeper's house be entered at its old post, so that a father could be fed.

And she did not smile, because she does not, and she did not frown either. She took her jade seal and she stamped it, once, at the bottom, under all the others, and she wrote _entered_, and she sent it down.

And that night the eldest son of the Tui house dreamed of his father for the first time in a year, and his father in the dream had his arms full of paper.

Because you must understand what had happened. For more than a year every bowl of rice the house had set before the cracked tablet, and every paper it had burned, had gone astray, and the Bureau of Passage had kept it all, the way it keeps everything that goes astray, in a great heap at a crossing, waiting for an address. When the Jade Sovereign's seal came down, the address was good again, and all of it was delivered on the same day. Every bowl of millet. Every coat of paper. Every string of paper cash, a year and more of it, all at once, into the father's little office at the lowest desk of the Bureau of Allocation.

He was the richest ancestor in the valley that morning. And he did what a good ancestor does when he has credit to spend. He walked down the corridor to the Minister of Storms' office, with the paper cash under his arm, and he argued.

That summer it rained on the ditches of the Tui house when it rained nowhere else in the valley. Not much. Enough. The rice stood up. The house had rice to burn for him the next year, and so he was strong again, and so the house was a little less poor, and so it had more to burn. You know how that goes, the other way. It is good when it goes that way.

That is not the end of the tale, though some people end it there. The ones who end it there have not been looking at the lantern.

When the old scribe in the Tengmou market heard that the Tui house had rain, he went up the valley to see the new tablet, because he was curious, and old men are allowed to be. And the eldest son showed it to him, and showed him the paper the Leigen had brought back from the father in a dream, which was a copy of the petition with every seal on it, because the father had kept the original, as any clerk would.

The old scribe counted the seals. He counted them twice. There are eleven bureaus in heaven, and hers is one, as every child at this lantern knows. He counted his own market seal at the top, which he knew very well, the ten that said _not this office_, and the Jade Sovereign's at the bottom, which makes twelve.

There were thirteen.

There was one seal on that paper that belonged to no bureau. It was not the Fire Bureau's or the Bureau of Unraveling's, or anybody's. Nobody in the valley could read it. The Tanchei from Tengmou came up and could not read it either, and went away again very quietly and did not file anything about it. It sat among the bureaus' seals as neat as you please, with the same words written beside it that were written beside all of them, _not this office_.

And the old women say, and my grandmother said, that a paper which goes everywhere in heaven that it is not addressed to must pass, sooner or later, through the one place in heaven that is no office at all. That is the crack between two bureaus where neither will sign. That is where the damp comes in. And something in that crack read the petition, the way everyone else had read it, and found it was not addressed there either, and was very correct about it, and stamped it, and sent it on.

Now. The Tui house is still in the valley below Tengmou. You can walk past it. The Ditch-Keeper's tablet is on the shelf, the new one, with no crack in it, and it is fed every dawn. And once in a long while, not often, the Leigen comes down from a dream and tells them that their father has been sent something addressed to nobody, and asks what he should do with it.

And they tell him: send it back.

That is why the temple clerk in Tengmou will not take your paper until you have told him the bureau, and why the scribes at the gate charge double in a White Ape year, and why nobody in this valley, not a child and not a Warlord, writes _not this office_ on anything.

Look at the lantern. It is still lit. Good. That is the end.
