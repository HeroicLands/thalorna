There are two kinds of Kaliharan and they are not much alike. The Kaliharan of the interior is heir to eighty centuries of continuous custodianship, has probably never met a foreigner, and does not expect to. The Kaliharan of the ports has met a great many, speaks three trade tongues, and has more in common with a factor in [[place-helionis|Helionis]] than with a custodian in an interior sanctuary. Both understand the division and neither resents it: it is, in the Kaliharan view, the cost of keeping an interface with the outside world without letting that world come any further in than it should.

## The Living Whole

Kaliharan spirituality is not a religion in any sense an Ankarian theologian would recognize. There are no gods, no deities who answer prayers or grant blessings or require sacrifice, no afterlife, and no reward or punishment beyond death. There is [[affiliation-tharivaan|Thári'vaan]]—the Living Whole.

All things are connected: the canopy and the reef, the volcanic soil and the monsoon, the village and the birds that nest in its gardens, and the health of any part affects every other part. To a Kaliharan this is neither metaphor nor doctrine but observed fact, confirmed by several thousand years of very careful attention to a single island ecosystem; the suggestion that it is a matter of faith does not translate.

What follows from it is a posture and not a creed. Kaliharans do not pray; they observe. They do not make offerings; they tend. The nearest thing to reverence is an abiding respect for the world as it is, with an obligation attached—to leave the balance no worse than it was found.

## The Balance Kept

**Good is the balance left no worse than it was found: a part of the whole watched, tended and handed on in the condition it was received. Evil is the part neglected or over-worked, and the balance broken by someone who would not look.** A Kaliharan does not weigh a deed by its intent. The test is the condition of the garden, grove or shore afterward, and the condition can be read off the part itself.

Success is a tended part in good order: a household that can recite an unbroken account of its own corner, a lineage that has held the same terrace or reach for thirty generations, a teacher whose pupils notice what she noticed. Failure is the part let go. A Kaliharan who takes no tending is regarded with the quiet concern an Ankarian community might extend to someone who refuses to wash or eat, and the concern is the first rung of a ladder whose last rung is to be Severed, spoken to by no one.

The Kaliharan value most attention, exactness and patience. They value least haste, the improvised answer to a question that the old way already answers, and the unsolicited explanation, which they treat as a presumption on the listener. Innovation is not forbidden, only weighty: it is undertaken when the old way has demonstrably failed and not before.

## The Man Admired

[[being-ilmnnlr|Ilumanei Naoliru]], the scribe of [[place-vashuran|Vashurán]], is the Kaliharan who keeps the record and keeps it for everyone. He was born in a poor street behind the quay, the son of a dock worker and a laundress, and his parents spent what they could to put him through a temple school, where he mastered five scripts. He was apprenticed at twelve to a master scribe, who tore up whole pages for a single faulty letter, and he rose to the [High Registry of Kalihara]{.name data-type="affiliation/governmental"} at the wardens' hall in [[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]], where he recorded the contracts, marriages and debts of the four ports. He left the Registry to work for himself, in a disused temple archive in Vashurán, where porters, boatmen and settled foreign families alike have their documents drawn up, witnessed and sealed. He teaches young people the craft in the evenings and refuses any post that would oblige him to favor the rich.

The Kaliharans admire in him the habit their whole practice rests on: a thing observed and set down exactly, for whoever needs it, in a hand that does not change. He does for a port what a household's memory does for a household. His cost is a stubbornness that others read as softness. Once he has decided what is fair, he will not move, and the great merchants think him presumptuous for treating a porter's contract with the care they claim for their own.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-olvlthlr|Olivael Ithaluori]], the astrologer of [[place-telvari|Telvari]], is the Kaliharan who watches the longest. She was born to a merchant family in Ilovanu and wept at a conjunction over the harbor when she was ten. At fifteen she ran away to the pilots' islet of Telvari, where she learned the practical astronomy of tides and navigation from the old keeper of its observatory. Foreign factors now have their voyages charted by her, and her name has crossed the water. She is close to finishing an atlas of the heavens for the two centuries to come, and she has seen the sky depart from the patterns she has trusted all her life.

The Kaliharans admire in her the discipline of looking and the honesty of saying so. She will not shade a reading to suit a patron. She is hard to know, seldom speaks first and answers a question with startling literalness, which is the Kaliharan manner taken to its limit. Her admiration is not a lesser form of Ilumanei's: he keeps the record that others can use, and she keeps the watch that finds out what the record must say. Her cost is what the looking shows. The sky is departing from its pattern, and a woman who will not shade a reading has no way to soften this one.

## What the Island Keeps

A Kaliharan can point to the things that hold the practice in place. **The sílhari account** is the record of its own corner that every Kaliharan begins to hold at six or seven and adds to for life, kept by memory and recited to the household. **The keepers' threads** (_kaan'sari'val_), the plaited palm-fiber cords a custodian receives at elevation, encode the line of teachers back to a founding custodian, and they are buried with their wearer. **The first records**, the carved slabs and inscribed plaques of the deepest sanctuary archives, bear a few of the Ithari's stone marks, each a sign that calls up, for a keeper who knows it, what the people were taught and the keepers hold in memory. **The held stones**, the bone-white pieces taken from works that have died, are kept at every sanctuary as samples of what becomes of the substance after a work ends.

Beyond all of these stand the works themselves. They range from the great installations of the highlands to the small tending-stones a village keeps, and they are not sacred in the sense of being set apart. They are tended like an orchard.

Ordinary Kaliharans do not write. The island's archive is what the people recite and remember, and they keep it by [[skill-vaarthari|Vaarthari]], a learned and prized discipline of deep memorization that is peculiar to them. A trained Kaliharan brings back the minute details of a very long oral history in a recall close to trance, and the discipline makes written works unnecessary. It is also secure: knowledge passes only by being taught, so nobody can steal it by taking a book or copying an inscription. The few stone marks the Ithari left are too few to make a script, and their meaning is open to the keepers alone, who seldom need to read them. They carry on the memorization themselves and closely guard the elaborate secrets of how the Ithari instruments work.

The skill has levels. At low mastery a Kaliharan can memorize some things and the details go muddy, and at very high mastery a memorizer holds vast amounts of detailed information. The masters teach it to others. The highest of them are the [Vaarnei]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the "holders of record", who are commissioned to record important documents and agreements much as notaries public are elsewhere. They attest to the details of complex agreements, and a master may hold the details of many hundreds of them, with laws, decrees and histories besides.

## Tending, Looking and Holding

An ordinary Kaliharan keeps the three observances. _[Sílhari]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}_ is the daily looking: a few minutes at dawn at the insects in a garden, an hour at dusk at a flock returning, one full day a season at a stretch of forest or shore. _[Vaan'ari]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}_ is the tending of the part that is one's own, whether it is a household garden, a grove, a reef or a terrace a lineage has worked for thirty generations. _[Kaan'vaar]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}_ is the holding of inherited practice by exact repetition, careful teaching and refusal of improvisation where it matters. Nobody prays and nobody makes an offering.

The [Ithakan]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"} carry the same observances to the works. A custodian, trained from childhood in ecology, seasonal cycles, agriculture and the care of the ancient structures, performs [The Dawn Tending]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} in every weather: the hour before sunrise, a walk around a structure's perimeter, a sequence of words of which half the meaning is lost, a hand laid on its surface in a fixed order.

Maintenance here means tending and procedure and nothing else, because the fabric of the works cannot be worked on. In eight thousand years there has never been anything for a custodian to mend, replace or shore up—only a sequence of actions performed at appointed times. Over eighty centuries the understanding of _why_ has drifted: procedures [[lore-theithari|the Ithari]] may have meant as engineering have acquired layers of ritual meaning, and the Ithakan now perform them as liturgy, with meticulous precision, in the settled conviction that the health of the island depends on it.

## The Answering

The works do not serve everyone alike. A terrace is watered and its neighbor is not; a household's stone warms for the grandmother and stays cold for the grandson. The pattern follows neither rank nor learning nor devotion, and the Kaliharans call being served the answering. They have always lived beside the unevenness, without asking for the mechanism, and they have kept their records of who was answered where and when.

The works are failing, and what the failing has begun to do is scramble the answering. A household that was answered for generations is not answered this year, and a stone that was silent for a lifetime warms for a child who never tended it. The sanctuaries record it and the Keeper of the Failing Works keeps the account. Nobody knows how to repair a work, because nobody has ever repaired one.

## The Turnings of the Year

The island's year has four turnings of the monsoon, and each is a quiet, widely observed rhythm and not a festival. [Dry-Turn]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} ends the wet season. Households clean their thresholds and recite the year's rainfall in the evenings, and children are given their first account to hold at six or seven. [Stillwater]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} is the mid-dry month, the occasion of [The Tenth-Year Walk]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} and the long survey days: custodians walk the full circuit of a region's works, and every Kaliharan spends the daylight in silent observation of a particular place, looking properly while the light is good. [Wet-Turn]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} is the return of the rains, and each Kaliharan spends the week after the first rain visiting the households whose tending borders their own, to compare observations and coordinate the season's work. The custodians visit households in rotation in the same week. [Deepwater]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} is the heart of the wet season and the most inward. Households keep to their own thresholds and spend the time reciting the household's accounts so that none drifts, teaching children the names of plants and reviewing the lineage's records. Years are counted in Tenth-Year Walks at each sanctuary, with the count since the departure kept at [[place-veshvaan|Veshvaan]].

Each is for something concrete. Dry-Turn is for beginnings, Stillwater for the looking, Wet-Turn for the neighbors and Deepwater for the record. Long spans are counted in Walks, a Walk being the decade between Tenth-Year Walks, so a Kaliharan dates a childhood or a long tending by the Walk it began in.

## Quiet Concern and the Severed

Reward is a larger part to tend and a name in the line. A child chosen between eight and twelve goes to a sanctuary as a kaan'sili, "one who watches." A kaan'vaar who has held a sanctuary's tending for decades becomes a kaan'thari, and the rarest, the silkan, coordinate the tending of the whole island. A lineage that has kept its reach for thirty generations is a lineage others come to for its memory. Its Elder sits in the councils.

Penalty runs the other way and is almost entirely quiet. The first rung is the concern shown to someone who takes no tending. A Bondservant is held in service to a lineage and answerable through its head. The Unkept are put outside the keeping of the Keepers of What Was Left, and in a society whose whole institutional life is custodial there is nothing else to belong to. The last rung is to be Severed: cut off from the lineages and from the structures' service, permitted to live, and spoken to by no one.

## The Keepers and the Councils

Kaliharan governance is among the oldest continuous political traditions in the world and among the hardest for a foreigner to describe, largely because Kaliharans have no interest in describing it. What can be pieced together is a system of layered councils and rotating responsibilities. It balances regional, familial and custodial authority, and it keeps power from settling in any one person or institution.

The Ithakan carry great influence and do not rule; their authority is custodial. The silkan convene the councils that decide when an inherited procedure may be adjusted against a failing structure. Temporal decisions rest with secular councils seated by lineage and by charge over the works: an Elder of a Lineage holds an old kindred's lands, memory and share of what the island knows, a Master of the Terraces wardens the agriculture and the weather-works it depends on, and a Warden of the Approaches keeps the sea-lanes, the pilots and the reception of anyone who arrives. The Speaker for the Island presides over the Council, which sits at Ilovanu, and is the only Kaliharan who may answer a foreign power.

Records are the household's and the lineage's, and they are recited and not written: the full name opens every account a Kaliharan holds and is spoken in the household's and the lineage's recitations. The ports run on a separate and frankly pragmatic structure, with a High Registry at the wardens' hall in Ilovanu for contracts between the foreign quarters and the port households, kept in the foreign scripts of the quays. Everyone involved treats this as a sensible division of labor and not a compromise. Where the two conflict, the custodians hold the procedure and the secular councils hold the decision, and the silkan convene the council that settles when a procedure may be adjusted.

## Patience as a Manner

The Kaliharan temperament is unhurried to a degree that foreign merchants find maddening and foreign diplomats find impenetrable. A people who measure in cycles, and who have been doing one thing for eighty centuries, do not experience a season's delay as delay.

A Kaliharan of the interior is courteous, watchful and extremely difficult to hurry into a commitment. He answers a direct question accurately and volunteers nothing, and he is not being evasive: he regards unsolicited explanation as a form of presumption, and assumes that whoever needed to know has already been told. Reticence about the interior is not policy handed down from a council—it is manners, held so consistently that it functions as policy.

## The Name the House Uses

You will hear a Kaliharan woman called _Koaluethei_ in the market and _Luethei_ in her own doorway, and the second name is not yours to use. A long Kalihari given name has a short **household name**, the end of the name from its stressed syllable on, formed by the same rule in every house on the island. Kin say it, and so does anyone raised under that roof. Nobody else does: the full name is the one her household and lineage recite, and the one every council and custodian uses. Use one you overheard and she does not correct you: she gives you her full name back, evenly, and from then on the conversation is very formal. A name of three syllables or fewer has no household name, and you may say it as freely as anyone. The [[skill-kalihrlng|Kalihari language]] note sets out how the short name is formed.

## The Ports

[[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]] and the smaller settlements at [[place-vashuran|Vashurán]], [[place-nalurithan|Nalurithan]] and [[place-telvari|Telvari]] are where the two worlds touch. Centuries of contact with Ankarian, [[lore-taruzelianclt|Taruzelian]] and [[lore-vedyariclt|Vedyaran]] merchants have left them genuinely cosmopolitan: mixed populations, mixed marriages, and well-kept temples of the faiths of the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]] serving foreign traders, long-settled immigrant families, and Kaliharans who have chosen the cosmopolitan zone over the interior.

A port Kaliharan is not a lesser Kaliharan and is not treated as one. He is understood to be doing necessary work at the boundary, and the interior's distance from him is philosophical and not contemptuous. He is also almost never admitted inland, and both he and the custodians know it, and it is not discussed.

## What the Whole Asks of an Ordinary Day

There is no gate, no judge and no hereafter. The Whole does not forget a part, and nothing is owed to a god. What follows is a way of spending a day: the looking at dawn, the tending of the part that is one's own, the exactness of a practice repeated as it was taught, and the day's looking recited at dusk. The test of a life is the condition in which the part is handed on, and it is observable by anyone, continuously, which the Kaliharans hold to be a stricter arrangement than judgment after death.

The belief reaches ethics, property and burial. A Kaliharan hands the tended part to a daughter or a niece in better order than it was received, because the part is a stretch of the Whole and not a possession. Inheritance runs in the line of the mother, and a household's wealth is the accounts it holds and the lineage's memory, with the part it tends. A custodian is buried with the keepers' thread. The dead are kept in the household's recitation the way the living are in their gardens. Money is spent on what the part needs: tools for the terrace, seed, the repair of a boat. A port Kaliharan who takes up Aureldian devotion keeps the Aureldian rites for his own death, and the interior treats that as a private matter.

## Reaching the Dead

The living cannot affect the dead, and the dead do not reach the living. There is nothing to ask for. The dead are in the Whole, as the living are, and the part they tended is tended by someone else. What a Kaliharan can do for a person who has died is keep their account in the household's recitation and keep their part in order, and the cost is the tending itself. Thári'vaan is not ancestor veneration and does not become one. In the ports a Kaliharan who follows one of the Aureldian faiths may have priests of the [[affiliation-selmoros|Faith of Sélmoros]] attend his dying, and the interior neither forbids nor shares it.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Kaliharan of the interior what a person owes, and he answers: the balance first, then the household, then whatever council has asked something of him this season.

He will not say he owes anything to a god, because there is none, and he will not say he owes anything to posterity, because posterity is not separate from the whole. What he means by the balance is that a person is accountable for the condition in which he hands the world on—and that this accounting is continuous, immediate, and visible in the part itself—a stricter arrangement, in his telling, than judgment after death.

In return the household owes a member a part to tend, a place in its recitation and the use of its name. The lineage owes its kin the feature it is named for and holds, and the memory of how to tend it. A teacher owes a pupil the exact sequence she was taught. The council owes the island the decision about what outsiders may be told. The Whole owes nothing.

## The Weak

Care is the household's, and the household is the mother's line. A woman's children, her sisters and their children belong to it, and a widow or an orphan has a place in the line she was born to. The first duty to anyone is a part to tend, and the Kaliharans give the weak a small one: a corner of the garden, the household's counts of birds. A Kaliharan who takes no tending is the one who needs help, and the household's quiet concern is the help.

The limit is the line. A Bondservant is held in service to a lineage and answerable through its head; the Severed are spoken to by no one. In scarcity the terraces feed the whole population without a trade in staple goods, and barter and lineage credit carry the rest. In a bad year the Master of the Terraces and the weather-works carry what the household cannot.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A custodian's duties are reduced as strength fails, and retirement is not recognized. The same holds for the household. An old person keeps the part they have tended and the looking, which needs no strength, and the house takes over the carrying and the digging. A person who cannot tend the whole of a part tends what they can reach. The looking needs no strength, and a person too weak to rise watches from the doorway and tells the household the day's page.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household's, and in the interior the line's. Teaching begins with the first account at six or seven and is carried by the household and, in the week after the first rain, by the Ithakan who visit in rotation. A child chosen at eight to twelve goes to an interior sanctuary for fifteen to twenty years of formation, living among the archives and the works. Defense is the island's approaches: the pilots, the Wardens of the Approaches, the currents the works are held to shape, and the ridgelines. [[affiliation-kalihara|Kalihara]] has never had an empire and never fought a war of conquest.

## The Line at Every Age

The unit is the household, a line that runs through the mother. Membership is the mother's lineage in the interior, and in the ports whichever house a person was raised in. A lineage is named for the feature it tends and holds, such as the still water (_Ithavanu_), the old vine (_Reiluvao_) or the bright canopy (_Theiluori_), and the name is a compound of two stems of the island's ground and weather. A child is given a first account to hold and a short household name, and learns the names of plants. A young person is assigned a part to tend, and some are chosen for the sanctuaries. An adult keeps the three observances and answers to whatever council has asked something of them. An old person holds the part and the memory of how to tend it, and a dying person is attended by the household with no rite.

## What the Tending Hides

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Kaliharans' is the exactness itself. A people who hold that the balance has lasted ten thousand years because the practice was never altered find the practice failing, and have only the practice to answer with. Thoughtful Kaliharans dispute when the old way has demonstrably failed, and whether the question is answered by the custodians who convene the councils or by the people who live beside the works that no longer answer.

The sin is the silence. The works are failing, and the silkan who carry the count of failures speak of it to no one outside their rank. A people whose first duty is to hand the world on in the condition they found it keep from their own households how far the world they were handed has gone. If the works that calm the storms fail, the harvest goes with them, and the household that is not answered on its terrace learns of it from its own account and not from its custodian. An office of recent and unadmitted creation, the Keeper of the Failing Works, holds the account. The habit of not explaining, which keeps the interior closed to the world, has reached the people it was meant to protect.
