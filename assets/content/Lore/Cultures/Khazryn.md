The desert is named for the Khazryn, and they will mention it early, because four of their seven cities are held by someone else and the name is one of the few things nobody has been able to take. They are an old, literate, urban people who lost. Ninety years ago the Ātárzád came into the oasis-belt as refugees, were taken in under the hospitality the desert extends to anyone in extremity, were settled on marginal land by princely patronage, and waited.

## Memory Is an Office

What followed the Khazryn call the Vēštákán—the Breaking—and they recite it at every festival, every formal court ceremonial, and every funeral of a senior member of a princely house. The recitation is not nostalgia and not grievance in the ordinary sense: it is a case being kept open, and the Khazryn will insist on the distinction. A feud seeks revenge; the Khazryn seek return.

The exile princes of Shirvan, Ashkarad, Zargandûr and Kethramír would, given the chance, walk back into their cities, take the palaces of their forebears, rebuild the temples that were pulled down, and resume governing—tolerating the Ātárzád settlers, they say, as a subject population with somewhat more dignity than the Khazryn have been shown. Whether that magnanimity would survive an actual reconquest is a question the exiles do not enjoy being asked.

A Khazryn household therefore keeps records the way other households keep silver: genealogies, deeds, temple registers, and the boundaries of fields nobody has farmed in three generations. The heir-in-exile of Shirvan has spent her life assembling the legal and historical case for recovery; it fills a small library and is updated continuously. Nobody expects to use it soon, and everybody expects it to be ready.

## Water, and the Mother of the Oasis

The Khazryn identify with the oasis so completely that their devotion runs through it. Zúráthrä, the Mother of the Oasis, receives the deepest popular love in Khazryn life: a shrine stands at the principal water-source of every oasis and every Khazryn quarter inside an Ātárzád city, every wedding involves her blessing, and every child is presented at her shrine within forty days of birth.

Formal devotion belongs to Āhúrdáén, and on this the Khazryn are immovable: no other god, the Sacred Flame included, may receive the weekly observance owed to the Keeper of Harmony. That is the whole theological quarrel with the conquerors, who elevated the Flame to exclusive worship, and the Khazryn answer that doing so breaks the order Āhúrdáén himself established. They keep the full pantheon, all twelve at their seasons, in the form their own priesthood systematized over centuries, and they regard the Ātárzád version as an amputation performed by people who did not understand the body.

The destruction of Zúráthrä's great shrine at Shirvan, and the sacrificial fires built over the holy spring, are the wounds a Khazryn names first when he stops being polite.

## The Mōbad

The Mōbadate is the institution that survived. A Mōbad studies fifteen to twenty years before ordination, and is expected to have mastered astronomy, mathematics, theology, law and at least three languages before he serves as something between a priest and a judge for his community.

Where there is no Khazryn court—which is to say in the four lost cities, where most Khazryn actually live—the Mōbad _is_ the government. He registers marriages, adjudicates inheritance, arbitrates commercial disputes, and keeps the community's records under a foreign magistracy that does not much care what he does so long as the taxes arrive. A people can lose its palaces and remain a people; it cannot lose its Mōbads, and the Khazryn know it, and a family sends its ablest child to the Mōbadate ahead of any trade.

## Being a Subject People

Most Khazryn are not in the free oases at all. They are craftsmen, merchants and clerks inside cities their grandparents ruled, and the habits that come with that are what most mark them out to a stranger.

A Khazryn is courteous, exact and reserved. He keeps two sets of records and two calendars—the ancient Khazryn year of twelve thirty-day months with a five-day festival at the spring equinox, and whatever the magistrate uses. He does not discuss politics with an Ātárzád and discusses little else with another Khazryn. He pays what is assessed, on time, and keeps the receipt, because a subject population with imperfect paperwork can be robbed legally.

He is also, quietly, connected. The exile houses fund shrines and schools in Amradad and the Haradian ports, and maintain a network of agents who keep the Council informed about conditions in the lost cities. A Khazryn merchant in Ashkarad may be nothing more than a merchant, or he may be the reason a prince in exile knows the grain price before the governor does.

## The Tongue and the Tablet

Khazri is written in a syllabary descended from an old mountain-script, and the Khazryn wrote it on baked clay for two thousand years before the Breaking. Clay does not burn well, which is the only reason anything survived the fall of Shirvan at all; what the fleeing households carried out is now scattered across the exile courts.

Literacy is expected of anyone above a laborer, and a Khazryn's handwriting is a matter of some vanity. An educated one reads Khazri and at least one trade tongue, and a Mōbad reads considerably more. Among the exile houses the ability to read the pre-Breaking hand is a quiet marker of seriousness, because the documents that matter are all written in it.

## Houses, and What a Name Carries

The seven princely houses are the spine of Khazryn identity, and every Khazryn knows which one his family stood to—not as a claim of descent from the prince, but as a statement of which court his people's records were kept in. A man from Kethramír says so, and another Khazryn hears _priest-house, theologically serious, took the worst of the killing of the clergy_ without a further word being spoken.

Marriage is arranged between households and is, where it can be, within the tradition. Marriage to an Ātárzád is possible, rare, and understood by both families as a decision about children and not about affection: the children will be raised in one tradition or the other, and everyone knows which way the pressure runs in a conquered city.

Hospitality remains close to absolute, and the Khazryn are bitterly aware of the irony. A stranger in extremity is fed, housed and asked nothing. They took the Ātárzád in under exactly that rule and have not amended it, because amending it would mean the Ātárzád had changed what the Khazryn are, and that is the one loss they refuse.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Khazryn what a man owes, and he answers in order: the household first, then the house his household stood to, then the Mōbad who keeps the community's law, then the shrine at the water. The magistrate is owed his tax and nothing else—not loyalty, not truth beyond what the ledger shows, and certainly not the location of anything that matters.

Above all of it he owes the record. A Khazryn who lets a genealogy lapse, or lets a child grow up unable to name the city his family came out of, has done the one thing the Breaking could not do by force.
