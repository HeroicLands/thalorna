The Jenchai keep the mainland's castes, script and calendar, and they have moved the center of all three. Where a [[lore-tantsouclt|Tantsou]] stands where the register files him, a Jenchai stands where his lord's word puts him, and his worth is the faith he keeps with that lord. Everything else about the islanders follows from that difference: the granted name, the stipend counted in bales, the hall where a man's seat shows his standing, and the ritual death by which a retainer says what the honorific grid does not let him say aloud.

## The Nine Above the Clouds

The islanders call [[place-jenchairgn|their land]] **Teyan Jenjai**, the Nine Mountains Above the Clouds of Heaven, and **Jenjai** in ordinary speech. The name states a ranking: the peaks stand above the clouds, and the people who live on them stand above every other people, the mainland first among them. The mainland's own name for the islands, **Chaiwong**, is a dismissal, and the islanders return it by counting the Tantsou among the barbarians of the west, the oldest and the most tiresome of them.

The claim beneath the pride is religious. The islanders hold that heaven withdrew the [[lore-mandatehvn|Mandate]] from the mainland at [[lore-jenchaiscs|the Secession]] and gave it to the Nine Mountains, and that the Emperor on [[place-zojai|Zojai]], the Dragon's Mountain, is its living presence. By that presence every terrace of the Nine is his. In practice the Emperor commands his household guard and nothing else, and eight **Daozhuo** (great lords) each rule a mountain with an army, a fleet, a granary and a law of their own. Outside the years when one lord forces the others to submit, each of the eight is a king in everything but the title, and the patent he holds from the Emperor is the only thing that says otherwise.

## What Is Good, and What Succeeds

**Good is faith kept: the word given upward to the lord who granted a name or holds a village, and the protection given downward to the people who serve him. Evil is faith broken, and at the far end of that road is the person who belongs to no lord at all.** The Tantsou fear the unclassified; the Jenchai fear the unsworn. A deed is weighed by whom it was done for and whether it kept the bond. A retainer who kills at his lord's order has done his lord's deed, and the account is the lord's. A retainer who kills his lord has done the gravest thing a person can do, and the law of every domain answers it with death for him and outcasting for his house.

Success is standing in the hall. A retainer household has succeeded when it holds a granted name, a stipend that rises over the generations, an office its sons inherit and a seat that moves nearer the lord. A lord has succeeded when he holds the patent of his mountain, a court dignity and a place near the front at the [Dawn Rite]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}. A village has succeeded when it pays its assessment as a body, year on year, and owes the granary nothing. Failure is the lapsed bond: a retainer whose lord has died or cast him out, whose name lapses with the bond, and who has no hall to sit in.

The Jenchai value most kept faith, composure under pressure, precedence observed to the inch, and beauty in a made thing—the counted verse, the formal hand, the blade, the terraced garden. They value least profit, which serves no lord; haste, which spoils both a verse and a campaign; the plain refusal, which forces a man to lose face before witnesses; and a long life bought with a broken word.

## The Man Admired

The admired man of the islands is a retainer of one name. His lord grants him his name at fifteen, before the hall, and he dies under it. Renaming is what makes this a boast: a man is renamed when he changes lords, takes vows or survives a defeat that ought to have killed him, so a retainer who keeps one name for fifty years has never left his lord, never retired from the field and never lived through a disgrace. He serves the same house under two or three successive lords, keeps the office his family holds, writes a fair hand and composes the short verse at need.

The islanders admire in him the bond held whole. When his lord is wrong he says so once, in the humble form the grid allows, and does as he is told. If the matter is grave enough and the lord does not hear him, he answers with the blade, and a death of that kind is read in the hall as a statement no one can withdraw. His cost is the cost of the bond. He carries out an order he has argued against, including an order to burn a village, and the islands' histories admire the composure of a man who does that in the afternoon and writes a verse in the evening as much as they admire anything.

## The Woman Admired

The admired woman of the islands is the keeper of the hall. Women of the retainer class receive granted names as their brothers do, and a retainer's wife holds the keys of the household: the storehouse, the accounts of the stipend, the shrine, the schooling of the children and the hall itself while the men are in the field. A lord's wife holds the same keys to the castle and its granary. When the castle is besieged she commands its defense, and the hall's histories record more than one siege held by a lord's widow.

The islanders admire in her the faith that holds what belongs to another and hands it back whole. Her highest act is to keep a dead husband's office or mountain until the heir comes of age, and then to give it up. Her admiration is no lesser form of the retainer's: he keeps the bond by obeying, and she keeps it by holding on when there is no one left to obey. The living case is [Hojujai Rewei]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, widow of [Hojujai Henwo]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the last Teng Ja, who has held the [[affiliation-hojujaidmn|Hojujai Domain]] on [[place-hojai|Hojai]] for her son [Hojujai Zija]{.name data-type="being/npc"} since 709 AF. Her cost is the one the islands attach to any woman who holds a mountain: she is admired while she is seen to hold it for another, and the omen-readers are already divided on whether Hojai's rumbling condemns a widow's regency.

## What the Hall Keeps

A Jenchai can point to the objects that hold a domain together, and most of them hang in a hall. **The patent of the mountain**, which the Emperor issues to each lord on succession, is kept in the lord's own hall and read aloud at his installation; a lord without one holds by force alone and can call on no other lord's duty. **The lord's roll** lists every village of the domain with its assessment in bales and whether its four parts go to the lord's granary, a retainer's fief, the court or the summit shrine. **The name-writ** is a granted name written in the lord's own hand and kept on the household shrine of the man who bears it; when a man is renamed, the old writ is burned.

**The house blade** of a lord or a retainer family is passed from holder to heir with the office, and the smiths' lineages that make them are named in the same breath as the houses they serve. **The mountain record** of each summit shrine holds every eruption, earthquake and spring of the mountain with the year and the lord in whose time it fell, and a lord's claim to heaven's favor rests on what it says. The court on Zojai keeps the mainland canon, the cosmological treatises and the formulae of the great rites, under the Keeper of the Sacred Texts. No domain keeps a census of the realm, and the Caste-Registrar at court registers nobody.

## The Day Kept

Ordinary religion is the mainland's household practice with the lord set into it. The shrine is swept and the brazier lit at dawn, food is set before the ancestor tablets, and spirit paper is burned for what the household owes or wants sent up. A retainer household keeps one tablet more than a mainland one: the tablet of the lord's line, set beside the family's own dead, because the household's service does not end when a lord dies. The mainland's dawn exercise survives in the retainers' halls as a drill with the blade, kept in the same courtyards at the same hour.

The rest of the day is service. The farmer works the terrace whose four parts in ten go to the granary; the smith works the forge whose blades go to the hall; the retainer attends his lord, keeps his office and trains. A farmer who wants something of heaven goes to the village shrine, and a lord who wants something of heaven goes up the mountain. The summit shrines are kept by the domain's **Tanchei**, and their readings of the mountain are taken as seriously as a mainland omen. An eruption is read as heaven's attention, so a household on a mountain that is rumbling sweeps its shrine twice and pays its arrears first.

## The Round of the Year

The islands keep the mainland calendar with two rites of their own at its two ends.

- [New Year Purification]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} (Shui Nie Wo) closes the year as it does on the mainland: households, shrines and halls are cleansed, and the year's spent spirit paper is burned.
- **The Dawn Rite** (_Leng Rui_) opens the year. The Emperor performs it at dawn on the first day of the year on Zojai's summit, and every lord's envoy attends. The seating order is the year's political map, and the lords' envoys negotiate it for months. Every household of the Nine lights a lamp at the same dawn, which is the one act the whole realm performs together.
- [Moon Festival]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} (You Jui) falls in late autumn as on the mainland, with lanterns lit against the unsorted and offerings to [[lore-youweidty|Youbui]], the Minister of the Moon.
- The [Festival of the Mountain]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} (_Chai Jui_) is each domain's own, held when the harvest is in and the assessment paid. The lord climbs his mountain to the summit shrine, the Tanchei read the mountain's year from the record, and the lord's roll is closed for the year. The day differs from mountain to mountain, and a lord who cannot climb sends his heir, which is how a domain learns who the heir is to be.
- [Festival of the Dead]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} , falls at midwinter and is the costliest event of the year, as on the mainland. Retainer households provision the dead of their lord's line as well as their own.

## Favor and Disgrace

Reward is favor, and favor is visible. A retainer is rewarded with a granted name, a stipend raised by so many bales, a village held as a fief, or a seat nearer the lord. A lord is rewarded by the Emperor with a court dignity or a better place at the Dawn Rite, which cost the court nothing and are prized above silver. A headman whose village has paid in full through a bad year may have his assessment eased the next. A commoner who does outstanding service in war may be taken into a lord's service and granted a name, which is the one door out of the commons.

Penalty runs from the hall's displeasure to death.

1. **The lowered seat.** A retainer at fault is moved down the hall, and everyone present reads the move.
2. **The stipend cut.** A fault in office costs bales, and a village in arrears owes the shortfall as a debt of the whole village.
3. **The lash.** Flogging is for commoners and soldiers; a retainer is never struck.
4. **Confinement.** A retainer may be shut in his own house for a season, with the gate closed and his office held by another.
5. **The burned writ.** A retainer stripped of his name loses his name-writ to the brazier, his stipend and his seat, and is no longer spoken to by his former name. He is not renamed, because no lord has taken him.
6. **Outcasting.** A man who has killed his lord, and a house that has harbored the deed, is entered among the **Nuitsou**.
7. [Ruising]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}. A retainer or a lord condemned to die may be ordered to take his own life, which keeps his house its name. The mainland's ordered suicide is the islands' word for every ritual death, ordered or chosen.
8. **Execution.** Death by the sword is for commoners and for outcasts. Few lords keep a prison, and confinement in one is rare.

## The Lord, the Shrine and the Throne

Within his mountain the lord holds civil authority whole. He appoints his own judges from among his retainers, and a judge hears the cases of the castle town and the cases between villages. A village settles its own quarrels through its headman and its elders, and only a quarrel with a retainer or a crime of blood goes up to the lord's judge. The records belong to retainer families that hold the office by inheritance and are named for it, as the [Besheju]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} keep the records of the salt. The assessment is collected through the headman, who answers for the whole village, into the lord's granary.

Religious authority belongs to the summit shrine and its Tanchei, and the two powers are allies by habit. The shrine's readings support the lord's claim to heaven's favor, and the lord provisions the shrine from its own villages. When the two conflict, the lord decides, but a bad reading in an eruption year can wound a lord's standing more than a lost battle. Above both stands the Emperor, the only claimant to the whole Mandate. An appeal can climb to him, but it reaches Zojai only over the roads of the lord who keeps its gate, and a ruling from the throne carries weight only when some lord is willing to enforce it.

## Service After Death

The Jenchai keep the mainland's ledger and its ancestor spirits, and they post the dead to their lord. In their teaching, a retainer who dies in faith with his lord takes his place among the dead of his lord's line, serving them in heaven as he served the living house, and his own descendants inherit his credit there. A retainer who dies faithless has no post in heaven, because no lord's line will receive him, and his household's dead lose standing with him. Ruising at a lord's death is the plainest form of this belief: the retainer who follows his lord is going on with his service.

That is why an ordinary day looks as it does. A retainer household keeps the tablet of its lord's line beside its own, and feeds both. A family polices its sons' faith more closely than its daughters' marriages, because a son's broken word debits every ancestor on the shrine. A retainer who dies in his lord's service leaves a household the lord goes on feeding, because the dead man is still in service, and an heir succeeds to the stipend only when the lord accepts him. Retainers are buried on the slopes of their lord's mountain, below the tombs of his house. A household spends what it can on the lord's festivals, the shrine's offerings and a good tablet, and a man who must choose between rice for his children and offerings for his lord's dead knows which one the hall expects.

A person lives accordingly. He keeps faith because his dead depend on it. He chooses his death with care, because the manner of it is entered in heaven and read in the hall, and a death that says nothing is a wasted entry. He does not outlive a lord's disgrace without saying something, and the something is often the blade.

## Reaching the Dead

The living and the dead owe one another as on the mainland: offerings, a kept shrine and the keeping of death-days on one side, advocacy in heaven, warnings through dreams and counsel on great decisions on the other. The channel is the **Leigen**, the spirit master, a Nouwen born with the gift of speaking to the dead, who carries a household's business to its ancestors and brings back the answer for the price of the offering the dead demand. Lords consult the Leigen in private, as mainland lords do.

The islands add the mountain. A lord's dead are taken to sit in the mountain with the house, and the summit shrine's readings of the mountain are taken as their word. An eruption is the mountain speaking; a quiet year is the mountain content. So the lord's dead are consulted through the Tanchei of his shrine, the costliest channel in the domain, and the reading arrives in a form that the whole domain hears at once.

## Speech, the Blade and the Verse

The Jenchai manner is formal, indirect and graded to the inch. Every sentence of [[skill-jurthatlng|Jurthati]] places speaker, listener and subject on the honorific grid, so precedence is settled by the first verb and governs everything after it. A refusal is an expression of the difficulty of the thing. A retainer may press a lord no further than "Does my lord answer?", and a lord who does not answer has answered.

Ruising is the end of that grid. A retainer cannot contradict his lord before witnesses in any form the grammar provides, but he can die, and a death in the proper form is a statement the lord must answer in the hall. The proverb for it is _Ja ku wa_, "The blade answers." A Ruising can protest an order, keep a secret, follow a fallen lord, or say that a man's honor outweighs his life, and the hall reads which of these it is from the manner, the place and the verse left behind. A lord who orders a Ruising is judged by it as closely as the man who performs it.

Refinement and brutality sit in the same person. A retainer is schooled in the formal hand, the counted verse of nine and eighteen beats, the garden and the blade as one curriculum, and he carries out his lord's raids on the mainland coast and his lord's reprisals on the villages. The islanders see no tension in it. Both are service, and both are judged by whether they were done well.

## What a Person Owes

A Jenchai's obligations bind him in a fixed order: the lord who granted his name or holds his village comes first, then the house and its dead, then the mountain and its shrine, then the Emperor whose presence makes the land sacred. The Emperor comes last, and what reaches him depends on the lord.

In return he is owed what the bond gives. A retainer is owed his stipend or his fief, his lord's protection, his lord's justice, a seat in the hall and a place among the lord's dead. A village is owed its six parts in ten, its headman's voice before the lord's judge and the lord's defense when raiders come. A lord is owed service and owes his retainers their rice and his word. The Emperor is owed reverence, and he owes the realm the patents, the dignities and the Dawn Rite, which are all he has to give.

## The Weak

The islanders divide the weak by whether they stand inside a bond. Inside it, care is a duty. A village pays its assessment as one body, so its widows, orphans and old belong to the whole village, and the headman who lets one starve has let the village's share fall short. A retainer's widow keeps his stipend until the heir succeeds; a retainer's orphan is taken into the lord's hall as a page and raised in the lord's service. Outside a bond, care is a kindness, and the mainland's teaching that alms are credit in heaven survives to make it one.

The man with no lord is the hardest case. A retainer whose lord has died without an heir, or whose house has been dispossessed, has no stipend, no seat and no name that anyone is bound to use. He may find another lord and be renamed, he may take vows and be renamed, or he may sell his blade to whoever pays. The last road leads to the raiding fleets and the mainland's mercenary trade, and the islands hold the men on it in contempt and hire them all the same. Below every bond are the Nuitsou, fed as property and owed nothing.

## Those Who Cannot Serve

A retainer wounded in his lord's service keeps his name and his stipend, because the wound is part of the service. He is set to the work he can still do, and most such men end as teachers of arms and letters in the lord's hall. A commoner who cannot work stays on his village's share, and his neighbors carry his days of labor on the castle and the roads.

Age has its own door. A lord or a retainer who can no longer serve well retires while he lives, takes vows and is renamed, and his heir succeeds at once. Retirement settles a succession while the old man can still enforce it, and it is the ordinary end of a long life at the top of a house. The Retired Emperor in [[place-lengwou|Lengwou]] is the realm's best-known example, and his household is one the court cannot afford.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the lord's. Every village sits on land entered in the lord's roll, and a village that pays its assessment cannot be moved off it. A retainer lives in the castle town in a house the lord provides, or on the fief he holds. When a family fails, the village takes in its commoners and the lord's hall takes in its retainers' children.

Teaching runs through the household and the hall. A craft is taught in its workshop from master to son and apprentice, and the smiths' lineages teach theirs in the forge-yards, one of which an [[being-embrhnd|Emberhound]] guards. Retainer children are schooled in the hall, the boys in arms and letters, the girls in letters, accounts and the keeping of a household. At ten every child of the Nine is tested for the gift, as on the mainland. A child who shows it is claimed by the lord and raised a retainer, with a stipend and in time a granted name, and the family may see the child; on the islands a [[lore-wanduo|Wanduo]] is a retainer like any other, and is not property.

Defense is layered. The **Tsaihuo** soldiers are raised from the castle town and the villages at a fixed rate on the assessment; the retainers command them and fill the lord's ships. Each lord keeps his own fleet. When a mountain erupts and a [[being-pyrclsm|Pyroclasm]] walks out of it, the lord's retainers clear the villages in its path and his sorcerers and shrine priests try to turn it toward the sea.

## The House at Every Age

The unit is the house: a lord's house, a retainer family or a village household, with one shrine, one set of dead and one head. Membership is decided by the head and, for a retainer family, confirmed by the lord, so a house that lacks a fit heir adopts one, often a son-in-law, who takes the house name and its office with it. Marriages among the retainer class are arranged between houses and need the lord's consent, and a marriage between the retainer families of two lords is a treaty between them. The commons live together and raise children within their caste, presented to the dead of both families by the village Leigen as on the mainland.

A child is entered on the household's shrine at birth and is known by house and position until it is named, if it ever is. At ten it is tested for the gift. At fifteen a retainer's son or daughter comes of age before the hall and receives a name from the lord; a commoner never does. An heir who succeeds to a mountain as a child is named at his installation, from the Emperor's patent. The young adult serves: the retainer as an attendant in the hall, the commoner in the village's share of labor and, when the lord calls, the levy. An adult keeps the shrine, the office or the terrace, and the house's faith with its lord. The old retire, take vows and a new name, and become the house's living memory, closest to the dead the shrine serves. A dying retainer composes his last verse before witnesses, and the funeral enters him among his lord's dead.

## What Faith Cannot Admit

The blind spot of the islands is the bond itself. Faith is owed to the lord, and nothing in the culture says what a retainer owes when the lord is wrong. Thoughtful Jenchai disagree about it. One school holds that faith is owed to the lord whatever he commands, and that a retainer's only answer to a wrong order is the blade. The other holds that faith is owed to the house and the mountain, and that a retainer may set his lord aside to save them. The first school costs the islands their best men at the moments they are most needed, and the second has started every war between the houses.

The sin is the [War of the Empty Seat]{.name data-type="lore/history"}. When Hojujai Henwo, the last Teng Ja, died in 709 AF, his heir was three years old. The houses of the Nine preach faith to a lord's heir as the first of virtues, and the [[affiliation-shuiwojaidmn|Shuiwojai Domain]] and the [[affiliation-wojaidmn|Wojai Domain]] pressed the court not to confer the office on a regency, so that no house should hold it and the balance among them should stand. The war that followed ran until 712 AF. The peace was bought with the throne: the Emperor abdicated in favor of his eleven-year-old son, who has conferred the office on nobody since. The islands hold that the mainland lost the Mandate because its line of emperors was broken. Their own line stands unbroken because the lords made a Son of Heaven step down to pay for their quarrel, and they keep the Dawn Rite each year with the Retired Emperor watching from [[place-lengwou|Lengwou]].
