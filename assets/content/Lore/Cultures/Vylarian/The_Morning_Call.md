**The Morning Call** is the third satire in the book of **Nizelar**, a client-poet of [[place-orzara|Orzara]] who lived in the Warrens and attended a patron on the Heights, and who says so in every poem he wrote. He was a veteran's son, and so a citizen only until he was grown, and the satires never let a listener forget that his right to sleep inside the line was his patron's to sign each year. Satire is the one form of verse the Vylarian schools call their own and not a borrowing, and it is in a lighter line than the epic, quick and broken, with the speech of the street let into it. Nizelar's book is read aloud after dinner in the houses of [[place-selvin|the Laurel]] and the Coronal by the people it describes, and they laugh in the right places. The houses it names by their streets have argued for a long time about which of them it means, and each house holds that it means another.

The satire takes a client's day from the dark before the morning call to the evening. Its refrain is a line of the Alessa street speech, with the worn-down _a'_ the schoolmasters correct: _a' thelmos lokator; a' daimela falvator_, "the client's come; the patron's out." The steward at the door says the same thing in High Vylari, with one vowel changed, and the poem turns on the vowel: the steward gives the patron's absence in the subjunctive, as a thing alleged, which is the steward's way of saying that the patron is upstairs. The rendering marks the High Vylari with "so it is said."

```poetry {form=satire lang=en}
Up before the cocks, before the bakers, before the gods are dressed,
up, Nizelar, in the dark, in the room at the top of the Warrens,
ninety stairs above the street, and every one a different height,
because the builder was paid by the stair and was not paid for the measure.
Blow on the coals in the pot. That is the kept fire of my house.
Bow to it. Bow to the shelf where the household gods would stand
if the household had any gods, or a shelf. Put on the gown.
The white gown, my father's gown, a veteran's, who served his twenty years
and went in at the one door, and the door shut behind him on his son,
at sixteen, as the law shuts it, and left the son the gown.
It was white when the city gave it to my father,
and it is white now in the way a winter sky in Moktur is white.
Down the ninety stairs, mind the ninth, it is missing, mind the fortieth,
it is there but it is a rumor. Out. The street. Orzara. Morning.

The client's come. The patron's out.

Up from the River Hill to the Laurel, every street a stair,
past the fountain that has not thrown water since the last Orzas but one,
which the ward-magistrate's clerk has entered in the roll as resting,
past the colonnade whose columns are resting, and the arch,
which is resting very hard on two of its legs and considering the third,
past the porters, who are not resting, and who curse me in Hilen speech
for a man in a white gown who walks where they need to put a sack.
Here is the Laurel. Here are the houses. Here is the garden wall,
and the door, and the bronze on the door, and in front of the door,
in the grey, in their gowns, in a line, the clients. Here I am. Place me.

For the steward places us. The steward is the one true magistrate.
He stands at the door with his staff and his list and his memory,
and he knows who is whose, and whose that one is, and where it stops,
and he sets the line in order down the steps, as the Senate is set,
the first client at the top step and the last at the bottom,
and between them all the empire, step by step, measured to the hair.
Twelve years ago I stood on the fourth step. That was a good year.
Then the third son of the senator from Alessar came, and I was on the fifth.
Then a freedman of the house, whose master had died and left him a vineyard
and a seal and an opinion of himself, and I was on the sixth.
Then a dealer in Taruzelian silk, whose patron's patron sits in the Senate,
and I was on the seventh, and he looked at me, and I looked at the silk.
This year a boy of fourteen who writes verses. His verses are bad.
His uncle is the patron's physician. I am on the eighth step.
Next year the ninth. The year after, the street, and then the Clearance.

Halfway down, a stranger, a provincial, nobody knows whose he is.
He will not say, because in his province nobody asks. The steward asks.
The stranger smiles. The steward asks again, in the formal voice,
the voice for a man of standing, in case he is one. The stranger smiles.
Unplaced! The steward cannot put him on a step. The line is troubled,
as a flock is troubled when a dog it does not know comes into the field,
not because the dog bites, but because the shepherd has no word for it.
They put him at the bottom, below the boy, below me, below the silk,
and every man on every step is happier for the morning, and so am I.

Now the bronze door opens a hand's width. Now the steward bends his ear.
Now the steward straightens, and he speaks to us, in High Vylari,
the speech of the courts, the speech of the temples, with every ending on it:
the patron is away, so it is said, because, so it is said, he is away.
One vowel. One little vowel moved from the place where it lives.
The steward is a grammarian. The steward means: the patron is upstairs,
and he has heard you, and he does not choose this morning to be seen.
And every man on every step bows to the door, from the top to the bottom,
in order, as the line is set, and the provincial does not bow, being lost,
and we go down the steps in order, the first first and the last last,
and the provincial goes first, being at the bottom, and does not know why we laugh.

The client's come. The patron's out.

What did I get for the morning? I got my place. I got the eighth step.
I got the steward's nod, which is a coin worth about a wedge of a wedge.
I got the right to say at the baths: I was at the door this morning,
and the right to be told: so was I, at a better door, on a higher step.
I got the waiver. That is the real coin, and the steward pays it once a year:
a paper with the patron's seal that says the man on the eighth step
may sleep inside the line while he is the patron's, and not an hour after.
The dole is a citizen's. My father had it, on the first of the month,
short and late, and the clerk at the granary told him the measure was the measure,
and the measure was a man's two hands put together, with the thumbs out.
I would take it short. I would take it late. I would take it in one hand.

The patron dines. I am asked to dine. That was the year of the fourth step.
I lay on the lowest couch at the end where the draught comes in,
and the wine came round, and the wine was red from the Hilen hills,
and it came round to me, and when it reached me it was pink.
The patron spoke of the empire and its frontiers. The patron spoke of the dole,
and said it was the ruin of the treasury, and the man beside me agreed,
and the man beside me was eating a quail that cost my month.
The patron spoke of the virtue of our fathers. Let me speak of the fathers.

Two doors down the Laurel there is a new house, raised last spring,
an elevated house, a house the sovereign raised for a gift of ships,
and the cupboard of masks by its door was empty when they raised it,
since a house that is new has no faces, and a house with no faces is not a house.
They had it filled in a month from a sculptor's yard in the Gilded Quarter.
The faces are very fine. The faces are wax, and the wax is good wax.
The faces are consuls and generals and a priestess and a judge
of the courts of the Republic, with his seal in his hand, and a founder
who walked behind a cart, I think, or behind an army, the sculptor was not sure.
They are all very grave. Not one of them has seen the house they belong to.
On the night of the Silent Passage the steward of the new house
read out the names of the faces in the window, in order, without a stumble,
and I stood in the street and heard them, and I knew three of them
from the sculptor's yard, where they had been somebody else's the year before.
Forging a seal is death. Forging a name is unnaming. Forging a face
is a gift of ships, and the new house sits on the fourth step of the Senate.

The client's come. The patron's out.

Home, then. Down from the Laurel at evening, by the long way,
the avenue, the Orzas's Circle, the great one, and in the Circle
the Founder, the colossus, standing over the triumphs and the accessions,
with the crowned mark cut on the plinth where the rule of the old letters says
a god's name goes, or a founder's, and inside the mark the stone smoothed blank.
I stopped. I am a client. A client stops in front of anything that will not see him.
Here, I said to him, is a man placed lower than the eighth step.
Here is a man the steward could not find on any list. Unplaced!
And they set you at the top of the city, and the triumphs go round you,
and the boys in the schools learn the poem about you before any other,
and the city says you every morning, without the name, without the step.
He did not answer. He is in the hallowed endings, and he is busy.
I went down to the River Hill in the dark, and up the ninety stairs,
and blew on the coals, and they took, and I bowed to them, being my house,
and folded the white gown, which is white the way the sky over Moktur is white,
and lay down, and set my mind to wake before the cocks, before the bakers,
because the step is the eighth step, and the eighth step is mine,
and a client who misses a morning is placed outside the line, on the Clearance,
with the lean-tos and the dogs, and I will be on the step.

The client's come. The patron's out. The client will come in the morning.
```
