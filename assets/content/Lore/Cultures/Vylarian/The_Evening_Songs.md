The Evening Songs are five elegies, _hilthersorime_, that [Thulemar]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the Zoralos of a watchtower on the [[place-moktur|Moktur]] frontier, wrote on the backs of his tower's smoke tallies. A Zoralos is a century's watch officer, and on the northern towers the watch officer keeps the tally of the smoke counted from the wall at dusk, a board of wax for each evening, sent down to [[place-dolvar|Dolvar]] with the dispatch when it is full. Thulemar wrote on the backs of the boards. The dispatch rider carried them to Dolvar and a carter carried them south, and they reached the woman they were written to in the [Warrens]{.name data-type="place/site"} of [[place-orzara|Orzara]], and the Warrens copied them. They are sung there by soldiers' wives to a tune of the street, and the schools do not teach them.

An elegy in [[skill-vylarilng|Vylari]] is a song of the evening, sung for what is lost or kept from the singer, and its line is a pair: a long line and a shorter one after it that falls short of the first, as the voice falls at the end of a day. The rendering keeps the pair. The woman is [Yalzeme]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a veteran's daughter in her father's hand, and the songs call her by her calling name, Yalzizon, the nursery name cut from her given name, which belongs to her family and her husband and nobody else. A man who uses it has no right to it, and the songs know that. The fifth song calls her Yalzeme.

Thulemar was in his sixteenth year of the twenty when he wrote the first song. He was a resident, as every commoner soldier is until his twenty years are served, and her father, who was a citizen by his own twenty, would not hand his daughter into the household of a man who was not.

: The First Evening {#first}

```poetry {form=elegy lang=en}
Dusk on the wall, Yalzizon, and I am counting the smoke of the forest,
  the columns that stand up out of the trees as the light goes.
One is a hearth. Three is a camp. Ten is a war-band over the passes.
  Tonight there are four, and I have written four on the wax.
Four is a village, or four is a lie. The sergeant says count it again.
  I count it again. There are four, and the dark comes up the valley.
I count the years as I count the smoke, by the evening, and write them.
  Sixteen are on the wax. Four are still standing in the trees.
One is a hearth, Yalzizon. I know whose. It is your father's,
  up ninety stairs, where you feed the coals in the morning and bow.
Three is a camp, and the camp is the wall, and I am the watch in it.
  Ten is the war-band. Ten is the years I have counted already.
Four is a village, or four is a lie, or four is the four I have left.
  I have written four on the wax. The Kamezos is lighting the lamp.
I have no right to the name I call you. It is your mother's,
  and your father's, and will be your husband's, and I am none.
Let the wax say it. The wax goes south in a bag with the count of the smoke,
  and the clerk at Dolvar reads the front and does not turn it over.
```

: The Second Evening {#second}

```poetry {form=elegy lang=en}
We do not look at the marsh. It is the first thing a recruit is taught here,
  before the count, before the horn: we look at the forest, not the marsh.
The marsh is grey at the foot of the valley, and nothing comes out of it,
  and the old men of the tower have a reason, and do not say it.
Tonight, Yalzizon, I looked. I am a Zoralos, I have the watch,
  and nobody saw me look, and I am telling only the back of the wax.
There are lights in the reeds. There is no wind, and the reeds are moving.
  There is a sound like men who have run a long way, breathing.
I thought of your lamp. I thought of your lamp in the window up ninety stairs,
  which you light at dusk so your father can see the stair.
I thought: those are lamps of men nobody lit a lamp for,
  standing in water up to the waist, looking for a stair.
I turned my back on the marsh and I counted the smoke of the forest.
  Two columns. Two is nothing. I wrote two, and I wrote you this.
Light the lamp, Yalzizon. Light it for me, at the hour of the count.
  I will not see it. I will count it. That is what the wall is for.
```

: The Third Evening {#third}

```poetry {form=elegy lang=en}
The centurion said it again to the recruits this morning, as he says it,
  the speech they say comes down from the old law, which he does not know:
twenty years under the eagle, and then there is one door, and you are a citizen,
  one door, and the army holds it open. I stood at the back and said it with him.
One door. I have been walking toward it sixteen years and some months.
  It is a long road, and the door is at the end of it, and you are behind it.
Your father went through it. He was a citizen at thirty-five,
  and took your mother into his hand, and took a room, and took the dole.
He says to you: I will not hand you to a man the law has not made.
  He is right. That is the law. I would say it, standing where he stands.
I am not angry with your father. Tell him. I am a soldier.
  I know what it is to keep a line you did not draw.
Four years, Yalzizon. Four years on the wax, and then the road south,
  and the door, and the clerk, and the roll, and my name on it at last,
and the stairs, ninety, I have counted them in the dark from your letters,
  and the door at the top, and your father, and the coals, and you.
Keep the coals in. Keep the lamp. Let the old man keep the door.
  The count tonight was one. One is a hearth. I wrote one.
```

: The Fourth Evening {#fourth}

```poetry {form=elegy lang=en}
Your father wrote. He wrote in his own hand, which is a soldier's hand,
  square, and the wax pressed hard, as a man presses who is not used to it.
He wrote that a man with the dole and a room came up the stairs in the spring,
  a citizen, a veteran, a man of the Watch, a good man, and asked.
He wrote that he did not ask you. That is the law. You were in his hand.
  He wrote that he handed you over at the door of the room in the summer,
with the dowry, which was a pot and a blanket and the lamp, and that you went.
  He wrote that you did not weep. He wrote that he wanted me to know it.
He wrote: I am sorry, Zoralos. I could not wait for a resident's four years.
  He wrote my rank, and not my name. He was right to. He is a soldier.
There is no court for this. There is no roll it is wrong on. Nothing was forged.
  A father handed his daughter into a good household. It is all correct.
The count tonight was nine. Nine is the forest moving. I woke the officer.
  We stood on the wall till the morning and nothing came out of the trees.
Nine columns of smoke, Yalzizon, standing up in the dark, and none of them yours.
  In the morning they were gone. I have written nine on the wax.
```

: The Fifth Evening {#fifth}

```poetry {form=elegy lang=en}
Yalzeme, I write your name as a stranger writes it, the whole of it,
  as a clerk would, as the roll would, if the roll had you on it.
The other name is your husband's now, to say in the room by the coals.
  I have given it back. I have smoothed it off the wax.
Three years are standing in the forest. I have written three on the front.
  The sergeant says a man with three years left should count more carefully.
I count. I am the most careful counter on the northern wall.
  One is a hearth. Three is a camp. Ten is the war-band. I count.
At the end of the three there is the door, and I will go through it,
  and be a citizen, and take a room, and the dole, and climb some stairs.
Not your stairs. Somebody's. A man must keep a fire.
  The tower keeps one. I have learned how, these seventeen years.
I do not look at the marsh. I have stopped looking. The lights can find their own way.
  I look at the forest, where the smoke stands up and is counted.
Dusk on the wall, Yalzeme. The Kamezos is lighting the lamp.
  Tonight there is one column of smoke. One is a hearth. I have written one.
```
