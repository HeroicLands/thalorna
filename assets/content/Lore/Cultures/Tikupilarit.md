The Ice-Edge People live on the far northwestern coast of the north, beyond the last fjord of the Cedar People, where the sea freezes every winter and the ice lies fast to the shore for half the year. They call themselves the [[affiliation-tikupilarit|Tikupilarit]], the people of the ice edge, after the line where the shore ice meets open water (_tikupila_), because that is where the spring hunt is made and where a family's year is won or lost. About 50,000 of them live in some two hundred villages on the capes and islands of the [[place-tikumanikrgn|Ice Coast]] and on the river channels of the [[place-taluqnapiqrgn|Lake Tundra]] behind it. No village rules another.

They hunt seal, walrus and whale from kayaks and from open skin boats, with harpoons whose heads turn sideways in the wound and hold, and they eat what the sea gives with seal oil from a stone lamp. Every village has a men's house (_kaniaq_), where the men and boys sleep and the whole village holds its winter feasts. The animals they hunt are persons to them, who watch how they are treated and decide whether to come back.

The Ice-Edge People stand outside the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] and barely know its name. What makes them one of its dangers is their kin. Crews from the northern whaling villages have gone east along the frozen coast for three generations and stayed there, and the older people of the eastern tundra fall back before them.

## Where They Came From

Raven (_kalgaq_) made the land, in the story every child is told first: he pulled it up out of the sea on a harpoon line, dried it in the sun, and then lost the sun through greed, so that the winters are dark. Raven taught people to hunt and left them to learn the rest from the animals, who teach by coming or by staying away.

The coast remembers its own past as a time of war. The villages of each river and each stretch of coast were one people, and the peoples fought each other with bows for generations, in raids at the dark of the year that burned men's houses with the men inside. The men wore armor (_salaq_) of bone and ivory plates laced with thong, and some families still keep a set. The wars ended, in the coast's telling, with [Piqanmalu]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Sun Running, a hunter of [[place-kavrumiq|Kavrumiq]] whose two brothers had died in a raid. He walked unarmed into the men's house of the people who had killed them, carrying an invitation and a list of things his village wanted, and asked them to come to a feast. They came, and the [Messenger Feast]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} has been held between villages every winter since. Feuds still happen. Wars between whole peoples have not happened in living memory.

The great whales came later, or the means to take them did. The village of [[place-imlakkagaq|Imlak Kagaq]], Whale Point, at the northern tip of the coast, learned to hunt the great whales in the spring leads from skin boats with crews of eight. A whale feeds a village for a year, and the men who own the boats (_kalviaqtiq_) became the richest on the coast. The whales also led the Whale Point crews east, as told at the end of this note.

## The Rules of the Catch

**Good is what answers a gift. The seal gives its body to a hunter who has treated seals well, and the hunter answers by giving its soul a drink, sending its bladder home and sharing its meat with every house. Evil is the gift taken and not answered: waste, boasting, hoarding and the closed door.** The Ice-Edge People hold that the animals are persons with their own houses beneath the sea and on the tundra, who see how they are treated and talk among themselves. A seal that is shared, thanked and sent home is reborn, and comes back to the hunter who treated it well. A seal that is wasted or boasted over tells the others, and the seals stay away from that hunter and that village.

The elders teach the [Rules of the Catch]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} (_nuqan_) in the men's house every winter, and every child knows the first of them by heart. A hunter does not boast of a catch, and speaks of a large seal as a small one. Nothing is wasted: the meat is eaten, the blubber rendered into oil, the skin sewn, the gut made into rain-shirts and the bones into tools. A woman gives every seal brought into her house a drink of fresh water (_ivi_), because seals are always thirsty in the salt sea. The bladder (_kuplaq_) of every seal and walrus is kept, because the soul rides in it. And every catch is shared.

Wrongs among people are judged the same way. A theft is a gift taken that nobody gave. A lie spoils the talk of the men's house, where the village decides everything by talking until it agrees. A killing takes a person from a family, and the family is owed that person back.

## Standing in a Village

Success is how many people a household feeds. A boy's first catch (_malqa_), a seal or a bird or even a fish, is given away whole to the old women of the village, and his family holds a small feast for it, so that the first thing a hunter learns is that a catch is for giving. A youth of the men's house (_tiqlak_) hunts for his parents and his elders. A man with a wife and a kayak of his own is a hunter (_kuvlatiq_). A man whose catch feeds more houses than his own is a provider (_nalitiq_), and that is the highest standing a man can earn; no office comes with it, but the men's house listens when he speaks. A woman's standing goes with her house: one whose seams never leak, whose lamp is never out and whose door is never closed is the woman the village admires, and she is the one who decides how her husband's catch is shared.

Failure is the closed door. A man who keeps his catch, or eats in secret, is mocked in songs at the winter feasts, passed over in the shares of other men's catches, and left out of the talk of the men's house. Lazy men are the commonest failures, and the stories are full of them.

The Ice-Edge People value most a skilled hand, a full lamp, a shared catch, patience, a quiet voice, and the elders' teaching. They value least boasting, open anger and waste. A man who shouts is thought dangerous, because the anger he shows is a little of the anger he hides.

## The Man Admired

[Kavrukuvla]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Walrus Hunting, is a provider of Kavrumiq, and the coast names him when it says what a man should be. He was an orphan. His father drowned through the ice when he was four and his mother died two winters later, and he grew up an _ukanaq_, a child without a provider, sleeping at the edge of the men's house and eating what the old women gave him from other men's catches. He made his first kayak frame from driftwood the other boys had left, and his first float from the skin of a seal a widow gave him.

He is past forty now, and his catch feeds eleven houses. The first share of every animal he takes goes to the orphans and widows of the village before his own wife's kin take theirs, and he has done this every year since his first walrus. He takes walrus at the ice edge in spring with a crew of three kayaks, and he is known on the whole coast for going out in weather when others stay in, because a hungry village is worse than a rough sea. He speaks little in the men's house. When he does, the elders stop talking.

The coast admires two things in him: the catch and the giving. His cost is plain. He has lost two kayak partners to the sea in twenty years, and his wife's family complains that the orphans of the village eat before her sisters do.

## The Woman Admired

[Taluqivi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Lake Giving Drink, is the oldest woman of [[place-taqvamiq|Taqvamiq]], Weir Place, on a river of the Lake Tundra, and the women of the coast name her when they say what a woman should be. She has given the drink of water to every seal brought into Taqvamiq for fifty years, and every girl of the village has learned from her how to cut a seal so that nothing is wasted and how to sew a seam that does not leak.

She is remembered for the hungry winter, eighteen years ago, when the fish did not come up the river in autumn and the seals stayed under the ice. She went from house to house with the village speaker and counted every cache, then took charge of the shares herself. Every house got the same, the providers' houses included. She gave her own namesake's feast clothes to a widow to trade upriver for caribou meat, and she ate last. Two old men died that winter, both by their own choice, walking out onto the tundra so that the young could eat. Nobody else died.

Her admiration is a different thing from Kavrukuvla's. He brings food in; she sees that it reaches everyone, and that the animals are treated so that they come back. The coast says that a provider is only as good as the woman who shares his catch.

## What the Villages Keep

The Ice-Edge People write nothing. What holds them together is carved, sewn, sung or remembered.

- **The Rules of the Catch** are their decalogue: the rules of how animals are treated and how a catch is shared, taught by the elders in the men's house every winter night and repeated by heart.
- **The men's house** (_kaniaq_) is the village's charter and its courthouse. It is a large house dug half into the ground and roofed with driftwood and sod, entered through a tunnel, with benches on three sides and a fire pit in the middle. The elders sit on the bench at the back, the providers and hunters on the sides, and the boys near the door.
- **The masks** (_talqun_) are carved for each Asking by a mask maker as a shaman describes them, worn once, and left on the tundra to rot afterward. A mask shows a being the shaman has met: the soul of a seal, the face of the wind, a spirit of the moon with a hand that lets the animals through. No mask is kept.
- **The bladders** of a year's seals and walrus, kept dried and hung in the men's house, are the year's record of what the sea has given.
- **The names** are the most valuable thing a family owns. Every name has been carried before, and the family keeps the memory of who carried it and what they did.
- **The armor** of bone plates kept by a few families is the memory of the wars. Nobody wears it now except at Trading Shore, where the Cedar People's war canoes still come.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is the catch. A hunter leaving in his kayak takes nothing he has not repaired, because the animals do not give themselves to a careless man. A woman whose husband brings in a seal meets it at the door with a dipper of fresh water and pours a little into its mouth. She cuts it on the floor of her house in the order her mother taught her, keeps the bladder, and sends a child with shares to every house that is owed one before the family eats. The bones go back into the sea, not to the dogs.

Men and boys take the fire bath (_aqsun_) in the men's house most evenings, sitting naked around the fire with the door shut until the heat drives the sweat out of them, and then talk until they sleep. The winter talk is the school: the elders tell the Rules, the stories of Raven, the histories of names, and how to read the ice.

## The Round of the Year

The year has two halves. In the light half the people scatter to hunting and fishing camps, and in the dark half they gather in the villages for the feasts.

- **The [Bladder Feast]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** comes at midwinter. The bladders of every seal and walrus taken in the year are blown up, painted and hung in the men's house for five days, with lamps burning under them and songs sung to them. On the last night the men carry them out onto the sea ice, cut a hole, and push them down through it one by one. It is for sending the animals' souls home to be reborn, so that they come back.
- **The [Feast of the Dead]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** comes every few winters, when a village has enough dead to honor. Each family that has lost someone feeds and clothes the namesake who carries that person's name, in new boots and a new parka, and the dead come to eat through them. It is for feeding the dead, told below.
- **The Messenger Feast** comes in the dark of the year, between two villages. The hosts send two messengers (_malutiq_) to the guest village with a list of the things they want, a wolf skin, a new kayak frame, a wife's sister for a widower, and the guests arrive a month later with the things on the list and a list of their own. The hosts feed them for days and give them what they asked. Next winter the roles turn. It is for peace between villages and for trade, and most marriages between villages are arranged there.
- **The [Asking]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** (_tayuqun_) comes in late winter, before the ice opens. The shaman describes to a mask maker what lies beneath the sea, the mask maker carves it, and men dance the masks in the men's house to the drums while the women dance behind them with finger masks and feather wands. It is for asking the animals to come in the spring.

## Praise, Song and the Door

Reward comes from the village's talk. A boy's first catch is announced in the men's house and his family feasts the village. A provider is given the seat nearest the elders and a song is made about him. A woman whose sewing is admired is asked to cut the skins for a new skin boat, and her name is spoken when the boat is launched.

Punishment runs from words to death, in this order. The first is a word from an elder, in private. The second is the song: at a winter feast the offender's fault is sung in front of the village, and the offender must sit through it and laugh. The third is the share withheld: the offender is passed over when other men's catches are divided. The fourth is silence: nobody speaks to the offender, in the men's house or out of it. The fifth is the door: the offender is put out of the men's house and must sleep in a women's house like a child, or leave the village. The last is death. A man who has killed more than once, or a shaman known to have sent sickness, may be killed by agreement of the men's house, and the men of his own family carry it out, so that no feud follows.

## The Men's House and the Shaman

Civil and religious authority sit in one house. The men's house is the council, the court, the school and the church of a village, and no one person rules it. The elders (_utkiq_) sit on the back bench, and one of them, the speaker (_kalitiq_), opens and closes every council. A matter is talked over until the men agree; a provider's word weighs more than a youth's, and the speaker's most of all, but nobody gives an order. The elders try a dispute by hearing both sides and the village's memory of both families. The women decide the shares of a catch, and the speaker keeps the village's memory of debts, gifts and names.

The shaman (_aklutiq_), a man or a woman, holds the other half. The shaman goes into trance in the men's house, beneath the sea to the houses of the animals and down to the village of the dead, and comes back with what they want; describes the masks, heals the sick, finds the cause of a bad season, and names the person whose breach of the Rules has kept the seals away. A shaman is feared as well as needed, because the power that heals can sicken. When a shaman and the elders disagree, the elders decide what the village does, and the shaman decides what the animals want. When a shaman is accused of sending sickness, the men's house tries the case, and some shamans have been killed for it.

## The Name Comes Back

After death the soul (_tinaq_) stays near the village for five days. For those five days nobody in the village uses a knife or a needle, so as not to cut it, and nobody hunts. Then it goes to the village of the dead, which is like a living village but dark and hungry, where the dead wait to be fed. The name does not go with it. The next child born in the village, or in the family, takes the name (_kilu_), and with it something of the dead person: their skill, their temper, and their kin.

That belief settles an ordinary day more than any law.

- **A namesake is that person.** A girl named for a dead grandmother is called "mother" by the old woman's sons and given her sewing bag. A boy named for a drowned hunter is called "husband" by the widow in jest and fed first at her fire. Children are rarely scolded, because the person in them is often old and owed respect.
- **Food goes to the dead.** At every meal a pinch of food is dropped by the lamp and a little water poured on the floor, for the dead who are hungry and thirsty. A family spends more on the Feast of the Dead than on anything else: a year's sewing, new clothes for every namesake, and food for the village.
- **The old are respected as future children.** A dying person often says aloud which unborn child should carry the name, and the family honors it if the child is born in time.
- **A grave holds tools.** The dead are laid on the tundra in a box of driftwood, because the ground is frozen, with their kayak, their bow or their sewing bag beside them. Each tool is broken first, so that it dies too and goes with its owner.
- **A feud ends with a name.** When one man kills another, the dead man's kin are owed him back. The elders of both men's houses settle it with gifts and with a name: the next child born to the killer's family is named for the dead man and raised as one of his kin's. Most feuds stop there. A family that refuses the name may take blood instead, and the men's house does not stop it.

## The Living and the Dead

The living feed the dead through their namesakes, and the dead know it. A dead person whose namesake goes cold and hungry, or whose name is given to nobody, comes back as a ghost (_kamuq_) at the edge of the village in the dark months, and brings sickness and bad hunting with it. At the Feast of the Dead the families light lamps on the graves, sing to the dead to invite them, and feed and clothe the namesakes in the men's house while the dead eat through them.

The shaman can go down to the village of the dead in trance and bring back word of what one of the dead wants, and is paid with a gift, usually a share of the next seal the family takes, and nobody thinks that wrong.

## What a Person Owes

A person owes the village a share of every catch; the animals a drink, a sent bladder and no waste; the dead food at every meal and clothes for their namesakes; the elders silence while they speak; and a guest a place by the lamp and food before any question. A man owes his wife's parents a year of hunting before the marriage is settled. A woman owes her household's catch a fair cutting and a fair sharing.

A person is owed a share of every large catch taken by anyone in the village, a place in a house, a name, a hearing in the men's house, a namesake's care after death, and, if killed, either the killer's blood or a child to carry the name. The Rules run both ways. A provider who does not share forfeits the shares of others, and a village that lets a widow starve is a village the seals leave.

## The Weak

Every catch shares out the same way: the first portions go to the houses without a provider (_ukanaq_), the widows, the orphans and the old, before the hunter's own kin take theirs. This is a rule, not a kindness, and a hunter who skips it is sung about at the next feast. A widow keeps her house, which was always hers, and lives on the shares until she remarries or a son or a son-in-law begins to hunt for her. An orphan is taken in by its mother's kin, or by a household that needs a child, and adoption is common and carries no shame. The stories make heroes of orphans, and Kavrukuvla is the living proof the coast points to.

A stranger who reaches a village is fed in the men's house before anyone asks his business. A stranger of the Ice-Edge People is a guest anywhere on the coast. A stranger from outside, a Cedar trader or a forest hunter come down a river, is fed and watched, and the Red Stone Compact's pipe gives no one any standing here.

## Those Who Cannot Hunt

A man who can no longer go out in a kayak works in the men's house: he carves harpoon heads, bends kayak frames, makes nets, and teaches the boys. A woman whose eyes fail cuts thread from sinew and chews boot soles soft. The old tell the stories and the Rules, and they hold the memory of every name. They are fed from the shares like the widows.

In a starving winter an old person who can no longer work may choose to walk out onto the tundra or the ice so that the young can eat, and the family lets them go. It is a choice, never an order; the village speaks of it as a gift, the largest one a person can give, and the name goes to the next child born.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the house: a woman's sod house for women, girls and small children, the men's house for men and boys. A family that loses its house to fire or flood moves into another's until a new one is dug. Teaching is divided by sex. At five or six a boy moves into the men's house, where his father, his uncles and the old men teach him to make tools, to handle a kayak, to read the ice and the weather, and the Rules. A girl stays in her mother's house and learns to cut, sew, cook and render oil, and the stories her grandmother tells.

Defense is the men's house and the village. The Ice-Edge People fight with sinew-backed bows, spears and knives of stone and copper, and most of them have never fought at all. Trading Shore, the village nearest the Cedar People, keeps bone armor and a watch on the headland, because the Cedar war canoes come north for slaves every few years. The villages of the coast have no walls. Against southern steel they have no answer, and they have never heard of it.

## The House and the Family

The family is the extended kin of both parents, and the household is two houses: the women's house, where a wife, her mother, her unmarried daughters and the small children live, and the men's house, where her husband, her sons and her father sleep. A husband eats the meals his wife sends to the men's house and visits her house at night. A new husband lives a year with his wife's family and hunts for them; afterward the couple decide where to live, and most stay near the wife's mother. Children belong to both parents' kin, and their namesake ties bind them to a third family as well.

The age ladder is plain. An infant is named for the dead and treated as that person come back. A small child plays, gathers eggs and berries, and is rarely scolded. At five or six a boy moves to the men's house; a girl's first sewing is shown to the village at about the same age. A child's first catch is given away whole. A youth of the men's house hunts for others and waits for a wife and a kayak. A married man hunts, and becomes a provider if he can. A married woman runs a house, cuts the catch and sews. The old teach, carve, remember and are fed. The dying decide who carries their name. The dead wait in the dark to be fed, and come back in the next child.

## The Taking

The sin the Ice-Edge People committed is the [Taking]{.name data-type="lore/history"}. Three generations ago a skin boat crew of Whale Point, led by its boat-holder [Imlakaklu]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Whale Diving, followed a struck whale east along the northern ice and could not get home before the sea froze. They wintered on a point far to the east among the people the coast calls [Those Who Give Way]{.name data-type="lore/culture"} (_kipirit_), the older people of the eastern tundra, who hunt seals at their breathing holes on foot and have neither skin boats nor dogs. The camp fed the crew through the winter. In spring both struck the same whale in a lead off the point, and Imlakaklu's crew killed the camp's hunters over it and took the point. The camp's women and children walked south across the tundra and were not seen again.

Imlakaklu came home the next summer with the whale's jaw on his boat and the news that the east was full of whales. Whale Point made a song of it, and his mask is danced at the Asking in the men's house of Whale Point every winter. Crews have gone east from Whale Point every spring since, and many have stayed, and they are the [East People]{.name data-type="lore/culture"} (_mapirit_). Where they settle, Those Who Give Way lose their hunting places and fall back south toward the forest, into the hunting country at the edge of the [[affiliation-osetakona|Maple People]].

Not every crew came home with Imlakaklu. [Kilnaqivi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Star Giving Drink, the wife of one of his crew, crossed to the east with the next summer's boats, found two children of the camp living alone at the point, and brought them back to Whale Point to raise in her own house. Their descendants live there still, and the coast counts her a hero alongside the song she argued against.

The blind spot is the Rules themselves, and thoughtful Ice-Edge People argue over it. The coast holds that every gift must be answered. It answers the seal with a drink and the village with a share, and it settles a killing with a name. It does not apply the Rules to Those Who Give Way. The coast's name for them says that the land is finished with them and that they step aside, as the seals leave a careless hunter. Whale Point's boat-holders, among them Imlakaklu's descendant [Tikumalu]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Ice Running, hold that the whales chose the Whale Point crews and that a people the animals leave has broken the Rules somewhere. The speaker of Kavrumiq's men's house answers that the camp gave the crew a winter's food and was answered with killing, that a gift taken and not answered is the first evil the Rules name, and that the whales remember. The Bladder Feast has not been held jointly between Whale Point and Kavrumiq since the argument began.
