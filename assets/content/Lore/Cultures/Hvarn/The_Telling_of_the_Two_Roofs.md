**The Telling of the Two Roofs** is the hearth-telling, _kuzhdzel_, of how the long house at [[place-nurvhrn|Nurvharn]] began. A _kuzhdzel_ is told in the closed months, at a fire, while the hands are busy, by whoever is telling, and the others at the fire cut in when they know it otherwise. This one is told in the dark weeks before midwinter, before the rotation, and every hearth tells it, each a little differently. It is the nearest thing the Hvarn tell to an account of their own beginning, and it begins with a house rather than with the world.

The telling below is the one given at the Rhonzhel fire by [Sulzhe]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Rhonzhel hearth, an old woman greasing a rope, with [Ovru]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Klemverd hearth at the next fire mending a pack-saddle. The Klemverd hearth tells one part of it differently, and Ovru says so when the telling reaches it. The figures of the telling are [Habvern]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Nurvharn fire, [Rhistne]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Cold Valley fire and [Zhavel]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Habvern's son, whom the storm took at the [Butter Stone]{.name data-type="place/feature"} on the upper shelf of [[place-jnanadvar|Jnanadvar]], where Habvern's father [Melerhu]{.name data-type="being/npc"} and Rhistne's sister [Sulzhe]{.name data-type="being/npc"} had been found before him. The other places the telling names are the [Cold Ford]{.name data-type="place/feature"} below the scholars' door and the [Black Gully]{.name data-type="place/feature"} on the conch-door. The winter is the [Winter of the Two Roofs]{.name data-type="lore/history"}.

## The Telling

Hands to the work, and I will tell it. Give me the grease. No, the pot, the other pot. That one is for the saddles.

This is the telling of the two roofs. It is told now, in the dark before the turn, because this is when it happened, and because the turn is what it is about, and you will sit through the turn in a few weeks and hear the hearth-heads argue in the middle of the house till the fire is ash, and you should know why they are sitting where they sit.

Before the house, there was no house. Each fire wintered by itself. A fire was what a fire is now: the people who eat at it, the old, the children, the ones taken in. Each fire had its own head-valley, and in its head-valley its own turf house, low, the size of this end of this hearth and no bigger, and the fire went into it at the first hard frost and came out at the thaw. In summer they went up, as we go up, to the grass with the beasts. In winter each fire was by itself under the snow, in its own valley, and the next fire was over a ridge, and the ridge was shut till spring.

There were two crossings then, as there are now. The scholars' door and the conch-door. They were there before us and they will be there after, and the telling does not say how they came to be. It says only that there were two, and more fires than two, and that each fire went over when it chose and took what parties it could get. When two fires wanted one party, they argued for it at the foot of the road, and sometimes one fire's people went up the road early in the dark to be first on the shelf, so that the lowland man would have to take them or go home. That was how a road was kept then. Each fire kept it as though it were its own.

And each fire kept its dead. They had names then as we have, off the dead, because every fire said its dead over to its children, and a child was named for one of them, and answered with the place. But each fire said its own. A child at one fire knew where his grandmother's brother was found and did not know where anyone at the next fire was found. Why would he? It was over the ridge.

[Ovru, from the next fire:]{.speaker} And knowing the ground was worth a party.

Ovru says it. He is right. Knowing where the ground fails was worth a party, because a fire that knew the shelf was the fire a lowland man would take. So a fire that lost someone at a bad place on the shelf said it to its own children and to nobody else. That is the thing to keep in your head through the telling. A fire that knew where the ground failed kept it to itself.

There was a fire in this valley, the valley the house stands in now. The telling calls it the Nurvharn fire, for the valley. The head of the fire was a woman, Habvern, with a son of the age to walk on a rope, Zhavel. Her father, Melerhu, had been found at the Butter Stone.

You know the Butter Stone. Round, the size of a hut, out of the shelf above the Wet Step, walked round on the gorge side because the face side is too narrow for a pack. The lip under it on the gorge side goes soft in the thaw. Habvern's father went round it with a pack in a thaw and the lip went, and the Nurvharn fire went down for him and brought him up, and said it to its children: the lip under the Butter Stone, the gorge side, in the thaw. They said it to their children. They did not say it over the ridge.

Over the ridge to the west there was the Cold Valley, and the Cold Valley fire. Its head was a man, Rhistne. His sister, Sulzhe, had been found at the Butter Stone, two summers after Habvern's father. My own name is hers, which is why the Rhonzhel fire tells this part slowly. The lip under the stone, the gorge side. The Cold Valley fire went down for her and brought her up and said it to its children, and did not say it over the ridge.

So both fires knew. Each knew by itself.

[A child, at Sulzhe's knee:]{.speaker} Did they not know that the other one knew?

How would they? It was over the ridge. Pass me the end. Hold it there. No, there, where my thumb is.

That autumn the frost came late, and both fires had a party on the scholars' door, and both parties were late on the road. The Nurvharn fire had taken a lowland man with books and a beast, and Habvern was on the rope in front and Zhavel was second. The Cold Valley fire had taken two lowland men with medicines, and Rhistne was in front of his. They went up the shelf a day apart, Habvern's first. They had argued about it at the foot of the road.

And on the shelf the first hard frost came with a storm behind it. You have seen a storm come up the eastern valleys. There is nothing, and then there is the Ear speaking in daylight, and then there is nothing to see past your own hand. Habvern's party was at the Butter Stone when it came. She went round on the gorge side, because a pack will not go round the face side. The lip held under her. She called Zhavel on, and he came round after her with the lowland man's beast, and the lip went.

It went under the beast. The beast went down. Zhavel was roped to it, because he had tied himself to the beast to keep it on the shelf in the wind, and the beast took him off the lip, and the rope between Zhavel and his mother came tight, and held, and held, and then the weight of the beast and the boy together was more than she could hold on that shelf in that wind. She had a knife. The telling does not say whether she used it. It says the rope parted, and that is all it says. Do not ask me which. I do not know, and the telling does not know.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} Nobody knows.

Nobody knows. Habvern and the lowland man were on the shelf, at the stone, in the storm, and Zhavel and the beast were in the gorge.

They did not go on and they could not go down. They sat in against the face of the stone with their backs to it, and the storm went on through the night. In the morning it had eased enough to see, and the next party was coming up the shelf below them. It was Rhistne's.

Rhistne came up to the stone on the face side, and stopped, and looked at it, and sent his lowland men to sit against the face, and came round to the gorge side to go on. Habvern was sitting there. She said to him, "Not the gorge side. The lip goes there."

He said, "I know it does."

She said, "It went under my father."

He said, "It went under my sister."

That is all that was said at the stone, and that is where the house began, and this is the part of the telling a child is made to say back. _It went under my father. It went under my sister._ Say it.

[The child:]{.speaker} It went under my father. It went under my sister.

Good. Now. Rhistne stood there at the stone with the storm coming up again behind the ridge, and he had known the lip was bad, and she had known it, and her son was in the gorge, and his sister had been in the same gorge in the same place. Neither of them said anything about that. What Rhistne said was, "We will go down together. One rope." And they did. Two fires on one rope, both lowland parties tied in between them, Rhistne in front and Habvern at the back, all the way down the shelf, past the Wet Step, onto the grass, where the beasts were. It is the first time two fires walked on one rope, and the telling says so.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} The Klemverd telling says it was Habvern in front.

The Klemverd telling would. It does not matter who was in front. They were on one rope.

They came off the road into the snow, because the snow had come down while they were on the shelf, and it was still coming. The Cold Valley was over the ridge, and the ridge was shut. The Nurvharn valley was below them. So they all went down into this valley to Habvern's house, the Cold Valley fire and its two lowland men and the Nurvharn fire and its one, and they went in under the Nurvharn roof, and the snow shut the valley that night and did not open it till spring.

Now you have to see the house. The Nurvharn house then was one fire's house. Low. The length of this hearth and the next. Everything the Nurvharn fire was lived in it all winter: the old ones and the children and the beasts at the end, and the winter's barley in the pit, and the butter, and the rope. And now there was a second fire in it, as many again, and three lowland men who did not know where to put their feet, and the beasts of both, and the snow on the roof.

They could not all lie down at once. They tried it the first night, and the Cold Valley children slept on top of the Nurvharn children, and the old slept sitting up, and in the morning the barley pit had been stepped in. On the second night the old woman of the Nurvharn fire, who was Habvern's mother and whose husband was at the Butter Stone, got up in the dark, and took the turf knife, and went to the end wall, the west wall, and began to cut it.

That is the Rhonzhel telling, and this is the Rhonzhel fire, and I tell it as it is told here. She cut the end wall. The Cold Valley people went out into the snow with her, the ones who could dig, and cut turf from under the snow on the slope, frozen, in blocks, and built on, out from the cut end, a second house on the end of the first, low, as long again. They had no ridge-pole for it. They had the long poles of the lowland men's beast-frames, and they lashed those end to end with rope, and lashed the end of them to the Nurvharn ridge-pole where the wall had been, and laid the turf on. The place where the two poles were lashed is the seam. It leaked all that winter. They greased the lashing to keep the water out, and it went on leaking, and they went on greasing it.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} Now I will say it.

Say it. Hands to the saddle while you do.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} The Klemverd telling has it this way. The Cold Valley fire was not cut a hole for. On the third day there was one clear morning, and Rhistne and every Cold Valley man and woman who could walk went back over the ridge to the Cold Valley, in the snow, on one rope, all of them, and took their own house down. They cut the turf off their own roof and carried it, and their own ridge-pole, back over the ridge in the one clear day and the night after, and built their own roof on the end of the Nurvharn house, with their own pole, lashed. Two roofs. Not one roof made longer. That is why it is the Two Roofs. The Cold Valley fire was not taken in under anyone's roof. It brought its roof.

That is the Klemverd telling, and Ovru's grandmothers were of the Cold Valley fire, and he says it every winter at this point, and every winter I let him. You hear why it matters to them. A fire that is taken in under another's roof takes that fire's name. The Taken-in eat at a fire that is not theirs. The Klemverd hearth says it was never taken in, by anyone, ever, and it says it here, at the seam, and the telling keeps both.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} Both, and ours is right.

Both. And the Rhonzhel fire asks how a fire carried a roof over a ridge in a winter storm in one day, and the Klemverd fire says, the way you carry anything, on one rope. Both. Get the end, child, it is on the floor.

So there were two roofs, or one roof made long, and two fires under it, and a seam in the middle that leaked. And then there was the winter.

You know a winter in this house. You have never had a winter anywhere else. Think what it was for them, who had never had a winter anywhere but their own house with their own people. Now there was nowhere to go. A quarrel had nowhere to go. A Nurvharn man who would not sit with a Cold Valley man had nowhere else to eat, because the barley was in one pit and the fire was the fire. Nobody had a corner. The lowland men wanted corners and there were none, and they sat with their backs to the wall and looked at the roof, all winter.

And there was the boy in the gorge. Zhavel was under the Butter Stone, in the gorge, in the snow, and could not be fetched till the thaw. Habvern sat at her fire. Rhistne sat at his, which was the same fire, near enough, the house being what it was. On the longest night, the old woman, Habvern's mother, who had cut the wall—

[Ovru:]{.speaker} Who had not cut the wall.

—who had cut the wall, or had not, said to the house that it was time to say the dead.

Each fire, you remember, said its dead over to its own children in the dark of the year. They had always done it. The Nurvharn fire said theirs on the longest night, and the Cold Valley fire said theirs on the longest night, and that winter there was one longest night and one house. So the old woman said: say them together. Say ours and say yours. There is nowhere for the children to go not to hear.

Rhistne said, say yours first. It is your house.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} It was both houses.

He said, it is your end of the house. Say yours.

And she began to say them. Not the way the Bone-bringer says the roll now. The way a fire said its dead then, in the order they had died, one after another, a name, a place, what the ground did. Her own grandmother at the Cold Ford, below the scholars' door, in the spring water. Her husband's brother at the Black Gully on the conch-door. And when she said the Black Gully, one of the Cold Valley women said out loud from the other end, "The stones at the top of it, that come down in the afternoon?" and the old woman said, "The stones at the top, when the sun is on them," and the Cold Valley woman said, "Ours too. My aunt. In the afternoon." And the house went quiet.

Then the old woman went on, and a Cold Valley voice stopped her again at the Cold Ford. And again at the head of the Black Gully. And when she came to her own husband, at the Butter Stone, the lip on the gorge side, in the thaw, Rhistne said it with her: "In the thaw." And she stopped, and looked at him down the length of the house, and he said, "My sister. The same. Two summers after."

And the old woman said, "You knew. When my son went round."

Habvern said, "He knew. I knew. We both knew."

Then she said the rest of it, at the stone, the words a child is made to say. _It went under my father. It went under my sister._ She said it to the house.

[The child:]{.speaker} It went under my father. It went under my sister.

Yes. And when the Nurvharn dead were all said, Rhistne said the Cold Valley dead, from the oldest, and the Nurvharn people stopped him at the Black Gully and at the Cold Ford, and three times more where the Cold Valley had lost someone at a place the Nurvharn fire had already lost someone, and had not been told. By the end of it the fire was down to ash and nobody had built it up, and they had all of them, both fires, heard every place on both crossings where the ground had ever been known to fail, from the grass to the summit, and every one of the places, nearly, had been known to one fire and not said to the other.

So they counted. That is the next part. Rhistne counted, aloud, and the old woman counted, aloud, the way you count a debt between hearths now. How many of the Cold Valley dead were found at a place the Nurvharn fire had known was bad, and had not said. How many of the Nurvharn dead at a place the Cold Valley fire had known. The telling does not give the numbers. The Rhonzhel telling says they came out near even. The Klemverd telling says—

[Ovru:]{.speaker} Three and two. Three of ours on your account. Two of yours on ours.

—says three and two, and you see whose favour that is. But both tellings say the last one is Zhavel. Zhavel was on the Cold Valley account. Rhistne's sister had been found at the Butter Stone and Rhistne had not said it over the ridge, and Zhavel went round on the gorge side. Rhistne said it. He said, "Your son is on my account." In front of the whole house, at the seam, under the leak. Nobody had ever said a thing like that to another fire. It is the first debt said aloud between hearths, and it was said at the seam, and the seam is the middle of the house. You will see the hearth-heads sitting there in a few weeks. That is why they sit there.

[The child:]{.speaker} How did he pay it?

There is no paying it. You cannot pay a boy. You know that. What would you give? Barley? A beast? A boy is not on the same account as barley. Habvern said so. She said, "There is nothing in your end of the house that is the size of him."

And Rhistne said, "Then the road."

He said: the Cold Valley fire will not go up the road early in the dark to be first on the shelf. The Cold Valley fire will not argue at the foot of the road. When there is a party on the scholars' door, we will say together, in this house, in the winter, which fire walks it. And when one fire has walked it, it will be the other fire's turn. And whatever one fire knows of the ground, it will say in this house, in the winter, to everybody, both ends. That is what the Cold Valley has that is the size of a boy. The road, and the ground.

Habvern said nothing for a long time. The telling says "the length of the roll," which is long, though there was no roll then. And then she said the Nurvharn fire would do the same. And the old woman built up the fire.

That is the first turn. You see it. The turn is not a sharing of the work because the work is short. The work is short, yes, two roads and too many fires, but that is not why there is a turn. The turn is a payment. It is the Cold Valley paying for Zhavel, and the Nurvharn fire paying for the ones on its account, and it is never finished, because a boy is never paid for. So it is made again every winter, from nothing, in the middle of the house, at the seam.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} That part the Klemverd telling has the same.

That part everyone has the same.

Then the thaw. When the snow went off the ridge they went up, both fires, on one rope, with the lowland men, and over the scholars' door to the far side, which was late, very late, and the lowland men were glad to go and did not come back. And on the way up, at the Butter Stone, they went down for Zhavel. Rhistne went down on the rope, and Habvern held it, and two Cold Valley men behind her. He was in the scree with the beast, a day's work down. They brought him up. Rhistne brought him up. The telling says that, and says it plain, and it is the only thing it says about Rhistne after the debt. Brought up by Rhistne of the Cold Valley fire.

And at the next winter, which they wintered together again, in the same house, both roofs, the old woman said the dead again on the longest night. Only this time she did not say the Nurvharn dead and then the Cold Valley dead. She said them by the ground. The Cold Ford, and everyone of both fires who had been found there. Then the shelf, the Butter Stone, and everyone. Then the next place, and the next. She walked the road aloud, from the grass to the summit, the scholars' door first and then the conch-door, and at every place she said every name of both fires that the place had. And at the Butter Stone she said Melerhu her husband, and Sulzhe, Rhistne's sister, and Zhavel, in that order, the oldest first, and _brought up by Rhistne of the Cold Valley fire_. That is the first roll. That is why the roll goes by the ground and not by the years. It is two fires' dead said as one people's.

[A child, at the next fire:]{.speaker} And the names?

The names. Yes. That winter the house gave the fires names. Not the valley names any longer, because a valley is over a ridge and the house is here. A hearth name is the house's. The Nurvharn fire kept the valley's name for its hearth name, because its wall was the first wall, and it owns nothing by it. The Cold Valley fire took the name Klemverd.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} Took it, with its own roof over it.

With its own roof over it, says the Klemverd hearth. And the next winter a third fire came over the ridge from the east in the autumn, and asked to winter at the end, because it had heard what the two fires had said to each other and wanted to hear the rest. And the house cut the east wall and built on. And the winter after, a fourth at the west end, and so on, at either end, as you see it now, so far that you can walk from this fire to the far end in the dark and pass through eleven households and never put your head outside. The two first roofs are in the middle. The seam is in the middle. It does not leak now. It is greased every winter anyway, by whoever is Turn-keeper, before the turn is made, and the telling does not say why, and the Turn-keeper does it anyway.

[The child:]{.speaker} Is Zhavel in the roll?

He is in the roll. The Butter Stone. The lip on the gorge side, under the beast, in the storm. Brought up by Rhistne of the Klemverd hearth, which was the Cold Valley fire. The Bone-bringer says it every winter, and the floor has never had a word against it. And his name is at a fire in this house now, and the child who carries it is in this house now, and when the Bone-bringer comes to the Butter Stone in a few weeks you will see who stands up.

[The child:]{.speaker} And Rhistne?

Rhistne of the Cold Valley died old, in this house, sitting up, in the winter, of nothing. A person who dies in the house is not in the roll. The roll is for the ground. So nobody is named for him, and nobody answers for him with a place.

[Ovru:]{.speaker} He is in the telling.

He is in the telling instead, which is a different thing, and the Klemverd hearth keeps him in it.

That is told. Give me the other end of the rope. It is greased to here. Hands to the work.
