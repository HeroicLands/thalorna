**The Drover Who Went Over Alone** is a hearth-telling, _kuzhdzel_, told at the Hvurnash fire against the Hvurnash hearth. Its fool is [Ourn]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Hvurnash hearth, a young Drover, who went up the scholars' door, [[place-jnanadvar|Jnanadvar]], on a day the Storm-reader had closed, because he wanted to be seen at the snow-line before anyone else, and walked the whole of the upper shelf by himself, by the roll, with one beast and a lowland envoy's crate. The Reach-guide whose party it was is [Tsevrel]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Dzelbern hearth. It is a funny telling, and it is told to children of the Hvurnash fire when one of them has been caught doing something keen, and to anyone else when the fire wants a laugh.

The other hearths tell it too, and the Tsedvern hearth says the saying at the end of it is older than Ourn. The telling below is the one given by [Vurn]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Hvurnash hearth, an old woman, to a Drover of her own fire who had gone up to the grass a day early that autumn.

## The Telling

Sit down. No, there, where I can see you. Bring the butter. Hands to it.

You went up to the grass a day early. You did. Your mother told me, and the Turn-keeper told me, and the beasts told me, because the beasts came down a day early too, and looked at me. You wanted to be there first. You wanted the Reach-guide to come up in the morning and find you already there, with the beasts watered, and think: that one. That is what you wanted. Do not tell me it was not.

So now you get Ourn. Every child at this fire who has been keen gets Ourn. I got Ourn. My mother got Ourn. Ourn was ours, of this hearth, and the other hearths tell it to laugh at us, and we tell it first, so they can only laugh second.

Ourn was a Drover of this hearth, a long time ago, at about your age. He kept the beasts. He was good with the beasts, better than you, and he knew the roll, all of it, both crossings, the way you do, the way everyone does who has sat through the recitation every winter of his life. He could say the upper shelf from the grass to the ushtakas' wall faster than the Bone-bringer and never miss a place. That matters. Remember it.

The turn that winter gave the scholars' door to the Dzelbern hearth, and the Dzelbern hearth gave it to Tsevrel, who was a Reach-guide of some years. In the summer an envoy came up from the lowland, from one of the eastern janpodas, going to talk to the frontier officers on the far side. He had two servants and four beasts, and one of the beasts was to carry a crate. The crate was a gift for the frontier officers. It was heavy. Nobody knew what was in it, and the envoy said not to ask, and so of course everybody at the foot of the road asked, and he did not say.

Tsevrel took the party. She took two Roadwalkers on the rope with her. And she asked this hearth for Drovers, as a hearth does, to bring the beasts up through the lower stages and hand them on at the snow-line, and this hearth gave her Ourn and one other, and the fee would come to the Dzelbern hearth, because it was the Dzelbern hearth's road, and Ourn would be owed his place at a fire, as a Drover is.

The first day they went up through the lower stages to the grass under the snow-line, and camped. Ourn and the other Drover had the beasts. The plan was the plan. In the morning, the Storm-reader would read the day. If it was good, the party would go on to the snow-line with the beasts, and the Drovers would hand on the beasts that were going up the shelf, and go back down with the rest.

Now. In the night, Ourn lay by the beasts and thought. He thought: in the morning, Tsevrel will come up to the snow-line, and the beasts will be there, and the crate on its beast, and Ourn with them, already, waiting, with the frost on him. And she will see. She will remember it at the turn.

So he got up in the dark. He did not wake the other Drover. He took the crate-beast, the one with the envoy's crate, because that was the one that mattered, and he went up from the grass to the snow-line in the dark, by the path, the way you went up to the grass a day early. And he got there before light, and sat down with the beast in the lee of a rock, and waited for the party, with the frost on him, looking keen.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} What is wrong with that?

Nothing. Nothing yet. Butter.

In the morning, at the grass, the Storm-reader went out of the tent and looked at the east, and came back in, and said, _Not today._ You know what that is. That is the word. There is no word after it. Tsevrel said nothing, because there is nothing to say. The party stayed at the grass. The envoy complained. Let him. And the other Drover woke up and counted the beasts and found one short, and the crate short with it, and Ourn short with that. And he told Tsevrel. And Tsevrel looked up at the snow-line, and there was cloud on it already, low, coming over. And she said, "He will come down when he sees it."

He did not see it. That is the first thing. He was in the lee of the rock with his back to the east, looking down the path for the party, looking keen. The cloud came over the snow-line behind him and sat down on it. And when he turned round there was nothing to see past his own hand, and when he turned back the path down was gone too.

Now, a sensible Drover would sit where he is, in the lee of the rock, with the beast, until the cloud lifts. That is what you would do. Say it.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} I would sit.

You would sit. Ourn looked at the ground in front of him, at the snow-line, in the cloud, and he saw tracks. Old tracks, in old snow, going up. Some other party's, from some other week, going up onto the shelf. And Ourn thought: the party has gone up. They went past me in the cloud. They have gone up and left me. And he got up, and he took the beast, and he went after them.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} Alone?

Alone. By himself. In his own form. Say that back to me.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} By himself. In his own form.

So he went up onto the shelf. And here is the part the other hearths laugh at, and the part this hearth tells slowest, because here Ourn did nothing wrong. Every step of the upper shelf, he walked by the roll.

The Wet Step. _After noon. On the inside._ He knew it. It was morning. So he sat down at the foot of the Wet Step, in the cloud, with the beast, and waited till the sun was high, though he could not see the sun, and he guessed it by his belly, and it was near enough. And when his belly said noon he walked it on the inside, the face side, with the beast on the short rope behind him, and the ice was off the step, and he was over.

The Butter Stone. _Round the stone with a stick. Try the lip. The second stone of the lip._ He had no stick. Drovers carry a goad for the beasts and he had left it at the grass. So he opened the envoy's crate.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} He did not.

He opened it. He broke the cord on the lid with his teeth. Nobody had told him not to open it. They had told him not to ask what was in it, and he did not ask; he looked. And in the crate there was a cloth, and in the cloth there was a staff of black wood the length of a man, with a silver head on it shaped like something nobody at this fire has ever agreed on, and under that there were other things, and the telling does not say what. Ourn took the staff. He put his hand on the Butter Stone going round, because everyone does, and he tried the lip on the gorge side with the envoy's silver staff. He tried the first stone and it was firm. He tried the second stone and the staff went into it, and a piece of it dropped into the gorge, and he listened a long time and did not hear it land. He stepped over the second stone, onto the third, and brought the beast round after him, with the beast's feet put one by one where his had been. Over.

The Knee. _At the Knee, call. At the Knee the rope is short. Call._ He shortened the beast's rope. He went round the outside of the bend, because the snow was on the inside, a hand deep, flat, and he had heard the roll, and he looked at the snow, and he did not believe it. _Look behind the snow._ And at the Knee, he called. He called out loud, as you call to the one behind. And nobody answered. Of course nobody answered. There was nobody behind him but the beast.

So he answered himself.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} He did not.

The telling says he did. He called, and he waited, and nothing answered, and he thought, the call has to be answered or it is not a call, and he answered it, in his own voice, from the outside of the bend. And the beast came round. Over.

[A woman at the next fire:]{.speaker} The Rhonzhel telling says the beast answered.

The Rhonzhel telling says the beast answered. The Rhonzhel fire would. Go on with your own butter.

The Two Teeth. _Lean on neither tooth. Hand the pack over. Do not let go of it till it is taken._ And the beast would not go between the teeth, because no beast does, and the crate would not go between them either. So he had to unload the crate and hand it over the top. To whom? There was nobody on the other side to take it. So he set the crate on the top of the gap, between the teeth, balanced, without leaning on either tooth, and he climbed through the gap sideways, and reached up from the far side, and took it. He took it from himself. He did not let go of it till it was taken. Then he went back through for the beast, and the beast went round, I do not know how, the telling does not say, the telling says only that the beast went round and that nobody has ever got a beast round the Two Teeth since. Over.

The Ear. _When the Ear speaks in daylight, the Storm-reader has already spoken._ And the Ear was speaking. He heard it. Breath at the ear. In daylight. And he knew the saying, and he thought about it, and he knew then what it meant. It meant the Storm-reader had spoken. It meant the party was not ahead of him. It meant he was alone on the upper shelf in a storm on a closed day with an envoy's crate and an envoy's staff and a beast.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} What did he do?

What does the roll say about the Ear? It says the ground is good under the Ear. It says a party sat out a storm under the Ear on the good shelf, roped, and in the morning six were living and one was not, from the cold, sitting. So Ourn sat down under the Ear on the good shelf. He roped himself to the beast. He put the beast between himself and the wind, and he lay against its belly, and he put the envoy's cloth over both of them, and they lay there all night with the storm on the face above them. And in the morning one was living and one was living, because a beast is warm and a man by himself is not. That is the only thing in the whole telling the roll did not tell him. He had to find it out.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} And then?

The Long Lip. _Walked face in, with the rope fixed. Look at the rope where it goes over._ The fixed rope was there. It had been greased that winter by somebody at this fire, as it happens. He looked at it where it went over. It was good. He walked the Lip face in, with the beast on the short rope behind him, the length of it, over the deepest of the gorge, with nothing else, and he did not look down, because the roll does not tell you to look down. And at the end of the Lip there was the ushtakas' wall, and the fire, and the summit.

Over.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} He crossed.

Wait.

The ushtakas came out of the wall when they saw him, because nobody comes over the Long Lip the morning after a storm on a closed day, and they looked at him, and at the beast, and at the crate with its cord bitten through, and at the silver staff he was still carrying. And they asked him where his party was. And Ourn said, "Behind me," because by then he knew it was. And they gave him the road-rite, because they give it to anyone who comes to the summit, and he took it, and then the register-writer went and got the book and wrote him in it. You know the ushtakas keep a book at the wall, of everyone who comes over, with how many beasts and in whose employ. And the register-writer said what he was writing, out loud, in the lowland tongue, slowly, which is how the telling knows it. _One Drover. One beast. One crate. No party._

Then Ourn did the only foolish thing in the whole telling that the roll had not told him not to do. He went on.

He thought, the envoy's crate is for the frontier officers on the far side. I have the crate. The officers are waiting. And he went past the ushtakas' wall to the far side of the col, and there, in their tent, were the frontier officers of the celestial administration, waiting, because they meet a lowland party at the col and not before. They had been waiting four days for the envoy. And over the col came a Hvarn Drover with a beast and a crate with its cord bitten through and a silver staff.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} What did they do?

They wrote him down. What else does anybody do? An officer came out of the tent with a book of his own, and a brush, and looked at Ourn, and at the beast, and at the crate, and wrote. Ourn could not read what he wrote. Nobody at this fire has ever seen that book, and nobody in the lowland has either. But the telling says the officer said it aloud too, in his own tongue, which Ourn did not have, and that it sounded like the register-writer had sounded, about the same length, with the same stop at the end. The Hvurnash fire has always said it was the same four things. _One Drover. One beast. One crate. No party._

And then they waited. The officer would not take the crate without the envoy. Ourn would not leave it without being told to. They sat at the col, the officer in his tent and Ourn outside it with the beast, for three days, and the officer sent out tea, and Ourn sent in barley, and neither of them could say a word to the other. And on the fourth day Tsevrel came over the summit with the party on one rope, the envoy, the servants, the two Roadwalkers, the beasts, everyone, with the storm gone and the sky clear. And she came down to the col and she saw Ourn sitting there with the beast and the crate, and the officer came out of his tent with his book and his brush and began to write the party in.

And Tsevrel looked at Ourn for a long time. And Ourn waited for her to say it. That one.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} What did she say?

She said, "Give the envoy his staff."

That is all she said. Ourn gave the envoy his staff. The envoy took his crate and looked at the cord. The officer wrote everything. Tsevrel took her party on down toward the throat, as the turn said, and when she came back to the col on her way home she put Ourn on her rope and brought him down the upper shelf with the Roadwalkers, the Long Lip, the Ear, the Two Teeth, the Knee, the Butter Stone, the Wet Step, to the grass, and he walked every place on her rope as one of the party, and she did not say one word to him about any of it the whole way down.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} And at the council?

Now you have it. At the council. Hands to the butter.

That winter, at the council, after the turn and the debts, the Winter-speaker asked if there was any other matter on the floor. And Ourn stood up. He was a Drover and he stood up at the seam, which a Drover may do, if he wants to be looked at by the whole house, and he did. And he said he had a crossing to be counted. He said he had walked the upper shelf of the scholars' door from the grass to the summit and over, and he wanted it said on the floor that he had crossed, so that this hearth might be owed something, or so that he might be a Roadwalker sooner than the house thought, or so that—the telling does not say what he wanted. It says he wanted it counted.

And the house went very quiet, the way it does when it is about to enjoy something.

The Turn-keeper asked first, because it is his question. "Who gave you the road?" And Ourn said, "Nobody." And the Turn-keeper said, "That is so."

The Storm-reader asked, "Was it my weather?" And Ourn said, "You said _Not today._" And the Storm-reader said, "I did," and sat down, and that was the end of the Storm-reader's part in it.

Then the Winter-speaker said, "Who was on the rope?"

And Ourn said, "I was. And the beast."

And then the Winter-speaker asked it. In our tongue, in the party's form, the way you ask whether a party made a crossing. _Did you cross?_ As one of a party.

And Ourn opened his mouth to say yes, and he could not say it, because in the party's form it is not true, there was no party, there was Ourn and a beast. And he said, in his own form, "I crossed." By himself. "I crossed."

And the Winter-speaker said, "That is so. You crossed, yourself." And then he said the thing that is kept, the thing the other hearths say at us whenever one of us does anything by himself, which is often:

"You crossed, and you did not cross."

And the whole house laughed. Every fire, both ends. This fire too. Ourn's own mother laughed. The telling says that Ourn laughed too, after a time, and the Hvurnash fire has always said so, and the other fires say he did not, and they were not there either.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} Was he owed anything?

He was owed his place at a fire. A Drover is owed his place at a fire. The fee came to the Dzelbern hearth, because it was the Dzelbern hearth's road and Tsevrel's party, and the party got over. The envoy's crate got over. Nothing was lost but a piece of the second stone, which the roll had already said was bad. And the council said nothing more, because there was nothing to weigh. Nobody was lost. Nobody is lost, there is no account. You know that.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} And the crate?

The cord on the crate was this hearth's debt to the Dzelbern hearth, and said aloud, and paid in butter, which is why you are churning it.

And that is where the saying comes from. _The Drover kept the beasts. The Drover did not cross._ The Hvurnash fire says it was first said at Ourn.

[The woman at the next fire:]{.speaker} The Tsedvern hearth says the saying is older than Ourn.

The Tsedvern hearth says the saying is older than Ourn, and it is. It is as old as the rope. But it was said at him, louder than it was ever said at anyone, and it has been said at this hearth ever since. Let them have it older. We have it louder.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} What happened to him?

He grew up. He was a Roadwalker, in someone else's charge, and then a Reach-guide, and he walked the scholars' door for twenty summers as the turn gave it to this hearth, and he never lost a party, and he never went up a day early, and he was the slowest guide on the upper shelf in living memory, and the lowland men complained of him every summer, and he let them. At the Knee he called, and he waited for the answer, and he did not answer himself.

And he died old, in this house, sitting up, in the winter, of nothing much. So he is not in the roll. A person who dies in the house is not in the roll; the roll is for the ground. So nobody is named for him. So nobody stands up for him at the recitation, and nobody answers for him with a place.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} Then he is nowhere.

He is in two books. That is where Ourn is. In the ushtakas' book at the summit wall, and in the frontier officers' book at the col, in two hands and two tongues, neither of which he could read, nor can you, nor can I. _One Drover. One beast. One crate. No party._ The only one of us written down, and written down twice, and the floor says he did not cross.

[The Drover:]{.speaker} And the beast?

The beast. Yes. Nobody has ever said on the floor that the beast did not cross. A beast is not the party, so nobody asks it. The beast crossed. The telling says so.

That is told. Is that butter? That is not butter yet. Hands to it.
