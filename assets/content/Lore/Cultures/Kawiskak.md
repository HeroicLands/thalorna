In midwinter a Spruce People band moves in single file on snowshoes, and the person at the front of the line works harder than everyone behind. Each step sinks into soft snow, and the snow has to be stamped down so that the toboggans and the old people can follow. The one who walks first is called the trail-breaker, and that is the whole of what leadership means in the northern forest: going first through the deep snow, and stepping aside when someone else can do it better. The people call themselves the Kawiskak, the people of the spruce, because the spruce stays green and standing through the winter, and they hold that a person should too. About 60,000 of them live in the [[place-kawiskakrgn|Kawiskak Region]], the forest between the northern grass and the treeline, in bands that meet only in summer. The south has never heard of them.

## The Bands and the Lakes

A winter band is two to five families, usually a set of brothers and sisters with their husbands, wives, children and old parents: twenty or thirty people in all. It winters on its own ground (_tapihk_), a stretch of forest, lakes and streams that the band has hunted for as long as its old people remember. A band knows every beaver lodge on its ground and how many beaver it can take from each without emptying it. The band holds it by custom, and another band that wants to hunt there asks first. A ground that no band has wintered on for a few years is open to any band that moves onto it.

When the ice goes out, the winter bands come down the rivers to their summer lake, where the fish run and the wind keeps the flies off the shore. A summer lake gathers anything from a hundred people to several hundred, and in a good sturgeon year the [Okiskosipa]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, the Fish Lake, holds two thousand. Summer is for fishing, marriages, dances, settling quarrels between bands and trading, and for hearing the news of the whole forest. When the geese go south, the bands scatter to their winter grounds again.

## Who Leads

Nobody gives orders in a band. The families talk until they agree, by the fire in the evening, and a family that will not agree packs its toboggan and joins another band. The trail-breaker is whoever the band follows, usually its best hunter: he chooses where the band moves, when it moves and where it sets its nets, and the band goes with him for as long as his choices feed it. A trail-breaker who leads his band into hunger finds it smaller each winter.

At a summer lake the trail-breakers of all the bands sit together, and the old women sit with them. The lake keeper is the eldest woman of the families that built the fish weirs at the lake's outlet, and she opens the fishing each spring. No band takes fish from the weirs before she does, and in a bad year she decides how the catch is shared. Disputes between bands go to the lake: a family that has trapped another band's beaver without asking pays in furs, and a family that refuses to pay is not welcome at the weirs.

## The Animals Give Themselves

**Good is the shared kill and the honored animal. Evil is food kept back from the hungry, and an animal shamed.** The Spruce People hold that game is not caught. An animal gives itself to a hunter who treats it with respect, and its spirit goes back to its own kind and tells them how it was treated. A hunter who wastes meat, lets dogs chew the bones or boasts of a kill will find the animals stop coming to him, and the band that shelters him starves with him.

The respect shows in an ordinary day. Fish bones go back into the water, beaver bones into the stream, and the bones of a caribou are tied up in a tree out of the dogs' reach. A bear killed in its den is spoken to before it is killed and feasted afterward, and its skull is painted and set high in a spruce to watch the forest. The first meat of a hunt goes to the old people and the widows of the band before the hunter's own family eats. The worst thing a person can do, worse than killing one of the people, is to become _mohkisk_, the starving spirit, and eat human flesh.

Success is to be followed and to be fed. A hunter is admired when other families choose to winter with him, and a woman is admired when her nets, her snares and her stores keep a band alive through a hare winter. Failure is to be left: a family nobody will winter with lives alone, and alone in the forest is the nearest thing to death.

## The Shaking Lodge

When a band needs to know something no one can see, such as where the caribou are, whether a band three lakes away is alive or why a child is sick, a lodge-shaker builds a shaking lodge (_tisoman_): a narrow tower of poles wrapped in hides, as tall as two people. The lodge-shaker goes in alone at dusk, and the band sits outside. The lodge begins to sway and shake, voices speak from inside it, and the spirits answer the band's questions through the lodge-shaker's mouth.

The lodge-shaker has no authority beyond the answers. A trail-breaker may move against what the spirits said, and the band may follow him. A lodge-shaker is paid in tobacco, a pelt or a share of the next kill, and one whose answers keep failing is not asked again.

## The Man Admired

[Kitoskapo]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Wolf Runs, is the trail-breaker of the Kopiskak, the Beaver band, and the forest bands name him when they say what a man should be. He has led his band through eleven winters and has never lost one of its people to hunger, because he moves early. When the hares begin to fail he takes the band to the fish before anyone is weak, and he does not wait for the caribou he hopes will come. Two winters ago he gave half his ground for a winter to a band of the [Barren-Ground People]{.name data-type="lore/culture"} that came south through the trees with nothing, and showed their hunters where his beaver lodges were. His band trapped less that winter, said so, and stayed with him.

The bands admire his judgment and his open ground. His cost is his beaver, which have not come back, and the anger of the bands on either side of him, who say he has taught strangers the way into everyone's forest.

## The Woman Admired

[Osipisa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Watches the Lake, is the lake keeper at the [Apowosipa]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, the Loon Lake, the northernmost summer lake of the forest, a few days below the treeline. She opens the weirs each spring, settles the quarrels of the eight bands who meet there, and holds more of the people's stories than anyone alive. She is admired for her weirs and her memory. Her weirs are the best-kept in the north, and her memory of every band's ground and of every debt between bands keeps the peace at the lake.

Last winter a family of Barren-Ground People came to the frozen lake starving. A lodge-shaker of another band said they were _mohkisk_, and some of the young men wanted to drive them back onto the barrens. Osipisa stood on the ice in front of them and told the story of the [Loon Lake Killing]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, below, from beginning to end. Then she fed the strangers from her own stores. They are still at the lake. Half the bands that meet there have not forgiven her.

## The Dead

The dead go north. They walk out of the forest past the treeline, onto the barren grounds, to the land where the caribou go each summer, and they live there among the herds without hunger or cold. The northern lights are the dead dancing.

That belief governs a burial. The dead are buried in their best clothes with their snowshoes, their knife and food for the walk, under a small roofed house of logs, and their name is not spoken until the next summer gathering, so that the call of it does not turn them back. A dead person's goods that are not buried with them are given away at the lake. A band leaves a place where someone has died and does not winter there again for years.

It also means that the land beyond the trees is the edge of the dead's country. The Barren-Ground People live there, and the Spruce People have always found them uncanny: people who walk where the dead walk, live without wood and come out of the north in winter like the dead coming back. The living reach the dead through the shaking lodge, and the dead reach the living in dreams and in the northern lights. A dead person who is not given food and goods for the walk comes back hungry, and the people fear a hungry dead person more than any living enemy.

## What a Person Owes

A person owes the band a share of every kill, the old and the widows first; respect to every animal killed; help to anyone who is hungry; and silence about the dead until the summer gathering. A family owes the band that shares a winter ground its customary limits, and asks before it hunts on another band's ground.

In return a person is owed a share of whatever the band has. A widow and her children stay with her band and eat from the first share of every kill, and an orphan is raised by the mother's sisters as their own child. One rule overrides every custom of the ground. A starving family may kill anything, anywhere, on any band's ground, and must tell the band whose ground it was when they next meet. To refuse food to a hungry person is the act closest to becoming _mohkisk_.

The hard case is the old person who cannot keep up on the winter trail. Most bands pull their old people on the toboggans. In a starving winter an old man or woman sometimes asks to be left behind with a fire and some food so that the band can move faster, and the band honors the request. The old people sing about it, and the young people do not talk about it.

## The Compact Peoples

The Spruce People trade with the compact nations at the edges of the forest and nowhere else. The northern bands of the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Grass People]] come to the Fish Lake each summer with bison fat, pounded meat and tobacco, and go home with furs and birch-bark canoes. The Pine People of the [[affiliation-osetakona|Maple People]] trade maple sugar, corn and flint for furs and the copper of the barren grounds at their northern villages. The forest bands like the goods and distrust the people. To a Spruce band a nation that lives in one place all year is a nation that must always want more ground, and every winter some of the Pine People's hunters are found on a Spruce band's ground without having asked.

No Spruce band holds a red pipe of the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]], and the compact nations count them as strangers. A Spruce trader who goes south past the Fish Lake does it with a band of the Grass People that he has married into or traded with for years, and he is that band's guest and no one else's.

## The Killing at Loon Lake

The sin the Spruce People committed is the Loon Lake Killing. It happened in a winter four generations ago, after the hares had failed and the caribou had stayed out on the barrens. A party of Barren-Ground People, about thirty men, women and children, came south through the trees to the Loon Lake, where two Spruce bands were wintering. They had come before, every summer, to trade copper at the treeline. This time they came in midwinter and starving.

[Wanoskomin]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Snow Sings, the lodge-shaker of one of the bands, built a shaking lodge, and the voices in it said that the strangers were _mohkisk_: the starving spirit walking out of the dead's country in human bodies, to eat the band. The men of both bands killed the strangers on the ice. They burned the bodies so the spirit could not come back, and they kept the strangers' copper. In spring the hares returned, and the Barren-Ground bands came south to the treeline to trade and found nobody to trade with. They did not bring copper again for a generation.

Every Spruce band knows the story, and every band now holds two rules because of it. No lodge-shaker's word can name a person _mohkisk_ on its own; the whole lake must hear the case in summer. And the copper trade at the treeline opens each year with a gift of food from the Spruce People to the Barren-Ground People, before anything is traded.

The blind spot the Spruce People argue over is the _mohkisk_ itself. Every band believes in it, and most of the old people have a story of a person who turned in a starving winter and had to be killed before they killed the band. The argument is over who can be named. Some trail-breakers say that the starving spirit comes out of hunger, and that a band that feeds the hungry stranger will never meet it. Others answer that Loon Lake was a mistake about one party of people and not about the spirit, and that a band too ashamed of an old killing to see the _mohkisk_ in front of it will be eaten. The question is a live one: the Barren-Ground People are coming south through the trees again, hungry, pushed off their land by the kin of the [[affiliation-tikupilarit|Ice-Edge People]], and each band that meets them decides alone what they are.
