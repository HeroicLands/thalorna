"You will see a hall cut a hole in its own wall this week," a gydja of [[lore-solrundty|Sólrún]] tells a newcomer who has come north in the season of a jarl's death, "and you will want to ask why. Ask me, not the widow." A death in a [[place-nrdlndsrgn|Nordlands]] hall starts a sequence of work that runs for seven nights and ends with a cup, and every part of it answers one of two questions: where the dead are going, and what they leave behind.

## The First Night

The nearest kin close the eyes and the mouth, wash the body and dress it in its best. The body is not carried out through the door the living use. The household opens a gap in the wall—a few planks lifted out, a section of turf cut away—carries the dead out through it, and closes it again behind them. The teaching is that the door of a hall is the door between birth and death, the one never barred, and the dead are given a way out that leads nowhere back. Restless dead are feared in the north, and a household that took its dead out by the front door and then heard knocking at it in winter would know whom to blame.

From that night the dead person's obligations are counted rather than cancelled. The kin reckon what was owed to the dead and what the dead owed, and nobody takes the dead person's seat until the reckoning is spoken at the grave-ale.

## The Pyre

The _eldsdulm_ (pyre), the fire's grave, is the common rite for the free: the bóndi and his household, the craftsman, the drengr who died at home. The body is laid on a pyre of split wood with what it will need at the table of the gods—a man's weapons or tools, a woman's keys and spindle, food for the road—and burned in daylight, where the smoke can be seen from the neighboring steads. The skalds hold that a high, straight smoke is a welcome and a low one a sign the dead had something left unsettled; the kin do not argue the point at the pyre.

What the fire leaves is gathered into a _gnúm_ (clay pot), set in the ground and marked. A household with standing raises a howe over it; a poorer one sets stones around it in the outline of a ship, so that the dead have a vessel to make the crossing in.

## The Ship

The _skipsdulm_ (ship-burial) belongs to those whose life was a ship or a hall that kept ships: a king, a jarl, a Skipstjóri who answered for a vessel in the levy, and a hersvald whose district gives a ship for him. The dead are laid in a ship with their weapons, their horse and dogs, their bed and their cooking-gear, because a jarl keeps hall among the gods on the same terms he kept it here. Two practices stand side by side, and the coasts argue over which is older. Inland and in the royal seats the ship is dragged ashore and buried under a howe whole. Along the outer coasts, a sea-captain's ship is set alight in the surf and pushed off on the ebb, and his kin watch it until it sinks or the light fails.

## The Howe

The _hrúm_ (howe) is the barrow raised over a grave, and it is a claim as well as a grave. A line that holds _odal_ (inherited land) proves it at the ting by its howes: the mounds of the people who held the ground before, standing on it. A howe is therefore raised over anyone who held land and over most who held standing, whether what lies under it is a pot of ash or a whole ship. Taking anything from a howe is theft from the dead and the line together, and travelers leave grave-goods where they lie. Some howes hold worse than bones: the [[lore-haugverdir|hrúmverdir]], the howe-wardens, are the reason a stranger asks the local custodians before going near one.

## Who Is Laid How

The rite follows the person's standing and the manner of their death, and the north is exact about it.

- **The honored dead.** A warrior fallen in battle, an oath-keeper who died keeping the oath, a mother who died in childbirth and a craftsman who fell defending his work are burned or buried with a warrior's honors, the mother and the craftsman as much as the warrior, because the halls of [[place-valsal|Valsal]] and [[place-solvangr|Sólvangr]] take all four.
- **The free.** The pyre, the clay pot and a stone setting or a howe, as the household can afford.
- **The great.** The ship and the howe over it, or the ship set burning on the water.
- **A thrall.** Buried in the ground without a pyre, near the hall he served; a master who honors a good thrall puts a tool in the grave with him.
- **A níding.** No rite at all. A man outlawed at the ting is laid at the tide-line, between the land and the sea, where no ground claims him and no line can raise a howe for him.
- **The drowned and the lost.** Where the sea keeps the body, the kin raise a carved stone on the headland with the name cut in runes, and hold the grave-ale over the stone. A drowned man nobody raises a stone for rides with the [[lore-jol|Jól-Ride]].

## The Grave-Ale

On the seventh night the kin hold the _hrúmsminni_ (grave-ale), and the dead person's affairs end with it. The household brews for it from the day of the death. The guests gather at the howe or the stone and then in the hall, and the heir does not sit in the _höfudsveld_ (high seat) until the cup has gone round.

The rite runs in a fixed order. A skald or the eldest of the kin gives the praise of the dead, and it turns on how the dead met what came, never on what they won; a _hnúra_ (lament) follows if the household has a skald to make one. The reckoning begun on the first night is spoken aloud: what the dead owed, what was owed to them, and who now answers for each. Then the heir takes the cup, drinks it standing at the foot of the high seat, swears before the guests the oath he means to keep as the hall's head, and steps up into the seat. A hall that drinks the grave-ale has an heir; a hall that has not drunk it has an empty chair and a dead man still in it.

The gydja of Sólrún tells newcomers why the grave-ale cannot be skipped. The dead who never receive it are the riders of the Jól-Ride, and the custom is held to start with the first grave there ever was, when **Dvarnrinna** drank to **Hrandmýl** at the first howe. That account is told in [[lore-gnumborinn|Gnúmborinn]].
