# terran_analog: "The British Isles joined into one inverted-V island (England-analog kingdoms in the south and east, Ireland-analog Peshtar country in the west, a Norse-settled Scottish-analog northeast). Departs: a single landmass of roughly seven hundred thousand people, strong fae places, Grukar in the empty third, a western republic imitating the Vylarian constitution from hearsay, and a Synod of mages governing Dúnavarre."

Seven hundred thousand people on one island conduct their affairs with the seriousness of continental powers. The great kingdoms' rivalries are followed as great politics; a family's place in the order of its realm is weighed, argued and remembered. A single province of the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian Empire]] holds many times the island's people, but most people of Aelwythan culture have no functional idea of that scale. Those told tend to take it for a traveler's exaggeration.

The manorial households of the [[affiliation-kngdmldrth|Kingdom of Aldorath]], the mage-ruled courts of the [[affiliation-kingdmdnvr|Kingdom of Dúnavarre]] and the citizen soldiery of the [[affiliation-repblcndrth|Republic of Ondareth]] make different demands. The [[affiliation-jrldmstrmvld|Jarldom of Stormveld]] is ruled by the [[lore-nordheimnclt|Nordheimn]], and its thralls from [[lore-vardain|The Vardain]] are not simply another Aelwythan estate. The western Peshtar clans are descended from the [[lore-pelwarpepl|Pelwar People]] like nearly every human on the island, and so Aelwythans by descent, but they live by clan law and herd rather than by realm, and stand apart from the realms' common life by that way of living.

## Good Name, Bad Name

An Aelwythan's first measure is whether they keep faith with those who depend on them: household, lord or court, realm. **Good is service that earns trust; evil is taking power for yourself or abandoning the people who rely on you.** Success is being trusted with another person's welfare; failure is taking protection and standing while refusing your duty. A broken oath, cruelty to a dependent or an office sold for private gain stains more than the actor; kin and clients may bear the shame too.

What the island values least is standing that rests on nothing but money or a name. [[place-ashford|Ashford]]'s wool factors are prosperous and unlanded, and in a kingdom where standing is land that leaves them of no account. Ondareth's rule that office follows service in the levy is broken by the [Senate]{.name data-type="affiliation/governmental"}'s own bought seats. Aldorath's barons say their families are older than the crown, which is true, and the claim binds them: a house that boasts of its age and fails its tenants has misread what the age is for. Land, a holding kept in good order, a craft done well and a word kept are the currency; wealth counts as far as it feeds a household and equips a retinue.

The island's blind spot is the narrowness of that circle. A household may condemn the injustice of a rival lord and overlook its own; Ondareth's citizens praise civic duty while accepting magistracies and senate seats bought by the wealthy. The conquest of [[place-stormplain|The Stormplain]] is the larger shame: Aelwythans call its loss an outrage, but the realms have never agreed to reclaim it together, and the Vardain laboring there remain outside their protection.

The sin the island committed itself sits beside that shame. The [[affiliation-dunavarsynod|Dúnavarre Synod]] declared the [[affiliation-saelvri|Saelvri]] a danger to the island and sent the [[affiliation-justbladeordr|Order of the Just Blade]], whose Knight-Captains drove them valley by valley into their oldest wood and killed the last of them, several thousand with their children, in three days ([[lore-saelvricrusade|The Saelvri Crusade]]). Dúnavarre's registers name the Saelvri an enemy and say no more, and the other realms that call Stormveld's enslavement an outrage do not ask what happened in [[place-hushwood|The Hushwood]]. Harm done to one's own is a scandal; harm done by one's own is a matter for the register. Some knights of the Order hold the crusade contrary to the god they serve, and most have not spoken of it.

What earns respect varies with a person's station, but service and competence travel farther than titles. [[being-lefrcbrnwl|Leofred Barnwell]], a male physician, is known for learning and calm care, and often treats poor patients without charge. [[being-hawiseshby|Hawise Ashby]], a veteran woman among the huscarls, is admired for loyalty, tactical experience and training younger fighters. Their work is not interchangeable: he earns trust at a sickbed; she earns it beside comrades and in the training yard. Both are respected because skill becomes service to others, and both carry the ideal's cost: he is absorbed by his work and weighed down by the losses of his calling, and she is hard on the young warriors she wants ready to stand beside their comrades.

## No Island Charter

An Aelwythan can point to no island charter. A baron's inherited claim, a Synod writ, a citizen's place on Ondareth's roll and the oath recorded at the year-turn of the [[affiliation-ardavon|Faith of Árdavon]] each bind people, but each carries weight in its own realm or temple. The standing stones and trackways of the wilderness mark older paths; the trackstones predate even the Peshtar and make no common law.

Each realm keeps its own founding object. Aldorath's barons keep a record in which the crown is their gift to a war-leader, and the chapel, hall and royal graves at [[place-kingsholow|Kingshollow]] hold the line of kings that gift began. Ondareth keeps the broken rock above [[place-thornbury|Thornbury]] where the last king's castle stood, and the Senate's bordered mantles come from a description of a republic nobody on the island has seen. Dúnavarre keeps the empty chair of its last king at [[place-ravenmoor|Ravenmoor]], shown to every petitioner on the way in. The [Crown Roads]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, fitted-stone causeways laid across the interior by an older kingdom, carry no law at all; the realms stand on land that came to humans because the elder races stopped, and none of their charters says so.

The Synod of [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Thordimalon]] (the Arcane Order) mages is the only island institution with reliable knowledge of [[place-aelwyth|Aelwyth]]'s place in the wider world. It governs Dúnavarre, advises every realm, and can withdraw those advisers. It keeps records of magical cargo inspected at Ravenmoor, but has not corrected its neighbors' inflated ideas of their own importance. Its schools and libraries make learning a source of standing in Dúnavarre; its secrecy and control also make it feared.

Almost nobody leaves the island. The mainland is a long, dangerous sail, and much of what arrives is second-hand and years old. Dúnavarre requires ships between Aelwyth and the mainland to call at Ravenmoor in both directions, where Arcane Order officers search for prohibited goods and record, examine or detain magical cargo. The Synod is not merely an observer of outside knowledge; it controls the island's narrowest gate to it.

The land itself resists any neat island-wide order. Fae are strong in places in the deep interior forest and wilder north; realm borders bend around them, and people who live nearby organize their lives with those powers in mind. The [[lore-grukarfolk|Grukar Folk]] have endured in the island's ungoverned third for centuries and cannot be driven out. An Aelwythan may be badly informed about distant politics and still know which crossroads to avoid after dark.

The conquered north-east makes a different mark on the island's memory. Nordmen took Stormveld's plain sixty years ago and work it with Vardain thralls. The other realms call the loss an outrage, but have never agreed who should lead a recovery or who would hold the land afterward. Aelwythan loyalty to the island can be real and still fail when it asks rival rulers to act together.

## A Faith at Every Door

The [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]] is strongest in the southern and eastern kingdoms. Lay faithful of its twelve distinct faiths keep their feasts without holding temple office. Ordinary devotion means the small repeated acts as well as a visit to the temple: tending a household shrine, making an offering, or seeking a priest's counsel. Árdavon's oath-bound justice has particular weight in Dúnavarre. In Ondareth, [[affiliation-blackflame|The Black Flame]] prospers where officials can be bought, while the [[affiliation-selmoros|Faith of Sélmoros]] draws people who want a judge beyond the reach of money.

At a crossroads or beside a lake, a person may leave an offering for its spirit and still attend the temple the same week. Ancestors and place-spirits are part of the island's living observance, not a rival church with a common priesthood. In the north-east, the Nordmen keep the gods of their own [[affiliation-asguardian|Asguardian Pantheon]]; their conquest has not made those faiths the island's common ones.

At the year-turn [Feast of Thresholds]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, Árdavon's faithful renew covenants and oaths in public and have them recorded. At the late-summer [Festival of the Golden Harvest]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, successful traders give a portion of their gain. During Sélmoros's three-day [Festival of the Silent Passage]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} in late autumn, households set black candles in their windows and speak the names of the dead while priests perform requested rites for the year's departed. A household may keep several observances; the calendar follows its gods and region.

Each realm adds days of its own, and each is for something. Aldorath's court keeps the midwinter feast at Kingshollow beside the royal graves, which reminds the barons whose gift the crown is. The autumn drove fair at [[place-ashford|Ashford]] is the one occasion on which a serf may lawfully be far from his manor, and the manorial country brings its wool, cattle and hides to it. Once a year Ondareth's Senate climbs the tyrant's rock above Thornbury in its mantles, in order of precedence, and stands for an oration among the foundation stones; the Black Flame's shrines send a delegation and Sélmoros's clergy, who take no civic office, stay away. In Stormveld the Nordmen keep their hall feasts and their moot, and the thralls keep their own observances out of sight and, mostly, out of hearing. In the [[place-pshtrwldrns|Peshtar Wilderness]] the druids' gatherings at the stone circles at each seasonal turn are the nearest thing the clans have to a shared calendar; disputes are heard there and alliances sworn, and a clan that rejects a ruling simply goes home.

What a person can earn or lose changes at a border. Service may win trust, an office, a household place or a hearing before a local court, and a long record of it may win a lord's patronage or a place in a retinue. Dúnavarre can interdict someone from its courts and markets; Ondareth can strike a citizen from the roll and outlaw them. On the island, the last punishment may be losing the standing and protection others once extended. No sentence binds every realm.

The order of severity is nonetheless recognizable. Reproof and the loss of a lord's or patron's favor come first and cost the most in a country that runs on favor. A fine, the loss of a tenancy or a place in a retinue follows, then interdiction or removal from the roll, then outlawry. A broken oath sworn before Árdavon's priests is the one offense that follows a person across the border, because the faith treats it as an offense against the god and the bloodline, and the faiths' ultimate sentence of being cast out denies a person the rites, the temple and the burial, which is the only penalty that outlives the body.

## Courts, Temples and the Dead

In Aldorath, the king and barons hold a feudal order whose families claim lands through generations; seers advise on prophecy. The Dúnavarren Synod writes law, administers justice and commands the army, so mage and ruler are the same institution. Ondareth's senate and magistrates govern civic life, though the priesthood of Sélmoros keeps death records, performs funerals and counsels the dying. Parties who want a dispute heard without buying a court may turn to its priests. They give final rites to paupers and unclaimed dead without payment. Stormveld settles matters at the jarls' moots under the Nordmen's own law, where a free bundi speaks and a thrall holds no voice. In the Peshtar country the druid holds ritual, inheritance and dispute and the war-chief the clan's arms; the two balance each other, and a clan whose pair quarrel splits. Where the offices overlap, the answer depends on the realm: in Aldorath the barons' court and the seers, in Dúnavarre the Synod, in Ondareth the Senate and the Sélmoran priests who keep the death roll, and in the Peshtar country whichever of druid and war-chief the clan will follow.

For a household devoted to Sélmoros, death is a passage no offering can bargain away. The dying may confess and give final testimony to a priest; nobles consult the priesthood on succession, and the dead's names are spoken again at the autumn observance. A person with a debt or inheritance to settle has reason to speak while still alive. The faith forbids violating graves or raising the dead. Other households honor ancestral and place spirits at shrines, stones and lakes; an Aelwythan's funeral customs tell you which tradition they follow.

The belief reaches an ordinary day through a few habits. A debt, a will or a promise left unsettled at death is the dying person's to answer for, so people with property settle it early, nobles ask the priesthood about succession while the heir can still be named, and a household that expects a death sends for the priest before the last day and not after it. A body goes into the ground or onto the pyre with the rites of the tradition the household keeps, and an embalmer such as [[being-edgarchltn|Hildmund Chilton]] treats the work as a last service whose quality the family remembers. The names of the year's dead are spoken at the autumn observance, so a household counts its dead each autumn. Walking home by a trackway at dusk, a farmer leaves a handful at the crossroads or the lake's margin, avoids the place after dark, and sees nothing odd in a prayer at the temple the same week. Graves and the old standing stones are left where they lie.

The living can honor the dead with prayers, names and rites. Sélmoros's priests receive payment for funerals and tomb care, not for answers from the dead; they also counsel the bereaved and bury paupers and unclaimed dead without fee. At a place-spirit's stone or a household shrine, the living leave what custom requires. Whether the dead answer there is not something the customs promise.

## The Years of a Life

A child learns the work of the household before anyone names a place for them, and their first claim is on the household that feeds them. When they can carry a share of the work the learning turns into a placement, and the placement is the young person's first adult tie: an apprenticeship to a craft master, a place in a lord's retinue or among the huscarls, a post in Dúnavarre's Synod schools or the Order of the Just Blade, a term in Ondareth's levy that opens the road to office, or a full share of the family holding. The unestablished young hold the least standing and the longest list of duties.

Adulthood is a holding or a post: a strip and a household of one's own, a master's place in a trade, a retinue seat, a name on a town roll. The household's claims on its adult are heaviest here, because adults feed the children and the old. Late middle age moves the work from strength toward teaching, which is the shape [[being-hawiseshby|Hawise Ashby]] has taken at forty-four: still a huscarl, and now the one who trains the younger fighters. A physician or a craft master hands on a practice the same way. No island custom fixes a retirement age or a provision for the old, and what they are owed depends on the household, lord or guild that claims them. Some die in the holding they were born on, and others fall to the hospices and the street.

The dying are the one rung that the island's traditions treat alike: someone sits with them, a priest where the household has one, and the last testimony settles what the dying person still owes and is owed.

## What a Person Owes

At an Aldorathi manor, a household works the demesne and its own strips; four in five Aldorathi are serfs or peasants bound to the land and lord. The family holding passes through generations, and the lord's court sets the claims attached to it. In Dúnavarre, a subject is protected and taxed but has no political voice; a freeman of a chartered town adds its courts, markets and guild rights. Ondareth asks military service of its citizens, but reserves office in practice for landholders and veterans. Aelwythans owe the work and service of their station; in return they expect the household, lord, court or realm that claims them to keep order and defend its own.

Care follows people and institutions rather than a common poor law. Physician [[being-lefrcbrnwl|Leofred Barnwell]] belongs to a local physicians' guild and often treats poor patients without charge. The [[affiliation-morvana|Faith of Morvana]]'s hospices in every major city shelter and comfort the dying, funded by gifts and endowments; Sélmoros's priests give funeral rites to paupers and the unclaimed without fee. In the streets, sixty-two-year-old [[being-maudosgood|Maud Osgood]]—frail, often ill and without family—survives through charity and the informal beggar network that looks out for its members. When a poor harvest costs [[being-aldwngrylng|Aldwald Grayling]] his farm work, people he knew from the farm sometimes help, though his pride makes him reluctant to accept it. These are real hands extended, but no island-wide institution promises them to every widow, orphan, sick person or hungry household.

## When the Harvest Fails

Aldorath's grain feeds the island, so a bad year on its manors is felt from [[place-greywater|Greywater]]'s quays to the Dúnavarren markets, and the serf's strips and the lord's demesne fail together. Obligation does not make food appear. What it does is decide who is carried. A holding passes through the family and not through the individual, so an injured or sick member stays on the holding as long as the household has other hands to work it, and the loss falls on the whole household's table. A person with no household has the temple hospice for the dying, the city charity, the guild's own, a neighbor who remembers them, and the street. [[being-elfrdlwknr|Kenrith Lewknor]], who took over her family's farm when her parents died, runs it on that logic: she keeps the holding productive because the holding is what feeds the people on it, and she struggles to ask anyone for help.

The same bad year shows the island's limits. In Stormveld a thrall is fed through an owner or not at all, and the free Vardain in the valleys of [[place-ironfells|The Ironfells]] survive on the [[lore-flkkhazar|Khazár Folk]]'s protection. In the Peshtar country the clan moves its herds to the winter valleys and the druid and war-chief divide what there is. There is no common backstop once family, employer and patron can no longer help.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

When a family cannot do these things the lord, the guild or the temple does. Nothing promises an orphan or a widow a roof; a relative, a neighbor, a temple willing to shelter them or a craft master who takes an apprentice may supply one. A craft master carries a trade's teaching further than a household can, Dúnavarre's Synod keeps schools and libraries, and the [[affiliation-panepistmm|Epistémion]] (the Academy of Knowledge) chapterhouses teach the gentry's children. Defense follows a person's place in the realm: a lord and his retinue for the manorial country, the citizen levy for Ondareth, the Synod's soldiers and the Order of the Just Blade for Dúnavarre, the war-chief and the clan for the Peshtar. The frontier steadings of [[place-wyndmarch|The Wyndmarch]] and [[place-nordfells|The Nordfells]] defend themselves against the Grukar and write off the farms the forest takes as a routine loss.

## Household, Holding and Roll

The unit is the household as it lives and works, and the household's shape differs by realm. On a manor it is the family on its holding across generations, with the holding as the thing that outlives its members. In a chartered town it is the master's household, which takes in apprentices and journeymen and appears on the town roll. In a lord's retinue the hall is the household and the sworn followers are its members. In the Peshtar country the clan is the unit. Membership follows local standing: a hereditary manor holding, a chartered-town roll and a lord's retinue give different claims, and a person without any of the three is a person nobody is bound to feed.

Inheritance and marriage tie households into the realm's order. A baron's claim passes with his land and his debts, and a serf's family keeps its strips on the lord's terms. A household that tends ancestors at a shrine or a stone has a further reason to want an heir.
