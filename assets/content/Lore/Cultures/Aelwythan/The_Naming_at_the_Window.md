**The Naming at the Window** is the lament a woman of [[place-wyndmarch|the Wyndmarch]] says at her window on the first night of the [Festival of the Silent Passage]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, the three days in late autumn when [[affiliation-selmoros|Sélmoros's temples]] close to the living, households set black candles in their windows, and the living speak the names of the dead aloud so that [[lore-selmorosdty|Sélmoros]], the Silent Judge, may guide them. Most households say the names and nothing more. This lament is said by a widow, [Leofith of Ashwold]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, whose steading of [Ashwold]{.name data-type="place/site"} at the forest edge was taken by the wood in a single year, and whose dead have no graves.

Her husband [Mildwald of Ashwold]{.name data-type="being/npc"} rode into the wood with the march watch on a night the fires were seen among the trees and did not come back; nothing of him came back. His mother [Osrun of Ashwold]{.name data-type="being/npc"} would not leave the steading when the family went down, and the steading burned with her in it. Her son [Wilwin of Ashwold]{.name data-type="being/npc"} went alone to the wood's edge in the next spring to look for his father, and was not found either. The steward of the march wrote the year's losses in his book as the march writes them, a few steadings and the men of a watch, one line ([[lore-grukaraelwyth|The Grukar of Aelwyth]]). She has kept the names.

The lament is said and not sung, in a low voice, with the candle lit at the first words and left burning when she stops. It names each of the dead in turn and asks the Judge to take each by the name, because there is nothing else of them to give him. It ends with her daughter, who says the names after her. It has been taken down as she says it, and the women of the forest edge say it the same way at their own windows, with their own names in it.

```verse {form=lament lang=en}
I set the candle. Judge, here is the light.
It is the first night, and the door is shut,
And thy door shut, and all the temple dark;
Only the windows, all down the street, are lit,
And every window hath a woman in it,
And every woman hath her names to say.
Here are my names. Judge, I will say them slow.

The first is Mildwald. He was my husband.
He rode out with the watch at candle-light
Upon the night the fires were in the trees;
He took his bill down from above the door,
And said, Keep up the fire, and I kept it.
I kept it all that night, and the next night,
And all that winter, and he came not home.
Nothing came home. They found the horses' tracks,
And where the tracks went in, and none came out.
There is no body, Judge. There is no grave.
I cannot bring thee him to the gate's mouth,
Nor wash him, nor sit by him, nor give him rites.
I have his name. I give thee that. Take it.
Mildwald. Mildwald of Ashwold. Take him by it.

The second is Osrun, and she was his mother.
When we came down she would not come with us.
She said, I was a bride beneath this roof,
And I will be a corse beneath it, then;
I have outlived the man that cut the beams,
I will not now outlive the beams. Go down.
She sat down at the hearth and turned her back,
And we went down, and in the spring it burned.
We saw the smoke from here. She would not come.
Osrun. Osrun of Ashwold. Take her by it.

The third is Wilwin, and he was my son.
He was fourteen. He was his father's height.
He said, I will go up and look for him,
And I said, There is nothing there to find,
And he said, Then I'll find the nothing, mother,
And come home and tell thee where it is.
He went up to the wood's edge in the spring
Before the light, and he went in, and stayed.
I do not know that he is dead. I say it
Because the candle wanteth all the names.
Wilwin. Wilwin of Ashwold. Take him by it.

The fourth is Ashwold, and it was our house.
It is no name for thee, Judge; it never died.
Yet I will say it, for there's none else will.
Where is the ash that stood above the door?
The wood hath it. Where is the door? The wood.
Where is the hearth my mother-in-law kept?
The nettles have it, and the nettles keep it.
Where is the strip that Mildwald's father cleared,
And his before him, out of the forest's foot?
The forest's foot is on it. Where's the well?
Choked up with leaves, and something drinketh there.
Where is the bench, the board, the salt, the bed,
The pot, the hook, the cradle of my son?
The wood hath all of it, and asketh more.
Ashwold. Ashwold in the march. I name it.

Now hear what the steward hath of all of these.
He wrote, the spring we came down, in his book,
Four steadings lost at the wood's edge this year,
And of the watch, six men, and that was all.
One line. I made him read it me. One line.
He said, Good woman, it is every year,
It is the march, the book is full of them.
I know the book is full. I said the names.
He wrote them not. He hath no room for names.
I have the room. I have the window, Judge,
And the black candle, and the night, and this.

Come here, my girl. Stand by me at the sill.
Thou hast heard the names. Now say them after me:
Mildwald. Osrun. Wilwin. And the house.
Again. And slower. So. Thou hast them now.
When I am in the steward's book, a line,
Thou shalt stand here, and set the candle up,
And say them, and say mine after Wilwin's,
And give them to thy daughter at the sill.
So long as there's a window in the march
And a woman in it, they are not in the wood.
The wood hath all the rest. It hath not these.

The candle burneth. Judge, I have said them all.
Take them by the names, for I have nothing else.
I will not shut the window till 'tis out.
```
