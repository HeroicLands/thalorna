There is no Nyáluba people in the way a foreigner means it. There are five nations that share a language, a faith and a pact, and that differ in dress, dialect, ritual, marriage custom and self-conception far enough that a [Ruwozi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} cattle-herder and a [Kolembozi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} reed-fisher are never taken for the same kind of person. What both would say at once is that they are heirs of the Long Pact (see [[affiliation-nylbtrblntn|Nyáluba Tribal Nation]]). The Pact is the culture; everything else is clan.

## The Pact, and What It Forbids

Nine generations ago, under a great baobab in Ruwozi country, five mutually hostile clans agreed to stop being picked apart one at a time. The catastrophe that drove them to it has been polished smooth by retelling, though the [Lobwezi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} griots hold that it was a militarist confederacy out of the central savannahs that took cattle, women and lives without distinction, and is now extinct.

The terms are deliberately few, and a Nyáluban child can recite them: the five clans will not raise arms against each other, and the old cattle-feuds are forsworn; the five will defend any one of the five; the five will share the great pasturelands, the rivers and the rains, each observing the others' ritual obligations to the spirits of those places; and the five will renew the Pact in person, every fourth dry season.

What the Pact does not do is make a state. There is no Nyáluban king, no capital, no standing army and no central religious authority—only a covenant renewed face to face by people who could in principle decline. That none of them ever has is both the whole strength of the confederation and the whole of its fragility.

The lion cult's memory-song, [[lore-swearbaobab|The Swearing Under the Baobab]], ends its account of the Pact on that point:

## Five Nations, One Grammar

The Ruwozi are the lion-clan and the most numerous: cattle-herders of the central plains, first among the clans because the Pact was sworn in their country. Their paramount convenes the [Great Lubemo]{.name data-type="affiliation/governmental"} and bears the duty of speaking last when consensus is sought. Men take a single lion-tooth at adulthood and women braid their lineage into their hair; their wealth is cattle, their watchword is courage, and their humor is as dry as the dry season.

The Kolembozi are the crocodile-clan, river-people of the eastern wetlands, and they hold the most elaborate shamanic tradition among the five. Their spirit-speakers are sought across the savannahs for judgment, healing and the negotiation of difficult spirits. A Kolembozi who loses a relative to a crocodile speaks of that crocodile by name and lineage, and regards the matter as a family affair.

The [Ngonzi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} are the eagle-clan: highlanders, herders and goldsmiths, and the only clan that works stone. Their relationship to the Old Hetos of the lost stone-builders is lineal, intimate and largely opaque to everyone else, including the other four clans. Ngonzi gold is the only Nyáluban export reaching the northern civilizations in quantity.

The [Sengala]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} are the leopard-clan, the northern traders and intermediaries with the rainforest peoples—the most outward-looking, the most polyglot, and the most stratified, with a class of professional traders approaching a hereditary specialty. The other clans regard that arrangement with a mixture of admiration and unease.

The Lobwezi are the baobab-clan: settled cultivators of the perennial-water districts and keepers of the senior griot tradition. They are the smallest and the ritually most senior, because the official memory of the Pact and the genealogies of all five clans are carried by Lobwezi griots. Each town centers on an ancient baobab whose custodian is the town's senior griot, and Lobwezi manner is slow and deliberate: they speak last in any council, and what they say is generally remembered.

## Memory Is a Profession

There is no writing here and no shortage of records, because there are griots—trained professionals whose accuracy is the confederation's title to its own history, holding genealogies, boundaries, the terms of the Pact, and who owes whom what and since when.

A Nyáluban therefore treats speech as an instrument with weight. What is said at a Lubemo is remembered exactly, by someone whose whole standing rests on remembering it exactly, and a man who says a careless thing in front of a griot has not made a remark. He has entered a record.

## What Is Good, and What Succeeds

**Good is the pact kept exactly: the bargain with the spirit, the oath at the Lubemo, the loan of cattle repaid, the word spoken before a griot and meant. Evil is the pact broken, and its commonest form is the thing held back, whether the cattle that stopped moving or the share a hunter did not leave.** A Nyáluban does not weigh a deed by its intent. The test is whether the account still balances, and the account is kept in memory by someone whose standing rests on remembering it right.

Success is a name the griots speak in a genealogy and a herd that circulates: cattle lent in a wet year and called back in a dry one, daughters married out with bridewealth that returns in time as grandsons, a lineage whose ancestors are remembered and whose spirits are fed. The Nyáluba call wealth that does not move dead wealth, and a clan that hoards cattle is failing in its obligations. Failure is the name a griot will not speak, a pact the clan has let lapse, and at the bottom the person cast out by his own kin and claimed by none.

The Nyáluba value most exactness of word, patience in council, courage that shows itself in the open, and reciprocity. They value least haste, the careless remark, hoarding and any decision taken for a clan without the clan's elders. A thing said quickly is entered in the record just as it was said.

## The Man Admired

[Bonzimbe of the Lobwezi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, senior griot of the Long Pact, is the Nyáluba's admired elder. He is about seventy, blind, and carries the terms of the Pact, the genealogies of all five paramounts and the recorded judgments of twenty-three Lubemos. All five clans hold him the closest living thing to a neutral arbiter. He does not rule, command or lead, and none of the five would have him do any of it. He is the man who is asked.

The Nyáluba admire in him what their whole tradition rests on: that he remembers it right, for everyone, and adds nothing. A griot who embellishes has not told it better but wrong, and Bonzimbe's long refusal to do so is what lets five clans trust one record. His cost is the cost of the office. A man whose whole standing is exactness is not free to be wrong, and he is the neutral voice the clans turn to when a dispute will not settle.

## The Woman Admired

[Lusheya nu Kolembozi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, senior spirit-speaker of the Kolembozi, is the Nyáluba's admired woman. She walked into an Old Heto at her initiation and walked out speaking [The Stone Tongue]{.name data-type="skill/language"}, the speech the Ngonzi elders say belonged to the stone-builders and no living Nyáluban understands. The griots have entered the report in the record of the Lubemo, and the Ngonzi have confirmed it. She says nothing of what the tongue is for.

The Nyáluba admire in her the same restraint the griots show with words, turned to the spirits: she stood at the border of the world the clans share with the dead, and she came back without claiming what she brought. Her admiration is not a lesser form of Bonzimbe's. He keeps what has been given, and she goes where the giving is hard. [Nzobu Ruwozi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the sixth woman to bear the title Nyoli Ruwozi, convenes the Lubemo and holds the higher rank; Lusheya holds the harder office. Her cost is that she cannot say what she knows: the Ngonzi hold one answer on whose dead the builders were and the baobab griots another, and the record holds only that the two differ.

## What the Five Keep

A Nyáluban can name the objects and words that hold the Pact in place. **The terms of the Pact** are counted on one hand, five fingers for the five. **The memory-songs**, such as the song of the swearing under the baobab, hold what each clan's cult remembers of it. **The griot's record** is the genealogies, boundaries, standing pacts and the debts between kindreds, held in one memory and recited before people who already know the answer. **The standing pacts** with the guides are the unwritten bargains each clan holds with every guide whose ground or lineage overlaps its own. **The town baobab** is the shrine of the Lobwezi, and [[place-hetolubemo|Heto Lubemo]], the ancient enclosure the Ngonzi maintain, is the ground where the Pact is renewed. A Ruwozi man's lion-tooth and a Ruwozi woman's braids record, on the body, his adulthood and her lineage.

## Venerate, Appease, Ward

The faith is a layered system of clan totems, land-spirits, water-spirits, hunt-spirits and ancestors, organized around three postures—venerate, appease, ward against—and administered by no priesthood at all. Griots keep memory, drum-speakers keep communication, spirit-speakers keep the spirits, elders keep the totemic law, and no one of them outranks the others.

Each clan keeps its primary totem—lion, crocodile, eagle, leopard, baobab—along with its subordinate totems, ancestor-shrines and land-spirit relationships. The clans recognize each other's totems as kin to their own, and inter-clan ceremonies name all five together. That mutual recognition is what makes the Pact a religious fact instead of a treaty, and it is why breaking it would be a far worse thing than breaking a treaty.

A Nyáluban finds the question whether he believes in the spirits a strange one. The spirits are neighbors, and the three postures are how a person behaves toward neighbors of that kind.

## The Postures in a Day

Most of religious life is not an event. It is the posture a person takes toward neighbors who happen to be spirits. A herd crosses the lion's ground with an offering, and a beast lost to lions on that ground is entered in the tally as a rendering and not a loss. A hunter gives the hunt-spirit its specific share and thanks it after the kill; a hunter who comes home full-handed with no offering has broken his clan's standing pact. Every household keeps a hearth-spirit, and the compound's fire is fed. The Lobwezi make offerings at the root of the town baobab, and the town's griot keeps the tally. A person at a river crossing follows the protocol of that crossing, because what keeps one person alive at one ford does not transfer to the next.

Between the household and the spirit-speaker stands the elder, who keeps the totemic law, and between the spirit-speaker and the clan stands the griot, who remembers which pact asks what.

## The Round of the Year

The year is told by the herd and the weather, and the occasions come with it. Nobody dates them by a calendar, because the Nyáluba write nothing down. They are fixed by what the land and the cattle do.

- **The turning of the cattle-seasons** is when the herds move and the lion-guide is greeted. It is for beginning the season on the right footing.
- **The cattle-circulations and the seasonal markets** are where cattle are lent, gifts are exchanged and every clan's totems are acknowledged together. They are for keeping the wealth moving and the five in company.
- **The initiations** bring each cohort through together into a shared regimental name and age-set totem. A Ruwozi man takes his lion-tooth, and young women of the Ruwozi and Kolembozi enter the regiments. They are for making a person a member.
- The Great Lubemo meets every fourth dry season at Heto Lubemo for about six weeks. Delegations camp outside the stone, the clans' decisions become commitments of the Pact, and the senior griots renew it before all. It is for the one thing the Pact requires.

## What the Guides Give and Withdraw

Reward is the Pact working. A clan that keeps its pacts prospers, a lineage that is remembered keeps its ancestors attending, a cattle gift is repaid when the giver is the guest, and a name spoken in a genealogy carries across generations.

Penalty is the same account run the other way, and it is specific and attributable: a failed hunt, a drowning at a crossing, a child born still, a fire that takes a compound. The Nyáluba do not call these superstition but consequence. The lion-guide enforces nothing on its own people; it withdraws, and the withdrawal is the sanction. A Ruwozi who hunts a lion has not committed a crime against the clan but against his own ancestry, and the griot will not speak his name in a genealogy afterward. A town whose baobab dies has lost its pact with the ground and disperses. The Sengala are forbidden to break a bargain struck under the leopard's notice, to hunt a leopard or to reveal the route by which a party came. A feud between kindreds of one clan is not forsworn, and the elders settle it in cattle with a Keeper of the Feud recording blood owed and blood paid. The worst is to be cast out by one's own kin and claimed by none, owed no hospitality, no water and no vengeance. An outsider who transgresses against a grove, a cairn or an Old Heto discovers that the guides take offense and that the clans do not intercede.

## Cattle, Consensus and the Slow Word

Wealth is cattle among the Ruwozi, gold among the Ngonzi, trade among the Sengala, grain among the Lobwezi and the river among the Kolembozi, and each clan quietly believes its own kind is the real kind.

Decision is by consensus, and consensus here is a technique with rules. A council does not vote: it talks until the objections stop, in an order fixed by custom, with the senior speaker last. The Ruwozi paramount and the Lobwezi griot therefore hold the same instrument—the right to speak into a silence everyone else has already filled—and the slowness foreigners take for indecision is the most sophisticated thing in Nyáluban public life.

Feuds between clans are forsworn. Feuds within a clan are not, and elders settle them in cattle.

## Where Civil and Spiritual Authority Meet

No priesthood and no throne stands above the clans. Religious function is spread across four offices, griot, drum-speaker, spirit-speaker and elder shaman, and a person commonly holds more than one. Where the tradition must decide something that touches all five clans, the matter goes to the Great Lubemo, which is a political body and not a religious one. The griot keeps the record and so decides, in practice, who exists. The Lawkeeper arbitrates where two tribes' customs differ, and the elders settle a feud within a clan. The Lubemo raises no levy and founds no institution beyond itself, so nobody collects and nobody tries a case about tribute. A matter the paramounts cannot converge on is held for the next Lubemo, and the Pact does not require every disagreement to be resolved.

## The Drums

The Nyáluba carry messages by drum-language, a tonal encoding of the tongue relayed through stations across all five territories, and a message crosses the whole confederation in about two days. The Great Lubemo meets once in four years; the paramounts are in near-continuous consultation.

Defense, disaster, deaths in senior lineages, ceremonial timing, market prices and herd movements all travel by drum. Northern peoples who depend on relay riders would find the speed startling if they knew of it, and the Sengala have been carefully unforthcoming with their rainforest neighbors about how the drums actually work.

## What the Brightness Asks of an Ordinary Day

The Nyáluba recognize no supreme god and would not accept that [[lore-shorinyadty|Shorinya Ndafo]], the Great Brightness, is one. All spirits came from it and all return to it, and it receives nothing: no offering, no song, no shrine. Nothing the dead are owed goes to it. The dead who matter on an ordinary day are the ancestors, and they are guides: an ancestor attends the descendants of the clan that remembers his name. The afterlife is therefore a matter of being remembered right, and the person who forgets a name or lets a griot lose a line has lost a guide for the clan.

The Lobwezi and Ngonzi griots tell how the Brightness came differently, and neither telling is ruled true. In the first it was a root, patient and under everything, and each guide is a piece of it that took the shape of the ground it fell on. In the second it was a flight across the sky from the east, the guides are the sparks it shed over the highlands, and it returns at the end of all things by the road it left. Neither clan has ever brought the difference to the Great Lubemo.

That is why an ordinary day looks as it does. A person speaks carefully, because what is said before a griot is entered. A host asks a stranger's name, his father's and where he slept, and repeats all three back, because a name once entered cannot be taken back. A man does not marry a person of his own totem, and a hunter does not return full-handed without the offering. The great fund of a lineage is its memory, and a death in a senior lineage travels by drum before the lineage has finished mourning.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the spirit-speaker and the griot. The spirit-speaker (_kwendari zohiring_) has been taken through the ordeal and trained to perceive, address and negotiate with the guides, each by its own discipline: the lion is not addressed as the crocodile is. The griot who holds a genealogy keeps the line to the ancestors alive by reciting it. The cost is the offering the pact names, in the season it names, and exactness: a pact misremembered is a pact broken. The ancestor-shrine of a lineage is where the offerings go. The dead reach the living in the same way as the guides, through consequence and through the spirit-speaker who reads it.

The stone-builder dead are another matter. They are not party to the Pact and not certainly anyone's ancestors, and the eagle-guide deals with them anyway. Entry to an Old Heto is refused to everyone but the Ngonzi, and the Ngonzi and the baobab griots hold different answers on whose dead they are.

## What a Person Owes

A Nyáluban owes in order: the lineage first, then the clan, then the Pact—and the Pact is always named aloud, because it is the thing owed across the boundary of one's own people and therefore the only one that costs anything.

Below all three he owes the spirits their proper posture, and what that asks is correctness: venerate what should be venerated, appease what must be appeased, ward against the rest, and do not confuse them.

In return a person is owed what each of those gives. The lineage gives its protection, its feud and its share. The clan gives its herds' circulation: cattle lent when he is short, to be repaid when the giver is a guest. The Pact gives defense by any of the five against a spear raised against him, and pasture, rivers and rains shared. The griot owes the record exactly right. The guides owe the prospering of a clan that keeps its pacts.

## The Weak

The household is the first shelter, and the Nyáluba grade it by closeness. A Kinsman is of the household's blood. A Dependent is sheltered without being of its blood: a widow's family, a client, a taken-in stranger. A Bondservant is held in service or debt and answers through the household's head, not in his own name. An orphan is raised in the lineage of its totem.

In scarcity the same principle that moves cattle through the year moves them through the lean one. A clan with surplus pasture in a wet year lends cattle to a clan with surplus need, a chief honors a guest knowing he will be honored in turn, and wealth that does not move is dead. The drought-war of three generations past is the measure of what a bad year does: famine pushed neighboring tribes into the Sengala country, and the regiments had to be called out.

At the bottom the Kinless are owed no hospitality, no water and no vengeance. The cost of being cast out is the loss of a name the griot will speak, and with it every claim a name carries.

## Those Who Cannot Work

The Nyáluba keep their record in memory, and a person who cannot herd is not without a place. The office of the griot is the clearest case: Bonzimbe is blind, and all five clans hold him the closest thing to a neutral arbiter. An elder who cannot walk the herd still holds the lineage's custom and settles what the young dispute, and the injured warrior of an age-set remains bound by that fellowship for life. A person who cannot meet his obligations keeps his household and his name, and the household keeps him as a Kinsman, since to turn him out would be to hold back what it owes.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household's and the lineage's, and past the lineage the clan's. Teaching is carried by the office. A griot's training is measured in decades, and the test of it is recitation before people who already know the answer. The spirit-speaker is taken through the ordeal and taught by the elder shamans. The initiate learns the cattle, the pacts and the totemic law from the elders of his lineage, and a young lineage representative attends the Lubemo to watch and learn. Defense is the age-set regiment: cohorts of young men, and young women among the Ruwozi and Kolembozi, who pass through initiation together, take a regimental name and totem and stay bound for life. A paramount calls for the regiments of a particular cohort, and within days several thousand are in the field. The drums coordinate dispersed forces, and the Pact binds all five to defend any one.

## The Line at Every Age

The household is the unit, and membership runs through the totem. Two of the same totem do not marry, and the prohibitions extend across allied totems. A marriage transfers cattle from the groom's lineage to the bride's. A child is of its clan's totem from birth and learns the three postures from the household. A young person goes through initiation with a cohort and enters a regiment. A man takes his lion-tooth among the Ruwozi; a woman braids her lineage into her hair. An adult herds, fights with the regiment, lends and borrows, and speaks in council in the order custom fixes. An elder holds the lineage's custom and settles what the young dispute. A dying person of a senior lineage is announced by the drums, and the griot enters the death in the genealogy and decides who is remembered.

## What the Pact Leaves Outside

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Nyáluban one is the edge of the five. The Pact binds five clans to each other and shares the pastures, the rivers and the rains among them, and it says nothing about anyone outside. The dry-country tribes west of the Ruwozi are not part of it and do not think themselves Nyáluban. Relations with them are mixed, with trade and intermarriage in some places and sustained low raiding in others, and the Ruwozi paramounts keep personal relations with their chieftains and no policy.

The sin is the drought-war. Three generations ago famine pushed neighboring tribes into the Sengala country, and the regiments were called out against them. The Nyáluba have not lost a war in living memory, and this is one of the wars they did not lose. The Pact's third term shares the rain with the five, and the starving were not among them. Thoughtful Nyáluba differ on whether the third term was ever meant to stop at the five. The Sengala, who trade out and marry out, have the most reason to doubt it, and the Pact's own simplicity gives the others the most reason to leave it alone. The Lubemo has never held the question.
