**The Telling of Hadushan the Kinless**, _sevvar re hadushan_, is the telling the [Mushtan]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} give on winter nights in the black tents of the northern circuit, in prose with its speeches in verse. The prose is the teller's to shape, and every teller shapes it: the length of the fight, the colour of the raiders' horses, the jokes. The verse is fixed. The four speeches are held word for word, and a teller who changes a line of them is stopped at the fire and made to say it again.

[Hadushan]{.name data-type="being/npc"} was of the [Gildash]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} until he killed his cousin [Varinash]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and then he was of nobody. A killing inside a house opens no feud, because a house cannot be at feud with itself, so the Gildash did the one thing the law left them and cast him out. The telling follows him to the tent of [Tizhva]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a Mushtan widow sheltered in the household of her husband's brother [Eskiran]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and through the moon of protection her salt gives him. Its four speeches are Hadushan's boast at the herd, Tizhva's claim, the answer of Varinash's mother [Kaminah]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and the settlement. The raiders are [[lore-sowidesclt|Sovidur]] out of the northern steppe, as raiders in a Mushtan telling generally are. The Gildash tell the same matter more briefly and end it at Kaminah's speech.

: The Telling of Hadushan the Kinless {#telling}

The bread is broken and the salt is eaten, so you may hear. Nobody hears this telling hungry. It is long, and it is about a man who was hungry, and he was fed, and it is about what that cost the ones who fed him.

Hear about Hadushan of the Gildash, who was Kinless, and who was claimed.

He was the second son of a household of the Gildash on the northern circuit, a household with forty camels and a good tent and a well-right at three waters, and he was a big man, broad in the shoulder and slow in the speech, who could lift a couched camel's hindquarters off the sand if it would not rise. He was not a poet. He was good with a spear and better with a horse. He had a bay mare with one white foot, the off fore, and she was the best thing he owned, and he will need her in this telling, so keep her in your head.

He had a cousin, Varinash, the son of his father's brother, who was a poet. Varinash could make a verse on anything, on a goat, on a pot, on a man's way of walking, and the verse would go round the camp by evening, and the man would hear his own walk in children's mouths for a year. Varinash was quick and Hadushan was slow, and they had been boys together, and they did not love each other, and nobody in the household thought that strange, because cousins are like that.

Now hear the killing. There was a marriage in the household, Hadushan's sister going to a man of the Shivash, and there were guests from three tribes in the tent, and the meat was served, and Varinash stood up to make a verse for the bride, as a poet does. And the verse was a good one, and everybody praised it, and then he made another, a small one, as a poet does at a marriage when the meat is good and the guests are laughing, and the small one was about Hadushan. It said that Hadushan's word was like his walk: it got there in the end, if you waited. It was a joke. The guests laughed. Hadushan did not laugh. He had given his word to a man of the Shivash about a camel, that day, and kept it, and the man was in the tent.

And Hadushan stood up and said: you have called me a liar in front of a guest. And Varinash said: I have called you slow. And Hadushan said: you have said my word is not good until it is late. That is a liar. And Varinash laughed, and turned to the bride's people, and said something else, and nobody has kept what it was. And Hadushan picked up the mallet that the tent-pegs had been driven with, which was lying by the door-pole, and struck him once on the side of the head, and Varinash fell down in the meat and did not get up.

That is the killing. The Mushtan tell it so, and the Gildash tell it so, and there is no other way it is told.

Now you know what a feud is. You know that when a man of one house kills a man of another, the Keeper of the Feud enters the blood in his tally, and the blood is owed, and one day it is paid, in blood or in stock or in a marriage or in a pilgrimage walked together, and the poets weigh it, and the settlement is recited, and it binds. That is the law, and it is a good law, and every exit from it is known.

But a man of one house who kills a man of the same house has made no feud. There is nobody to owe. A house cannot pay itself. The Keeper of the Feud of the Gildash had no tally to enter it in, and the poets had nothing to weigh. The law, which has an exit for everything, had one exit for this, and the Gildash took it.

They put Hadushan outside the tent. They gave him his mare, because she was his, and his spear, because it was his, and a skin of water, and they gave him nothing else. And his father stood at the door and said the words, and his mother did not come out, and his brother did not come out. And the sheikh of the Gildash sent a man round to every camp on the circuit to say that Hadushan, son of that household, was of the Gildash no longer, and was claimed by nobody, and was owed no water, no hospitality and no vengeance.

So he was Kinless. Hear what that is, if you are a child and have not been told. A Kinless man can be killed at a well and nobody will ask for his blood. A Kinless man can be turned from any fire. A Kinless man is not a man to anyone, and he may not be married, and he may not ride with the raid, and he may not sit at the moot, and he may not be spoken to by a decent person except to be told to go. That is the law too.

And Hadushan went north on the bay mare with the white off fore, into the country near the steppe, where the wells are few and the grass is thin and the Sovidur come down out of the north to take what they can. And he lived there two years, the tellers say, by hunting, and by drinking at wells when nobody was at them, and by being turned from fires. Some tellers put in a season among the hunters at the camp at the frontier where both deserts come to water. The Mushtan do not. They say he was alone.

Now hear about Tizhva of the Mushtan.

She was a widow. Her husband had died of a fever in the year before, and she had a son of seven and a daughter of four, and she lived in the household of her husband's brother, Eskiran, who was a householder of the Mushtan with a good herd and a name in the moot. That is where a widow's family lives. Eskiran gave her a place in his tent and a share of the herd's milk, and she kept the fire and the stores, and she kept them well. And she had one thing that was her own, which the Mushtan put in every telling: the tent she kept was never shut. Eskiran's door was open because it was a Mushtan door, and every Mushtan door is open. Tizhva's fire was the one the strangers came to. She had a way of seeing a man on the sand before the dogs did.

In the year of this telling, in the season after the rains, Eskiran and every man of the household who could ride went south with a caravan out of the cities, as escort, because the Mushtan carry caravans across their country and the caravan pays, and the pay that year was good. They took the horses, and the young men, and the spears. They left the herd in the north pasture with the old men and the boys and the women, because the north pasture was quiet that year, and the Sovidur had not come down in three seasons, and the readers of the wind said they would not.

And one evening a man came in on a bay mare with a white off fore, and stopped at the edge of the firelight, and did not ask. And the dogs did not see him, and Tizhva did.

She knew who he was. Everybody on the northern circuit knew. The Gildash had sent a man round. She had the bread on the stones, and she took it off, and broke it, and pressed the salt into the torn edge, and walked out to the edge of the firelight with it in her hand, and held it out.

Hear what this is. You may turn a Kinless man from your fire, and nobody will speak against you. He is owed nothing. But the salt is not his. The salt is the host's. If the host holds it out and he eats it, he is a guest for the count of days, and the count binds the host as if the guest were the sheikh of the Gildash himself. Tizhva knew that, and she held it out.

And Hadushan looked at the bread a long time, the tellers say, as long as it takes to say the line of the Mushtan back to the founder. And then he got down from the mare and took it and ate it.

And Tizhva said, as the Mushtan say: you are a guest here. And she asked him his name, though she knew it, because it is asked after the salt. And he said: Hadushan, of nobody. And she said: tonight you are of this fire.

Hear the count of the Mushtan, because it is in the telling and it is the hinge of it. A guest of the Mushtan stays three nights. When he goes, the salt goes with him until the moon that was new when he ate is new again. That is the Mushtan's count, and every tribe has its own, and both host and guest know it. Tizhva told it to him, as a host does, and he said it back to her, as a guest does.

He stayed three nights. He slept by the door, outside, with the mare. He would not come in. He would not eat with the children. He took water when it was given, and bread when it was given, and he went out each day with the boys to the herd, because he would not eat bread he had not done something for. The old men of the household would not speak to him, because he was Kinless, and they would not tell Tizhva she was wrong, because she was right.

And on the third night the Sovidur came.

They came down out of the north at the hour before light, when the herd is couched and the boys are asleep and the dogs are tired, twenty of them on small hard steppe horses with their manes cut short, and they came at the herd and not at the tents, because it was the herd they wanted. And the boys woke and shouted, and the old men came out with the spears that were left, and the dogs went mad, and the herd got up all at once with the noise a herd makes, and began to move north in front of the riders, because that is what raiders do, they get the herd moving and keep it moving and the herd does the rest.

And Hadushan was asleep by the door with the mare. And he woke, and he got up on her without a saddle, and he took his spear, and he rode.

Now the tellers give the fight, and every teller gives it differently, and you may hear it a different way at another fire. The Mushtan give it so. He rode out alone, ahead of the old men, on the bay mare with the white off fore, and he did not ride at the raiders. He rode round them. He rode wide in the dark on the west side, where the ground was stony and the steppe horses would not follow at speed, and he came round in front of the herd, between the herd and the north, so that the herd was running toward him. And he stood the mare across their path and shouted, and waved the spear, and the lead camels saw a horse and a man and a spear in front of them, and they turned. A running herd turns where its leaders turn. It turned east, and then south, back toward the tents, with the raiders on the wrong side of it.

And then the raiders came at him, because he had taken their herd off them, and there were twenty of them and one of him, and that is the fight.

He killed three. The Mushtan say three, and the old men of the household saw two of them die and found the third in the morning. He took a wound in the left side from a steppe sword, and a second in the thigh from a lance, and the mare was cut across the chest and did not fall. And he did not give way. He kept the mare between the raiders and the herd, and the herd went on south to the tents, and the old men and the boys and the women came out to it with fire and spears and pots to beat, and the raiders saw the light and the noise and the herd going away from them, and they turned north and went home, with three empty saddles.

And when the herd was in, and the raiders were gone, and the sun came up, the old men of the household went out and found Hadushan sitting on the ground beside the mare, holding her head, with the blood from his side gone black on his leg. And he said to them the speech the Mushtan keep. It is the first of the four, and it is fixed, and it is the same at every fire.

: Hadushan's boast at the herd {#boast}

::: {.verse form=sevvar lang=en}
Count the herd. I will wait. Count them by their marks and tell me the number./
I did not count them when I turned them. I could not see their marks in the dark./
Count the herd, and if one is missing it is mine to answer for, and I have nothing to answer with./

I am Hadushan, and I am of nobody, and nobody owes me water./
I did not ride for the Mushtan. I am not of the Mushtan. They do not owe me this./
I rode for the salt. The salt was in my mouth, and it was hers, and she held it out./
A man with her salt in his mouth sits by the door while her herd is driven north?/
Let the man who would do it be Kinless. I am Kinless already. I would not do it./

Look at the mare. She is cut across the chest and she did not fall. That is her blood and not mine on her./
She did not ask why. She turned when I turned her. She stood when I stood her./
If you have a word to say about me, say it to the mare, who heard all of it./

I had a name once. I struck it out myself, with a mallet, at a marriage./
I do not ask for it back. Nobody can give it. The Gildash cannot give it; they put it outside./
But count the herd, and if the number is right, say this much at your fire, when I am gone:/
that a man of nobody ate the salt of Tizhva of the Mushtan, and the salt held./
:::

And the old men counted the herd, and the number was right, and one more besides, a steppe horse that had come in with the camels and not known how to get out. And they did not speak to him, because he was Kinless. But they carried him into the tent, which they had not done in three days, and laid him by Tizhva's fire, and one of them went out and saw to the mare.

Now hear how the count ran.

He could not leave. His side was open and his thigh was open, and Tizhva sewed them with gut and bound them with the herbs of the north pasture, and he lay by her fire twelve days with a fever, and the children were afraid of him and brought him water. And all those days he was still a guest, because a guest who cannot rise is not a guest who has gone, and the Mushtan's count does not begin until he goes.

And the boys of the household told the boys of the next camp how Hadushan of nobody had turned the herd, and the next camp told the next, and a poet of the Mushtan made a verse about the bay mare with the white off fore, a little verse, and it went round the circuit in ten days. And on the circuit it came to the Gildash.

And the Gildash heard that Hadushan was alive, and where he was.

Hear this carefully, because the tellers of the Gildash tell it differently from the Mushtan, and both are told. The Mushtan say that the men who came were Varinash's brothers, three of them, and that they came because the Gildash had cast Hadushan out without paying for Varinash, and a dead man unpaid is a weight on his kin, and they had been carrying it two years. The Gildash say the brothers came because their mother, Kaminah, sent them. Either way they came, and they came lawfully. They came to the edge of the Mushtan's pasture and they made camp there, on the open sand, a long way from Tizhva's fire, and they sent word in.

Their word was this. We do not come into the tent. He has eaten your salt, and we do not break salt. We will wait for him to go, and we will wait for the moon of your count to be new again, and then he is nobody's, and we will do what is owed. And they sat down on the sand to wait.

And Hadushan heard it, lying by the fire with the fever going out of him, and he said to Tizhva: they are right. And she said: I know they are right. And he said: when I can stand, I will go, and they will wait a moon, and then they will kill me, and they will be right to. Do not be ashamed of it. And Tizhva said nothing.

And on the twentieth day Eskiran came home from the south with the escort and the caravan's pay, and found his herd whole and one more horse in it, and a Kinless man by his brother's widow's fire, and three Gildash on the sand at the edge of his pasture.

Hear Eskiran. He was a good householder and a careful one. He sat down by the fire and heard the whole of it from the old men, and from the boys, and from Tizhva, and then from Hadushan, and he did not interrupt any of them. And when he had heard it he said: the salt was yours, sister, and it was rightly given, and it has been rightly kept, and nobody will say otherwise. And when he goes, the count goes with him, and the Mushtan will keep the count to the last night, against the Gildash and against anyone. And when the moon is new, he is nobody's.

And Tizhva said: unless he is claimed.

Now you know what claiming is. A Kinless man is claimed by nobody, and the word is exact: nobody has claimed him. A household may claim a stranger, and take him in, and make him a dependent of its tent, sheltered without being of its blood, and then he is somebody's, and whoever kills him owes. But hear what comes with him. A man comes with his debts. A Kinless man has one debt the law cannot reach while he is Kinless, because there is nobody to owe it, and the moment he is claimed, there is somebody: the house that claimed him. Claim Hadushan, and Varinash's blood is owed by the Mushtan to the Gildash, and the Keeper of the Feud of the Gildash opens his tally at last, and there is a feud between two houses that had none, over a killing neither of them did.

Eskiran said that. He said it plainly. And the old men of the household said it after him. And Tizhva said: I know. And then she stood up by the fire and gave the second speech, which the Mushtan keep, and which the women of the Mushtan say at their own fires in her words.

: Tizhva's claim {#claim}

::: {.verse form=sevvar lang=en}
Brother of my husband, hear me, because I am in your tent and I speak in it by your leave./
I held out the salt. Nobody told me to. I saw him on the sand and I held it out./
Nobody would have spoken against me if I had let him ride by. I did not let him ride by./

I am a widow and I have nothing. The milk I give my children is from your herd./
The herd he turned is your herd. The herd he bled for is your herd. I have nothing to pay with./
I am not asking you to pay for him. I am telling you what he has paid already./

He did not have to go out. He had been here three nights. He was going in the morning./
A guest does not ride for the herd. The herd is not his. The men are not his. The children are not his./
He went out because the salt was in his mouth, and it was mine, and he would not have it said/
that a man sat by my door with my salt in him while my brother-in-law's herd went north./

If you let him go, the count will hold, and you will keep it, and I know you will keep it./
And the moon will be new, and the Gildash will kill him on the sand a day from here,/
and they will be right, and the law will be kept, and the Mushtan will be clean of it./
And every child of this tent will grow up drinking milk from a herd that a dead man turned./

I do not ask for him to be of our blood. He is not. I ask for him to be of our tent./
I ask for the claim. I know what comes with it. I know the blood of Varinash comes with it./
Let it come. Let the Keeper of the Gildash open his tally. A tally can be closed./
A feud is a thing with exits, and you are a man who can find one, and I have seen you find them./
A Kinless man has no exit. Give him one. Give him us.
:::

And Eskiran sat a long time. The tellers make it long. Some say he went out and walked round the herd three times. Some say he looked at the steppe horse that had come in with the camels. The Mushtan say he looked at the children, and that is all they say. And then he said: I will go out to the Gildash on the sand. And he went.

Now hear the exit. This is the part a child of the Mushtan is told to listen to, because it is the part that is law.

Eskiran went out to the camp of the three brothers on the sand with no spear, and he sat down with them, and he ate their bread, because they offered it, and he asked them for the thing a Mushtan has a right to ask: who is the one to whom Varinash's blood is owed? And they said: our mother, Kaminah. The Keeper of the Feud has no tally for it, because it was not a feud, and so it is hers. And Eskiran said: then I will go to her. And the brothers looked at each other, and one of them rode with him, and they rode three days to the Gildash.

And Eskiran came before Kaminah, the mother of Varinash, in her tent, and stood, because she did not ask him to sit. And he said: I am Eskiran of the Mushtan. My household means to claim Hadushan, who was of the Gildash, and when we claim him, your son's blood is ours to owe. We have come to ask what it is.

And Kaminah said: you have come to ask what my son is worth.

And Eskiran said: no. I have come to ask what you will take. What he is worth is not a thing I can ask.

And she asked him what the Mushtan had. And he told her: a good herd, the one Hadushan turned from the Sovidur, every camel of it, and the steppe horse besides. And she asked him why the Mushtan would give it for a Kinless man who had killed her son in a tent full of guests. And he told her what Tizhva had said, word for word, because a man who carries a speech carries it whole. And Kaminah sat with it a long time, and then she gave the third speech, which the Gildash keep, and which is where their telling ends.

: Kaminah's answer {#answer}

::: {.verse form=sevvar lang=en}
My son was a poet, and he made a verse at a marriage that was not his to make./
He called a man slow in front of a guest who had been given that man's word that morning, and had it kept./
I knew it when he said it. I saw the guest's face. I did not stop him. I was laughing./

The man he called slow killed him with a tent-mallet, and my son fell in the meat./
I washed him. I sewed him into the cloth. Nobody had sat round him to say his deeds; he had not stayed for it./
I rode with him into the deep sand, and the fire was lit where we set him down, and I came back./

The Gildash put the other one outside, and nobody paid me, and I have carried it two years./
I sent my sons to the Mushtan's sand to wait for the moon. I would send them again./
And now you come into my tent and offer me the herd he bled for. Hear what I say./

I will take the herd. I will not take it from you. I will take it from him./
The camels he turned in the dark with a cut in his side, let them come to me./
Let him drive them here himself, on the bay mare with the white foot, and let me see him do it./
Let my sons count them at my door in front of the Keeper, and let the Keeper close the tally/
that was never opened, and let it be recited, and let it bind, and let him go back to you./

I do not forgive him. Forgiveness is not in the settlement, and I am not asked for it./
I will not have it said that a woman of the Gildash took a herd for her son from strangers/
when the man who owed it was alive and could ride. Let him ride. That is my price.
:::

And Eskiran came home, and told it at Tizhva's fire, word for word. And Hadushan was standing by then, though not well. And he heard it, and he said nothing for a while, and then he said: that is a fair price. It is fairer than mine was.

And the household of Eskiran claimed him that night, by the fire, in the old words, which the Mushtan do not put in the telling, because they are said only when they are meant. And Hadushan was of the Mushtan's tent, a dependent, sheltered and not of the blood, and the moon of the count was not yet new, and it did not matter any more.

And in the morning Hadushan got up on the bay mare with the white off fore, with his side bound, and he and the boys of the household got the herd moving south, every camel of it and the steppe horse besides, the herd he had turned out of the north in the dark. And Eskiran rode with him, and Tizhva did not, because it was not hers to ride. And the herd went south for three days to the tents of the Gildash, and Hadushan drove it, and nobody spoke to him on the road, and the brothers of Varinash rode a long way off on either side, watching.

And at Kaminah's door the herd was couched, and her sons counted it in front of the Keeper of the Feud of the Gildash, camel by camel, by their marks, and the number was right. And Kaminah came out and looked at Hadushan on the mare, and she did not speak to him, and he did not speak to her. And the Keeper of the Feud opened the tally, which had never been opened, and entered the blood of Varinash in it, and in the same breath entered the herd against it, and closed it. And the poets of both tents recited the settlement, which is the fourth speech, and it is short, and it binds to this day.

: The settlement {#settlement}

::: {.verse form=sevvar lang=en}
The blood of Varinash of the Gildash is owed by the tent of Eskiran of the Mushtan, which has claimed the man who shed it./
It is paid with the herd the man turned from the north, every camel by its mark, and a steppe horse besides./
The man drove it to the door himself. The sons of Kaminah counted it in front of the Keeper./
The tally is opened and closed. Nothing is owed between the Gildash and the Mushtan for this./
This is recited. It binds.
:::

And Hadushan rode home to the Mushtan with Eskiran, with nothing, on the bay mare with the white off fore, and the household of Eskiran was poorer by a whole herd, and the Mushtan fed it out of the common herd that year as tribes do, and Eskiran was a long time getting back what he gave.

Hear the end of it, and then you may sleep.

Hadushan lived in the tent of Eskiran the rest of his life, and he was never of the Mushtan's blood, and he was never anything but a dependent of the tent, and he did not ask to be. He rode with the escort. He kept the herd. He slept by the door outside with the mare until she died, and then he slept by the door outside alone. He did not marry. Some tellers say he married Tizhva, and the Mushtan say that is a telling for children who want a marriage at the end, and it is not so: she was his host, and you do not marry your host, and he would not have had it said.

The Circle acclaimed him in his age. The tellers who put it in say it was for the turning of the herd, and the old men of the Circle say it was for the driving of it to the Gildash, and the Mushtan say both. He wore the indigo strip on his arm, and renewed it at the Stone at every season-turn he could ride to, and it never faded.

And when he was dying, the household sat round him as a household does, and each one said a deed of his, one deed each, for the god to record before the crossing begins. And the children of Tizhva, grown, said the herd. And Eskiran's sons said the herd. And Tizhva said the salt: she said he ate it. And Eskiran, who was very old then, said: he drove the herd to the Gildash himself, and they counted it, and the number was right.

And the Mushtan say the bridge was wide for him, and that is the one thing the telling says that nobody can know, and the tellers say it anyway, and the tent is quiet while they do.

That is the telling of Hadushan the Kinless, who was claimed. Bank the fire.
