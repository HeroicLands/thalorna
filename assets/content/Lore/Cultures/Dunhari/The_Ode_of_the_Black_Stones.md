**The Ode of the Black Stones**, _sharvend re qodar qazram_, is an ode of [Siaran Bergoz]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a poet of the [Bergoz]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}, and it is the ode the tribes call for first when the common council sits at [[place-ghavulavad|Ghavulavad]] and the poets contend in the market under the palms. A _sharvend_ is one long poem held on a single end-sound from its first line to its last, and it moves through stages every listener knows: the halt at an abandoned camp, the journey, and the boast. The rendering keeps the end-sound as a closing _d_ on every line, which is as near as the rendering comes to the one sound the poem never lets go of.

Siaran is long dead, and the ode is carried by the Bergoz transmitters, who hold a poet's work by heart and give it with his name at the head of it. The halt is at the empty camp of the [Beluri]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}, where a woman of that tribe, [Shandara]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, camped one season and moved on with her people. The journey crosses the [Pale Floor]{.name data-type="place/region"} on the day the apprentices at the toll-tent of the [[place-twrlghtnmrkd|Tower of the Lightning-Marked]] name as safe. The boast turns on Siaran's grandfather, [Hurdan Bergoz]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who in a year of no grass killed his last she-camel to feed a stranger, and learned when the stranger had eaten that he was a man of the [Khareman]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}, the tribe the Bergoz were at feud with. The Bergoz say the settlement that ended that feud a generation later names the camel among the things paid. The Khareman recite the settlement too, and their version names the camel as well.

: The Ode of the Black Stones {#ode}

::: {.verse form=sharvend lang=en}
Stop, both of you. Couch the camels here in the lee of the mesa, where the stone is red/
and the wadi bends, and the gravel gives out, and the wind has laid its long ribs in the sand./
This was the camp of the Beluri. Do not tell me it was not. I know the ground/
the way a man knows the ground of his own tent in the dark, by the feet and not the head./
Here were the tents, in a line along the bank, black hair on the poles, every rope tied/
to a tamarisk root, and the roots are here, and the knots have gone the way of the hand/
that tied them. Here is the trench they cut round the tents for the winter rain, and the sand/
has filled it to the lip, and you can trace it still, a ring around nothing, like a bead/
that has lost its string. Here the herds came down to the seep. The seep has dried./
Here the dung of the camels lies in heaps gone white, like peppercorns, and as hard./

And here are the three black stones. Look at them. Nobody moved them. They were laid/
in a hollow for the pot to sit on, three of them, and the fire between them fed/
every evening of a season, and the soot has gone into the stone and stayed./
The wind cannot clean it. The rain, in a year it comes, runs off it. They are black, and they stand/
where she set the pot on them, and where she knelt, and the ash of that fire is in the ground./
I have seen the hearths of a hundred camps. I have not stopped at them. I have stopped at this one. I said/
to my companions on the road that I would ride by, and I have not ridden by. Get down. Stand./

Shandara of the Beluri knelt at these stones in the season the rain was good, and the herd/
grew fat, and the guests came in from the road, and her people's welcome was a thing men heard/
of at wells three days off. I saw her once, through the smoke, with her sleeve tied/
back from the wrist, and her hand on the pot, and a coal had fallen out and she put it back with a reed./
That is all I saw. I am not a liar. I will not tell you that I saw more than I did./
I will not tell you that she looked at me. She looked at the pot. The Beluri struck the camp in the third month, and rode/
south and east, on the circuit their fathers rode, and I went north on mine, and I have not heard/
whether she is married, or whether she is dead, or whether the Beluri have taken a road/
that does not come back by this water. The wind has taken their tracks. It took them in a night. It has made/
a new face on the dune where their camels went over, and the face does not say which way they strayed./

Weep, if you want to. I am not weeping. A man of the Bergoz does not weep at a hearth that is cold./
He stands at it until the camels complain, and then he gets up into the saddle. So it was told/
to me by the old men, and so I do it, and I have stood here long enough for the sun to have moved/
the shadow of the mesa from my left foot to my right. Time has done to this camp what it does to every camp it is allowed/
to come to, and it is allowed to come to all of them. It came to the camps of the fathers, and they were proud,/
and their tents were wide, and their wells were deep, and their herds were a cloud/
of dust from one horizon to the other, and the wind has had them, and the herds are dead,/
and the wells are sand, and if their names are anywhere it is in a poet's mouth, and nowhere else, and that is the whole of what they held./
I know it. I am not a child. I have seen the black stones. Now I get up. The camel has waited until her patience is frayed,/
and the road is long, and it is nearly evening. Hold the halter. Hold her head./

Now hear about the camel, since we are riding. She is grey, and she is of the breed/
the Bergoz have kept on the northern circuit since before the first poet's word was made,/
narrow in the chest and long in the leg, with a neck like a palm-trunk the wind has bowed,/
and her pads are wide and hard, and they spread on the sand and close on the stone, and the road/
cannot find a way to lame her. She was dropped in a year of good rain, and she was weaned/
on the milk of three mothers, because her own went dry, and she has never been beaten, and she has never been sold./
When she runs, her hump does not shake. When she kneels, she kneels in four movements, like an old/
man praying, and when she rises she rises all at once, and the saddle is level, and nothing is spilled./
Her eye is large and dark and has long lashes against the blown sand, and her ear is turned/
toward my voice and not toward the wind, and when I speak she answers in her throat like a gourd/
that water is being poured from. I have ridden her in the heat until her shadow shrank under her and died,/
and she did not ask for water until I had drunk. I have ridden her in the cold/
of a winter night under the stars until the frost was white on the cloth of the saddle, and she held/
her pace like a woman carrying a jar on her head at the well, that does not slop and is not afraid./
There is no camel like her between the sea and the wall. I have said it. It is said./

We went south to the Pale Floor, she and I. You have heard of it. It is the floor of a sea that is dead,/
white salt from the edge of the sky to the edge of the sky, with the black tower standing in the middle of it like a thing that was not made/
by any people we know of, and the iron on its top, and the glass that the lightning has poured/
down its sides. A caravan that crosses on the wrong day is found in the spring with the glass in its wounds. It is not a word/
I make up for the poem. Ask the guides. At the north edge of the salt the young ones of the Lightning-Marked have pitched a tent and stayed/
to read the sky for the crossers, and they take one load in twenty of salt for it, and I paid/
in salt, though I had little, and the boy who read the sky for me was young enough that his beard/
was not yet a beard, and he looked up for a long time, and he looked at me, and then he named the day aloud./
And I waited for the day. And on the day I went. I had a skin of water and a bag of dates and a bit of bread,/
and the grey she-camel, and a spear, and nothing else. And the sun went down red/
and the salt went red, and then the salt went blue, and then the moon came up and the salt was white as a shroud,/
and her pads went over it with a sound like a woman tearing cloth, all night, and nothing else was heard./
I did not look at the tower. A man of the Bergoz who crosses the Floor does not look at the tower. It is understood./
I looked at her ears, and at the stars between them, and I kept the Rider's Spear behind my left shoulder, the way my father showed/
me when I was a boy and the stars were names I did not know yet. And once in the night the wind stirred/
and the salt moved over the salt like smoke, and she stopped, and stood, and I did not beat her. I said/
her name to her, and she went on. And once there was a sound behind us like iron being struck with a rod,/
far off, and the air smelled of the moment after the bolt, and the hair of her neck stood, and she bowed/
her head and went on, and I did not turn round, and in the morning the salt was gone and the gravel had come back, and we stood/
on the far side, and the boy at the tent had read the day right, and I gave him in my heart the thanks I could not give with my hand./
Say what you like of the Lightning-Marked. Their children read true. That is the most a man can say of a reader, and it is said./

And on the far side, on the gravel, I saw the storm. It stood on the southern ridges, and its foot was hid/
in its own rain, grey as the she-camel, and its head was white and high as a fire-temple, and it moved/
east along the ridges the way a great sheikh moves along the line of his guests, slow, and the lightning played/
in it, not striking, opening, the way a hand opens to show what is in it, and closes, and the thunder followed/
long after, and walked over the sand to me, and I felt it in the camel's ribs, and in my own, and in the ground./
That was the Thunder Rider going to the south and the east, where the Beluri had gone. So I prayed/
to him from the saddle, as a man may pray who has nothing to offer the god but his word:/
Rider, you are just, and you strike where you must, and nobody argues with your spear. I do not ask you to turn aside./
Go where you are going. Only, where you find the black tents of the Beluri on your road,/
stand over them a night, and let down your rain on their grazing, and fill their seep to the lip, and leave their herd/
fat, and their fire dry, and the woman who kneels at their stones unharmed. That is all I ask. I have no more to send./
And the storm went on to the south and east, and I do not know where it stopped, or whether it rained on the Beluri. I was not owed/
an answer, and I did not wait for one. The camel was thirsty. I turned her toward the well, and she smelled the water, and she was glad./

And after the storm I did not go to the Beluri. I did not know where they had gone. I turned and rode/
home on the circuit, to the tents of the Bergoz, and to my father's tent, and to the fire my mother fed,/
and I sat down in the guest's place, because I had been gone a season, and my mother broke the bread/
and pressed the salt into the edge of it and held it out to me as if I were a stranger, and I ate it, and she laughed until she had to sit down, and my father smiled behind his hand./
That is the custom of our tent for a son who has been a season away. It is a joke and it is not a joke. I have explained/
it to men of other tribes and they have not understood. Let them not understand. It is ours. I am glad./

Now I will boast, and you will hear me, because the boast is true, and a boast that is true is not a sin in the god's regard./
I am of the Bergoz. Our tents are pitched on the high side of the well, where the stranger coming in on the road/
sees the smoke of them first, because we want him to see it. Our dogs are tied at the back, where the stranger will not be afraid./
Our fires are kept high all night in the winter so that a man lost on the sand can steer by them, and many have steered, and come in, and been fed./
We have never turned a man from our fire. Not in the bad years. Not in the year the wells went to mud,/
not in the year the raiders came out of the north and took the herd of my father's brother, and he followed/
them for nine days and came back with half of it, and fed the first guest who came in on the half that was saved./
Ask at Ghavulavad. Ask the Water-Warden, who keeps the order of the herds. Ask the caravan-masters, who plan their road/
around our smoke, which tents on the circuit are open, and which are closed. Ask them where the Bergoz stand./

Hear about my grandfather, Hurdan, the son of Rushak. In his time there was a year with no rain, and the grass died/
in the ground before it came up, and the herds died standing, and the Bergoz went from well to well and found/
the wells low, and the guides went out for water and came back with less than they had carried out, and of all his herd Hurdan had/
one she-camel, white, the last of his herd, that he had raised from a calf at the breast of a dead/
dam, and she was all he had, and he had a wife and two sons, and the camel was their milk and their road./
And a man came in at evening from the south, on foot, with no beast at all, and his feet were blistered and his lips were cracked, and he stood/
at the edge of the firelight, and he did not ask, because a man in that state does not ask. He stood./
And Hurdan got up from the fire and went to the white camel and spoke to her, and took the knife from his belt, and the blood/
went into the sand, and the sand drank it, and nobody in the tent said a word,/
not his wife, not his sons, and my grandmother built up the fire and set the pot on the stones, and the meat was boiled,/
and Hurdan broke the bread with his own hands and pressed the salt into the edge, and the stranger ate what he was offered,/
and only when he had eaten did my grandfather ask his name. And the stranger said/
he was of the Khareman. And the Khareman and the Bergoz were at feud. Two of ours were dead/
at their hands that the Keeper of the Feud had in his tally, unpaid, and one of theirs at ours, and the blood/
was open between the tents, and every man of the Bergoz who heard the name put his hand to his sword./

And Hurdan said: he has eaten. Sit down. And they sat. And the Khareman stayed/
the count of days that the salt gives a guest in the tents of the Bergoz, and he was guarded/
by the men who would have killed him at the well, and when the count was done Hurdan gave him the white camel's saddle and a skin of water and a bag of bread/
and showed him the road home, and the Khareman went home on foot, and the feud was open, and Hurdan stood/
on the high side of the well with no camel and two sons and a wife, and the Bergoz fed/
them out of the common herd until the rain came, and the rain came in the next year, and the grass, and the herds were bred/
again from what was left. That is the whole of it. Do not tell me that the feud should have ended. It did not end./
It ended in my father's time, before the poets of the third tribe, and the Keeper of the Feud read out the tally, and the blood was weighed/
against the blood, and the herds were counted out, and the poets of both tents recited the settlement, and in the Khareman's own recital, the camel is paid./
The white she-camel of Hurdan Bergoz is paid for in the settlement. The Khareman put her in it. They said/
that a debt is a debt, and they had eaten her, and they would not go across the bridge with her in their mouths unpaid./
That is the Khareman, and I will not satirize a tribe that keeps what it owed./

I have spoken of my grandfather. Now I will speak of myself, and then I am finished, and you may go to bed./
I have no herd to speak of. I have the grey she-camel and a spear and a tent that my father left me, and the bread/
I break in it is not much, and the salt is little, and I break it for anyone who comes, and I ask no name until it is eaten. I have stood/
at the hearth of the Beluri and not wept. I have crossed the Pale Floor on the named day and not looked at the tower. I have held/
my tongue when a sheikh of another tribe insulted my father, because I was his guest, and I have kept the word/
I gave, every word I gave, and some of them cost me, and I paid./
That is the whole of my boast. It is short, because the life is short, and I am not old./
And this is the end of it. The wind will have this camp, as it had the camp of the Beluri, and it will have the black stones when they are no longer black, and it will have the trench and the mound/
of my own fire, and the grey she-camel, and the spear, and the tent, and me. It has been allowed/
to have all of it, and I do not argue with what is allowed. But it is not allowed to have this. This is not in the sand./
The transmitters of the Bergoz have it, and they will give it to their sons, and one day a man will stop at the three black stones in the lee of the mesa where the stone is red,/
and get down from his camel, and say it, and his companions will hear it, and they will know the ground.
