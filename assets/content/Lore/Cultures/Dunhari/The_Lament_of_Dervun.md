**The Lament of Dervun**, _qorvend re dervun_, is the lament [Dervun]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the [Shivash]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} raised over her brother [Nemver]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the night he was brought in dead from the grazing, and raised again every year after at the [Festival of the Departed]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, when the camps speak their dead aloud around their own fires. A _qorvend_ is made by a woman over one of her own dead and is hers; the Shivash women keep this one because Dervun made it, and the woman of the household nearest her line leads it now. It is held on one end-sound, as an ode is, and the rendering closes every line on _n_. The women around the fire answer each refrain: he does not come in.

Nemver was killed by men of the [Sobukan]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} at a quarrel over grazing, and the feud between the two tribes found its exit in stock: the Keeper of the Feud entered his blood, the poets weighed it, and the Sobukan paid camels, and the settlement was recited and binds both tribes. The lament does not unsay it. Dervun counts the camels in it, and keeps the count, and asks for nothing. The rest of it is his deeds, said one by one as the camp says the deeds of the dying so that the god records them before the crossing, and the night ride on which she carried him into the deep sand and lit the fire where they set him down.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
Light the fire high. Higher. Let it be seen from the ridge, and from the ridge beyond, and from the plain/
past the ridge, where the road comes in from the south, where a man on the road would see it and turn in./
That was how he lit it. That was the height of my brother's fire. I am lighting it the same, and he is gone./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
He was the son of my mother and my father, the second son, and I was the daughter, and we were born/
one winter apart, and we were nursed by the one breast, and he was the one who carried me on/
his shoulders down to the seep when I was too small to walk on the stones, and set me down/
in the water to my knees, and laughed at me for crying at the cold of it. I have not cried since then./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
Hear his deeds. I will say them one by one, as we said them when he was brought home, for the god, who writes them down/
before the crossing, and wants them true, and wants them each in its place. I will not add one./
I will not take one away. A sister who adds to her brother's deeds narrows his bridge with her own mouth, and the bridge is all that is his own./
Hear his deeds, and let the god hear them again, in case the god was not listening then./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
The first deed. In the year the sand came, the black wind out of the south, that buried the camp at Ghavulavad to the ridgepole of the tents, and the caravans were blown/
off the road like chaff, he went out into it. He tied a cloth over his face and a rope round his waist and went out into the brown/
dark of it, and found a caravan of the cities lost in it, eleven men and their camels, and he brought them in/
on the rope, one camel at a time, along the rope he had laid from our tent to the place they lay down,/
and the merchant who owned it wept on his feet and offered him silver, and he would not take one coin./
That is the first. Write it, god of the bridge. I have said it as it was. It is done./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
The second deed. A man of the Sobukan came to our fire in the winter before the feud, a herdsman, cold, with his toes black, and his dog with him, and his camel down/
on the road a day back with a broken leg, and Nemver broke the bread and pressed the salt in/
and asked his name only when he had eaten, and when he heard Sobukan he did not change his face. He gave him his own cloak, the brown/
one with the red border that our mother wove, and the herdsman went home in it, and I saw it again. I will tell you when./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
The third deed. He kept his word to the Water-Warden at the long wells in the drought, when the order of the herds was given, and ours was set last, and the herds of three tribes went down/
to the water before ours, and ours stood at the trough with their tongues out, and the young men of the Shivash wanted to go in/
before their turn, and they had their hands on their swords, and he stood at the trough with his back to the water and his arms out, and said: when the Warden says. Not one man/
of ours drank out of turn. Our herd lost nine camels that day. He did not speak of it again./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
The fourth deed. He was not a poet. He could not make a verse. He said so at the moot, when the sheikh asked for one, and the sheikh was ashamed for him, and the poets of the other tribes looked at their hands and began to grin,/
and Nemver said: I cannot make you a verse. I can tell you where the water is under the black hill, which nobody has told you, and I will tell you for nothing. And he told them. And it was true. And they drink there yet, and their herds, and their kin,/
the poets who grinned. That is a verse. I have made it for him. He could not. I can./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
The fifth deed is the last. He went out to the grazing at the red wadi in the spring, where the Sobukan had come in with their herd, as they had a right to, and our herd was there, as we had a right to, and the grass was thin,/
and a word was said. I do not know the word. The Sobukan say one thing and the Shivash say another, and the poets weighed both, and the weighing is done./
He drew. They drew. There were three of them and two of ours, and he was the one who died. He had a spear-wound under the arm. It went in/
above the heart, the old women said, when they washed him, and he did not take long. That is the last deed, and it is not a deed. I count it with the others because it was his own./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
Now hear the night I rode with him. We put him on the fastest camel we had, the grey one, the one the Sobukan had always wanted, and I rode beside on the dun,/
and the fire-keeper rode with us, and my father did not, because my father could not, and my mother did not, because it was not hers, and I went because I am his sister and I would not let him be carried alone./
We rode all night into the deep sand south of the long wells, where there is no road and no well and no mark, and the stars came out and went round, and the moon/
came up behind us and went down ahead of us, and at the hour before morning the fire-keeper said: here. And we got down./
We set him on the sand. We did not dig. Nothing is dug. The fire-keeper kindled the fire at his head, and it caught, and burned, and the smoke went up straight as a spear, and the fire burned on,/
and we stood there until it was light, and then we got up on the camels and rode back, and I did not look behind me, and I do not know the place. Nobody knows the place. That is the law. No tomb is raised, and no grave is cut, and nobody can rob him of a single stone./
I do not know where my brother lies. I know the night. I know the stars. I know which way the smoke went up. I know the place is nowhere, and I know that he lies in it, and that the fire burned down./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
Now hear the settlement. Hear it, women of the Shivash. I will say it, and you will not stop me, because I put it in on the night the camels came in./
The blood of Nemver of the Shivash is owed by the Sobukan, and the Keeper entered it in his tally, and the poets of a third tribe weighed it, and the elders argued the rate, and the rate was settled: forty camels, she-camels, in milk, and two of them white, and the rest brown./
They came to our tents on the morning of the twentieth day. The Sobukan drove them in themselves. And the herdsman who drove the last of them wore a brown/
cloak with a red border, and he did not look at me, and I did not look at him, and I counted the camels with my father in front of the Keeper, forty, two white, the rest brown, and the number was right, and the tally was closed, and the settlement was recited, and it binds, and I keep it. I have not unsaid it. I will not unsay one line./
Forty camels for my brother. Two white and thirty-eight brown. Write that down also, god of the bridge. They paid in milk-camels. They paid in full. Nobody owes us, and we owe no one./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
Hear me, women. I am not asking for blood. Whoever says that the sister of Nemver asks for blood is a liar, and his bridge is narrow, and I will tell him so at the moot, in front of the Sobukan./
The blood is paid. The feud is closed. It had an exit and we took it, and my father took it, and I stood beside him and counted, and I would take it again. A feud without an exit is the Serpent's, and I am not the Serpent's. I am my brother's sister. I keep the law he kept at the trough with his arms out and his back to the water when the herd was dying in the sun./
But the law has no exit from this. The camels are in our herd, and the milk is in our children, and I drink it, and every night of my life I light the fire high, and he does not come in./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.

[The one who leads:]{.speaker}/
Higher. Light it higher. Let it be seen from the ridge, and the ridge beyond, and the road from the south, where a man would see it and turn in./
Let any man who sees it turn in, of any tribe, of the Sobukan, of nobody. Let him come in./
I will break the bread for him and press the salt in, and ask his name only when he has eaten, as my brother did, and if he is of the Sobukan I will not change my face. I have learned it from my brother, and my brother is gone./
Nemver, the tent is open, and you do not come in.

[The women:]{.speaker}/
He does not come in.
