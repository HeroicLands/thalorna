**The Song of the Last Shrine** is the fourth of the six songs of the long sea song of [Lentize]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a fisher's son of [[place-volsather|Volsater]] who took his ship past the last shrine of the [[affiliation-pozeras|Wayfarer]] and came back, and went again. It is sung in eight-line stanzas whose first six lines rhyme in turns and whose last two rhyme together, a form the síladors of the south use for nothing but the sea. A sílador sings it with a bowed instrument across his knee at the autumn equinox, at the [Celebration of Arrivals]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} of that faith, when travelers who have come home climb the Wayfarer's temple steps with what they vowed and tell their roads to the priests. Volsater sings it on the quay that night as the song for the one who did not arrive, and the singer runs through the first three songs in a stanza before he begins.

The ship is the [Solmere]{.name data-type="vehicle"}, which is the word for longing, and the song makes much of that. The headland is the [Last Shrine]{.name data-type="place/site"}, the farthest north and west of the god's way-shrines on the cliff coast of [[place-zuravlenrgn|Zuravlen]], where a keeper kept a lamp and a book of who went past. The song holds that the book is still open at Lentize's line. The woman who stands on the cut with a lamp is [Aulira]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and a line of hers stands in the song twice: Volsater says the line came out of the women's songs on the salt pans, and the women sing it there still ([[lore-saltpansongs|The Songs of the Salt Pans]]).

The southern ports sing the song shorter. In [[place-jalnazdal|Jalnasdal]] and [[place-kalveza|Kalveza]], where a patron pays for it, the sílador ends at the stanza in which Lentize sets his men ashore by their cities and pays them twice, and the song is a song of a master who kept the order of what he owed. Volsater sings the last five stanzas as well, and both versions close on the short stanza that sends the song to the patron who paid for it.

The poem is rhymed ABABABCC.
