**The Song of the Last Shrine** is the fourth of the six songs of the long sea song of **Lenthìze**, a fisher's son of [[place-portvent|Portvent]] who took his ship past the last shrine of the Wayfarer and came back, and went again. It is sung in eight-line stanzas whose first six lines rhyme in turns and whose last two rhyme together, a form the síladòrs of the south use for nothing but the sea. A síladòr sings it with a bowed instrument across his knee at the autumn equinox, at the Celebration of Arrivals of [[affiliation-pozeras|Pôzêrâs]], when travelers who have come home climb the Wayfarer's temple steps with what they vowed and tell their roads to the priests. Portvent sings it on the quay that night as the song for the one who did not arrive, and the singer runs through the first three songs in a stanza before he begins.

The ship is the **Solmère**, which is the word for longing, and the song makes much of that. The headland is the **Last Shrine**, the farthest north and west of the god's way-shrines on the cliff coast of [[place-zuravlenrgn|Zûravlen]], where a keeper kept a lamp and a book of who went past. The song holds that the book is still open at Lenthìze's line. The woman who stands on the cut with a lamp is **Aulìra**, and a line of hers stands in the song twice: Portvent says the line came out of the women's songs on the salt pans, and the women sing it there still ([[lore-saltpansongs|The Songs of the Salt Pans]]).

The southern ports sing the song shorter. In Lunacòrte and Belpòrte, where a patron pays for it, the síladòr ends at the stanza in which Lenthìze sets his men ashore by their cities and pays them twice, and the song is a song of a master who kept the order of what he owed. Portvent sings the last five stanzas as well, and both versions close on the short stanza that sends the song to the patron who paid for it.

: The Song of the Last Shrine {#song}

```poetry {form=voyage-song rhyme=ABABABCC lang=en}
Wayfarer, lord of every hallowed road,
you with the serpent staff and the needle crown,
I ask no wind of you. I ask what's owed:
the count of days, the soundings written down,
the water measured and the biscuit stowed,
the morning the last headland settled down.
Thunderer, let the weather have its say.
I sing a ship that sailed, and turned away.

You who are home again at the equinox
and climbed the temple steps with what you vowed,
the wine, the first fish, silver in a box,
and told the priests your road, and told it loud
for them to draw, its harbors and its rocks:
there is one road their maps are not allowed.
It goes past the Last Shrine and does not bend.
I sing it every year. It has no end.

You know the songs before: how Portvent cut
its one road down the rock face of the cliff;
how Lenthìze, a fisher's son, first put
his net out, nine years old, from his father's skiff;
how he laid the Solmère's cedar keel, and shut
his door on every counsel and every if,
and took her up the Gulf, and brought her back.
Now hear the fourth song, and the western track.

On the spring day the outbound ships are blessed,
the Wayfarer's priest came down to Portvent's strand.
He oiled the brows, the master and the rest,
and set a carven staff in every hand,
and spoke the oath, and they spoke it, twelve abreast
along the tideline, barefoot on the sand:
to go with honor, help the ones in distress,
and to come home, if possible. Say yes.

They said it, and he said it, every word,
the honor and the help, the coming back,
and the small word the god puts in. She heard,
Aulìra, high up on the cut's steep track,
the place he slowed on it. Nobody stirred.
The priest went on. The tide had turned to slack.
She said that night: You read it like a clause
you mean to plead in court. He said: It was.

She said: You named your ship for longing. Whose?
You'll have the water. Longing will be mine.
You name a thing for what you mean to lose
and paint it on the stern in a fine line
and sail away in it, and what you choose
to leave me is the name, the lamp, the brine.
He did not answer, which is how the coast
refuses, when it loves the asker most.

Now hear the twelve, and hear them as they're named,
the city first, the way a man is known:
Belpòrte, a caulker, whose seams were never blamed;
Lunacòrte, a clerk to keep the count, whose own
hand was a notary's; Fiòrdaure, famed
for salt, a cooper for the casks; from the old stone
of Aurèliane, a student of the stars
who'd read them in a book and not from spars.

Válaren, a sailmaker, to mend what tore;
Monvèrdre, a hill man who had only heard
the sea described, and wanted to see more;
Hárivren, a carpenter; a cook, preferred
for the dried peaches Mercadaure had in store;
Batáren, a pilot of the north, whose word
went half Élavendri when the wind was high;
and Portvent, three, his cousins, born nearby.

They went the way the fast boats go, at night,
when the wind comes off the valley, off the land,
out through the notch, and Portvent's single light
went small behind them, and the town would stand
along the cut with lamps until the white
of the sail was nothing on the dark, a strand
of foam, and then not that. She did not wave.
A wave lets go. A lamp is what you have.

Three days they ran north up the coast, grey on grey,
the wind astern, the cold swell on the beam,
until they raised the headland, and the ray
of a lamp upon it, small and steady gleam:
the Last Shrine of the Wayfarer, where the way
of shrines that runs from every road and stream
runs out. It has a roof, a lamp, a ledge.
It is the last word written at the edge.

He climbed up from the cove and poured the wine
and laid the voyage's first fish on the stone,
and the keeper came out, an acolyte, nine
and sixty years upon the road, alone
up there for twenty, and he asked the line
the keepers ask: What do you vow? Make it known.
And Lenthìze said: A shrine upon the far
shore, at my own cost, wherever the far shores are.

The keeper said: And if there is no shore?
This is the last shrine. Here the god's roads end.
I've kept the lamp up here for twenty years and more,
and some go past it, and the god will send
some home again, and of the rest I keep no store,
no count, no name, no word for them to mend.
And Lenthìze said: Then I will build it there,
out on the water, of whatever I can spare.

Past the Last Shrine the sea began to speak
the way a lady speaks from a high place,
a window on a square, her words oblique
behind a fan: his sail, his hands, his face,
the courage that it took for him to seek
her out, the honor of it, and the grace
with which he'd left that small and lesser shore.
Her voice was like a door. She was a door.

Come out to me, she said. Out here there's no
precedence, and no step where a man is placed,
no patron at the door, no debt to owe,
no roll to strike a name from, no disgraced
and no esteemed, no courtesy. Come. Go
where nothing's ordered. Have a single taste
of what it is to stand behind no one.
And he heard what she offered, and went on.

The headland settled down on the second day.
The clerk from Lunacòrte wrote it in his book:
Land out of sight. He wrote the date, the way
a notary would, and every morning took
the water's count, the biscuit's, and the grey
of the sky; and the student from the city would look
each night for the stars he knew, and find them there,
the same, the same, in empty-handed air.

On the eleventh day the Thunderer came
over the water and tore out the sail,
and took the hill man from Monvèrdre, the same
who'd never seen the sea, over the rail;
he saw it then. The Válaren man, his frame
bent over the canvas, sewed, and the gale
went on three days. The Solmère did not break.
She was well built. She was built for longing's sake.

Then calm, and calm. The sea lay flat as glass.
The sun stood on it, and the casks ran low.
The cooper knocked each cask and let it pass
along the deck to the clerk, who wrote it so,
and the cook's last peaches went round, and the last
of them went round, and every man ate slow.
And on the thirtieth day the clerk shut his book
and came aft with it, and gave the master a look.

Master, the clerk said, with the city's grace,
the compliment set first, as it is done,
no man aboard would give up his place
behind you, nor would any of us run.
But here's the count, which has no gift for praise
and owes no favors, and I've kept it. One
more day, and there is water for the way
back to the Shrine. Two days, and there's no day.

You are our patron, master. We're your train.
You know the order: first your house, and then
the man you stand behind, the city, plain
and last and owed, the clients. We're your men.
I've heard that order said and said again
since I could speak, by every tongue and pen,
and never once heard the far shore in the line.
We're in it, master. And he made no sign.

He looked a long time west, to where she lay
at her high window, with her fan, her cheek
turned to him; then he turned his own away
and looked at them, and was a while to speak.
Then: Bring her round, he said. The clerk is right. Say
we'll raise the Shrine by water's end. You keep
your places. I keep mine. The Solmère came round.
Behind, a window closed without a sound.

The west wind took them home. It took them east
nineteen days, and the casks were dry for two,
and on the twentieth, at dawn, the least
of lights, the Last Shrine's lamp, came into view,
and the keeper on the ledge, who had not ceased
to keep it, saw the sail and knew it, knew
the ship, and counted, as he counts each crew:
thirteen went out past him. Twelve came through.

They came in through the notch at the equinox,
the autumn one, when the god is paid what's owed.
He brought the Solmère in below the rocks
and set them on the quay, and named them, showed
them to the town, the way at a city's docks
a herald names an embassy, a load
of cities, then the men. And then he paid
each man his wage, and then again, and laid

the hill man's share in the priest of Sélmoros's hand
to carry up to the hills, and then once more
he paid the clerk, for the counting, and the stand
he took. That is the end the southern shore
will sing you, where the patrons pay. The land
holds them; the order's kept. But Portvent's store
of the song goes on, and Portvent pays the rest:
five stanzas more, and the last ones are the best.

He gave the god the wine and silver due
for a man come home, and told the priests his way;
the old priest drew it, as the priests all do,
a line out from the Last Shrine, west, that lay
across the empty page and stopped. Then, through
the temple's quiet, Lenthìze heard him say:
You vowed a shrine as well. You owe it still.
I know, said Lenthìze. And I know I will.

That winter he was home. The salt pans lay
under the rain; the wind came off the sea
the way it does; he mended nets all day
in his father's yard; and Aulìra, who was free
to ask, asked nothing. That's the Portvent way
with what is settled: let the settling be.
She sang the songs the salt pans know, at night,
and he heard every word, and heard it right.

The spring came, and the day the ships are blessed;
the priest came down the cut to Portvent's strand
and oiled the brows, and Lenthìze, with the rest,
said the whole oath, and took the staff in hand,
and did not slow on the small word. Then west,
that evening, on the land wind, off the land,
his father's skiff went out through the notch, one sail,
one man. Aulìra's lamp. No wave. No hail.

The keeper of the Last Shrine, the third night,
saw a small sail put in below the ledge,
and a man climb up and pour the wine, and light
his lamp from the shrine's lamp, and make his pledge
again: a shrine, his own cost. In whose sight?
There is no shore, the keeper said. The edge
is water. Then on water. In the morning, gone,
west. The keeper wrote it down: out, one. In, none.

The fourth song ends. There is no fifth song made.
The keepers at the Last Shrine keep the book
open at his line still, and none has laid
a hand to close it; every keeper took
the count on from the last, and none has weighed
the in against the out. And if you look
at Portvent's cut tonight, a lamp, a light,
Aulìra's lamp, is standing on the height.

Go, song, to my lord in Portvent, who has paid
for wine and lamp and singer, as is owed;
tell him his train is fed, his clients stayed,
and the Last Shrine stands at the end of the road.
```
