**How the South Bought Longing** is told in the houses of the southern coast on the night of the Festival of the Hearth, the winter solstice, when a flame is carried from the forge of the [[affiliation-sacredforge|Sacred Forge]]'s temple to light every household's fire. The household waits in the dark with its hearth laid and cold, and while the taper with the temple's flame burns in the eldest's hand the eldest tells this tale, and the fire is lit at its end. It is a tale for the youngest in the house, and the eldest tells it the way it was told to them, in the same words where the words are fixed.

It tells how the word _solmere_, longing, came into the tongue of the south. The tongue had words for every having and none for this, and it took the word from the north, as it took its words for light, dream and song ([[skill-provnzlng|Provenzal]]). The tale gives the reason for that, and the price. It has [[affiliation-olvenia|Ólvenia]] the Bountiful, the best loved of the gods in the south, keep [[affiliation-pozeras|Pozeras]] the Wayfarer at her table for a summer and lose him at the equinox. It explains, in passing, why the temples of the two gods stand side by side in the trading cities, and why the Golden Harvest is the last feast before the autumn equinox. The envoy who goes north for the word is called **Felze**, which is the word for an envoy. The tale says he paid for the word with his own name.

: How the South Bought Longing {#tale}

Hold the taper steady. No, give it to me. Sit there where you can see the flame. It came up from the Forge tonight, from the anvil-fire, through the whole city, hand to hand, and it is not going in the hearth till this is told, so sit.

In the first days, when Ólvenia the Bountiful first walked in the south, she filled it. You know what she put in it, because you have eaten it. She put the vine on the hills and the oil in the olive and the fish in the warm water and the salt on the flats at Lizela. She put the market in the square and the scale in the market and the seal on the scale. She put silver in the hills and gold in the purse and the purse on the belt and the belt on the merchant, and the merchant at her altar the morning after a good bargain, with a gift. Ólvenia the Bountiful, who has everything, and gives it.

And the south learned to speak of what she gave it. The south had a word for every having. It had a word for buying and a word for selling, a word for the price, a word for what is lent and a word for what the lending costs, which is _fezumal_, interest; a word for the debt in money and a word for the debt in favor, which is to stand under a man, _orfeate_. It had a word for keeping, and a word for the one who keeps a house, and a word for the dole that the city keeps for the poor. Every word the south had been a word for having something, or giving it, or owing it, or getting it back. That was how rich the south was. It had no need of any other kind of word.

Now hear who came down the road.

In the spring, at the equinox, the Wayfarer came down the great road from the east, with his staff and his serpents and his crown that points the way, the way he comes, the way he always comes, with dust on his feet and no baggage. And he stopped at the door of Ólvenia the Bountiful, who has everything, because hers was the best door on the road and he was a guest, and a guest stops at the best door.

She gave him the welcome. She gave him the room at the top of the house, the one that looks at the sea. She gave him the place at the table that a guest has, the high place, worked out before anyone sat, and she sat him there and filled his cup herself. Whether he was her guest and no more than her guest, the tale does not say. The tale is courteous.

He stayed the summer. That is the first thing you must remember. He stayed the whole of a summer, which he had never done at anyone's door. He sat at her table and told her the roads, all the roads, the road over the mountains to the east and the road along the sea and the road into the north where the trees are older than the roads. She told him the market and the price of everything in it. He made her laugh, and she made him stay, and they ate peaches off the same plate at the end of every evening, and the whole south was happy that summer, because when Ólvenia is happy the whole south is fat.

And at the end of summer, the last feast before the equinox, she gave the greatest feast the south had ever seen, three days of it, the music and the wine and the shops shut and the doors open, and the Wayfarer danced at it.

That is the Golden Harvest. That is why we keep it. It is the summer he stayed.

And then it was the equinox, the autumn one, when the dark and the light are even, and the Wayfarer got up from the table before it was light, and took his staff, and put the dust back on his feet, and went. He did not do it to be cruel. You must understand that. The road is what he is. A Wayfarer who stays is a guest who has become a lodger, and he is not a lodger. He kissed her at the door, and he paid his guest-right with his thanks, which is all a guest owes, and he went up the road, and she watched him go until the dust he went in was gone.

That is the second thing you must remember. He went at the equinox.

Ólvenia the Bountiful, who has everything, went back into her house. And her house had everything in it. The table was there, and the cups, and the room at the top of the house that looks at the sea, and the plate they had eaten peaches off. Nothing was missing from the house. She went through it and counted, the way she counts a merchant's gift, and nothing was missing. And there was something in her that was not a having. It sat in her where a having sits, and it was not one. It was the other way round from a having. And she had no word for it.

She went down to the market, because the market has a word for everything. She asked the merchants.

The first merchant said: Lady, it is a loss. Write it in the book as a loss and close the column.

And she said: No. A loss is a thing that was mine and is gone. He was never mine. He was my guest. It is not a loss.

The second merchant said: Lady, it is a debt. He has gone off owing you a summer's board. Send a man after him with the bill.

And she said: No. He paid his guest-right with his thanks, which is all a guest owes. He owes me nothing. It is not a debt.

The third merchant said: Lady, it is an empty place. There is a place at your table, and nobody in it. Put somebody in it, a guest is a guest, and the place will be full.

And she said: No. I could fill the place tonight from the square, and the thing would still be there. It is not a place. It is not a loss, it is not a debt and it is not a place. You have no word for it.

And the merchants were ashamed, because they had a word for everything, and they had not got this one.

So Ólvenia the Bountiful went up the hill to Marendas, the Keeper of Sacred Wisdom, at her academy, where all the words are kept. And Marendas heard her out, and went into her archive, and was a long time in it, and came back with nothing in her hands.

And Marendas said: Bountiful One, I have a word for everything that can be known. This cannot be known. It can only be had-not. And the south has no word for having-not, because the south has always had. You made it so yourself. You filled it.

And Ólvenia said: Then where is the word?

And Marendas said: In the north. Go north, over the hills, to the country where the trees are older than the roads and the light stays long in the summer and short in the winter. The people there have words for what they have not got, because the north has had to do without. They have a word for every kind of light, because they lose it every winter. Ask them.

That is the third thing you must remember. The south had to ask.

Now Ólvenia the Bountiful does not travel. She is the one who stays. The Wayfarer goes, and she stays, and the merchants come to her; that is her nature, as the road is his. So she could not go north herself, and she came down the hill and stood in the square and asked for an envoy.

And a young man of the first city stepped out of the crowd. He was a merchant's son and no great thing, and he had a good voice and a good head for a price, and he said: Lady, I'll go. And she gave him a purse of gold to buy the word with, and a letter in her own hand, and her seal, and he went north up the great road.

His name was something. The tale has lost it. The tale calls him Felze, which is only the word for an envoy, a man sent from one city to another. You will see why the tale lost his name.

He went up the road the Wayfarer had gone. He passed the Wayfarer's shrines, one after another, and left a coin at each. He went up out of the vines and into the hills and over the hills, and the air got thin and cold, and the trees got old, and the road got narrow and then was a path, and then he was in the north.

And at a lake in the hills, a mere, which is a word he brought back with the other, he found the singers of the north sitting on the shore. They sang. That is all they were doing. They had no market and no scale and no seal, and the light on the water was going from gold to grey, and they had a word for that, and they were singing it.

Felze stood in front of them and made them the compliments. You know how. He praised their lake and their trees and their singing and the long light, and he told them who had sent him and showed them the seal, and then he said: My lady has a thing in her and no word for it, and I am sent to buy the word. Name your price.

And the singers laughed at him. They did not laugh unkindly. They laughed the way you laugh at a child who brings you a coin for the sunset.

And the eldest of the singers said: We do not sell words. A word is not a thing in a purse. We will tell it to you. But you will not be able to carry it.

And Felze said, because he was a merchant's son: Tell it to me, and I will carry it as I carry anything. I have carried gold up a mountain.

And the eldest singer told him the word. And Felze said it back to them. And he said it right, every sound, and he knew what it was a word for, as you know what salt is a word for; and still he did not have it. It was a word in his mouth and nothing in him, like a coin of a country he had never been to.

And the eldest singer said: There. You see. You cannot carry the word. You can only carry the thing, and the word comes with it. And you cannot have the thing until you have something to have-not. You came here with everything. You are from the south.

And Felze, who was no fool, said: Then what is your price?

And the singer said: Stay. Stay a year with us, at our lake, under our trees, in our long light and our short light. Then go home. And the word will go with you, because you will have the thing.

That is the price the north asked. That is the fourth thing.

So he stayed. He stayed through the autumn, and the winter, when the light went short and the singers had a word for that, and he learned it. He stayed through the spring, when the light came back, and he learned the word for that. He learned their word for a dream and their word for a shadow and their word for a spirit and their word for the art, and their word for song, and he learned to sing. He stayed so long and learned so much that he brought back more words than he was sent for, which is what happens to an envoy who stays too long, and which is why your grandfather's songs are full of the north.

And at the end of the year, at the equinox, the autumn one, he said goodbye to them at the mere. The eldest singer kissed him at the shore, and he went down the path and onto the road and over the hills, and he looked back from the top of the last hill at the lake, and the light on it was going from gold to grey.

And he had it. There, on the hill. He had the thing, and the word came up in his mouth by itself, as a word does when you have the thing it is for. He did not have to remember it. He said it to the lake, and nobody heard him.

He came home down the great road into the south, out of the cold and into the vines, and it was the equinox again, a year to the day from when the Wayfarer went, and he went to the door of Ólvenia the Bountiful, who has everything, and knocked.

She opened it herself.

He did not make her the compliments. He had forgotten how, the tale says, or he did not want to. He said: Lady, I have it. And he said the word.

_Solmere._

And Ólvenia the Bountiful heard it, and knew it, the way you know your own name when someone calls it across the square. It was the word for the thing in her that was not a having. She had had it a year and a day, and now it had a name, and it sat in her where it had sat, but now she could say what it was. And she wept, which she had not done before, because the south had had no word for the thing that weeping is for.

And she paid him. She paid him what she had promised, and more, the purse of gold and the purse again, and a house in the city and a place at her table. That is how she is. The Bountiful One always pays.

But he could not stay at her table. That is the end of it, nearly. He had brought the word home, and to bring the word he had had to carry the thing, and he was carrying it still. It was the lake. Every evening at her table, when the light on the sea went from gold to grey, he said the word, and it was not for her. He stood on the great road at the edge of the city in the evenings and looked north. He had paid for the word with his name, which nobody remembers, and with the thing, which he never put down. That is why the tale calls him the envoy and nothing else. He spent his whole name on it.

Now you know the price. Every one of us who says the word pays a little of what it cost him. You cannot say _solmere_ and mean it about a thing you have. It is a word you can only say about what is gone, or away, or north, or out at sea, or under the ground, and it costs you a little every time, and the south has been paying it ever since, a little at a time. That is the interest. The south bought the word on credit, and it is still paying.

And what did the Bountiful One do, with the word in her and the Wayfarer gone up the road?

She built her house against his. You have seen it. Go down to the harbor any day and look. In the trading cities the Bountiful One's temple and the Wayfarer's stand side by side, and some of them share a wall, and in some there is a door in the wall. Ólvenia built them so. She is the one who stays and he is the one who goes, and she cannot keep him and she will not lose him, so she put her house against his, so that the one who stays and the one who goes have a wall between them and not a road. When the merchants go out at the spring equinox they go to his door for the Blessing. When they come home at the autumn equinox they go to his door to say they came, and then through the wall to hers with the gift. That is how she has arranged it. That is what having-not is for, when you are a goddess. You build.

And does he come back?

He comes back every spring. Down the great road, at the equinox, with dust on his feet. Every spring. And he stays the summer, some years, and some years he does not, and she gives the Harvest every year whether he is there or not. And every autumn he goes. The tale does not say she has stopped minding. It says she has the word for it.

That is the tale.

Now. Look at the taper. That flame came from the anvil-fire tonight, up from the Forge, through the whole city, hand to hand to hand. Every year the hearth goes out, and every year the fire is fetched. It does not come by itself. Somebody goes for it and carries it home. That is how a house knows that what goes away can be brought back, if somebody goes and fetches it.

Before it goes in the hearth, say the word once, all of you, for the ones who are not here. Your uncle at sea. Your grandmother on the road outside the wall. Your cousin at the academy, who writes. Say it once, and pay for them.

_Solmere._

Good. Now the fire.
