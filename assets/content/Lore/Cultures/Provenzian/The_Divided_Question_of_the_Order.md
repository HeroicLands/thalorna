**The Divided Question of the Order** is a debate in song between two síladors, made at a patron's table in [[place-kalveza|Kalveza]] on the second night of the [Festival of the Golden Harvest]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} of [[affiliation-olvenia|Ólvenia]], when patrons commission singers for their tables and the temple squares. In a divided question one singer sets a question with two answers and gives the other the choice; the other chooses an answer and defends it, and the first must defend the one left over, however little he believes it. They sing in turn, a stanza each, and close with a short stanza apiece that names the judge they agreed on before they began.

[Serdale]{.name data-type="being/npc"} sets the question and [Iltore]{.name data-type="being/npc"} chooses. The question is the one the order of duty leaves open. A Provenzian owes his house, his patron, his city and his clients, in that order, and the beloved of the courtly songs stands nowhere in it. The judge is [Solvima]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of Kalveza, who keeps her own name. She gives her judgment in the form a notary uses for an award, as the custom of these debates allows, and the songbooks copy it after the song. The academies of rhetoric teach the debate for Serdale's arguments and the judgment for its form.

The poem is rhymed ABABCC.

: The Question {#question}

::: {.verse form=divided-question lang=en work=question}
*SERDALE*/
Iltore, here's a question for the feast:/
choose which you'll hold, and I will hold the other./
The order runs: your house, your patron, your city, and least/
and last and owed, your clients; ask your mother./
Where does she stand: before them all, the first?/
Or on no step at all? Choose. I'll take the worst.

*ILTORE*/
I take the first: she stands before my line./
My house named me, and the city has the name./
She named me too, with no seal and no sign;/
no clerk has entered it; no herald came/
to cry it. My house named a child at birth./
She named the man. Judge which name has the worth.

*SERDALE*/
Then I take nowhere, and I'll make it good./
The order is a list of what we owe,/
and what we owe gets paid, as debtors should,/
and what is paid is settled, and can go:/
the client takes his release and walks away./
Put her upon the list, and one day you will pay.

*ILTORE*/
You'd keep her off the list? Then she's unplaced,/
the worst word that we have. You'd leave her there/
outside the room, where the stranger waits, disgraced,/
the man with no patron, standing on the stair,/
while the room is ordered. I would seat her first./
You'd leave her with the unplaced. Which is worse?

*SERDALE*/
Not with the unplaced. There are three the list/
has never held, and never will: the sea;/
the dead, the [[lore-selmorosdty|Judge]]'s, whom the clerks have missed/
on purpose; and the one you love. These three/
no steward seats; no herald ever could./
I keep her with the sea and the dead, for good.

*ILTORE*/
The dead are placed. The [[affiliation-selmoros|Silent Judge]]'s priest/
will place the dead of any field, for a fee;/
that is the rite. So strike the dead, at least./
That leaves your lady out there with the sea,/
which takes, and gives back salt, and pays no debt./
You've seated her with what's not been placed yet.

*SERDALE*/
Then yes, I seat her with the sea. And you,/
who seat her first: the day your house sends down/
to the notary with a contract, as houses do,/
a marriage with a good house of the town,/
who's first? Your lady, or the seal? Your list says so./
Your house says otherwise. Which will you keep? I know.

*ILTORE*/
I'll keep her first, and let the seal go cold./
Let my house strike me; let the herald cry/
my unnaming in the square; let it be told/
in every hall; and when they ask me why,/
I'll say: strike every roll, and I'm still placed./
I stand on her step. Yours stands in the waste.

*SERDALE*/
Lady Solvima, judge: he's won the song;/
I've won the case. Weigh both. You'll not go wrong.

*ILTORE*/
Lady Solvima, judge: his case is sound,/
and his lady's at sea. Mine stands on dry ground.
:::

: The Judgment {#judgment}

::: {.verse form=prose lang=en work=judgment}
Heard at Kalveza, in the house of the patron who gave the table, on the second night of the Festival of the Golden Harvest, before the company, by Solvima of Kalveza, chosen judge by both parties and sitting at their joint request.

The parties are Serdale and Iltore, síladors. The question is where the beloved stands in the order of what a man owes, which runs: his house, his patron, his city and his clients. Iltore holds that she stands first, before the house. Serdale holds that she stands nowhere in it.

The judge finds, first, that Serdale has recited the order correctly, and is right that whatever stands in it may be paid and released, and that what may be released may be lost. She finds the argument good and the conclusion cold.

She finds, second, that Iltore has argued that a name given in the dark outranks a name given at birth, and has offered to be unnamed by his city in order to keep it. She finds the offer handsome and notes that it has not been asked of him.

She finds, third, that Iltore is right that the dead are placed, since the priests of Sélmoros place them for a fee, and that Serdale has therefore left his lady with the sea alone. She finds that Serdale has not answered this, and that he has asked a better question in reply, which Iltore has answered with his heart and not with his house.

She finds, fourth, that neither party has asked the beloved where she stands. The beloved is not a party to the order and has not been called. The question is therefore improperly put, and no judgment between the parties can settle it.

She awards the song to Iltore and the case to Serdale, the cup to neither and the wine to the company. Each party shall pay the costs in a song before the festival closes.

The judge records, as notice and not as judgment, that she has kept her own name for forty years in a house of Kalveza that is not her father's, and that she stands where she pleases.
:::

solvima ol kalveza tavete de ir kalveza, ir dumva ol daimela, dene demate satore, ir etore navena ol lordeme ol elteze cholsama, pral trameze. tavemer panize tavemkurete dena fa nasde, ul dena ombete fa petandon ol tavemer.

panize ez serdale ul iltore, sourime. petandon ez sedon ol dameza ir ziromon ol jerval ol orvene, ul ziromon ez dumva ol dene, daimela ol dene, veramis ol dene ul liskonar ol dene. iltore taurate, dena talete olime, pral dumva. serdale taurate, dena ar kerate sedon ir letima.

nasde nasdate olimai, serdale lorezate ziromon sa nasdele fente, ul dene ez nasdele, laton ir de ziromon panize dunate frenzate ul liskate, ul laton ol liskaima dene dunate analvete. verdane ez daje. ortemandon ez nelve.

dena nasdate etorai, iltore vakate verdane, arlen ol olte ez telvare us arlen ol nurezandon, ul dene pubrate nearle ol veramis ol dene ta dene taurate de arlen. dena nasdate, pubrandon ez dajande, ul lendate denon, arurkis vereze petate dene fa denon.

dena nasdate karzore, iltore ez nasdele, cholvaze ez zirote, oleti sarcheande ol sélmoros zirotate denoi fa verdavis, ul fa denon serdale linkate dameza ol dene talv ornete urtai. serdale ar petrete de verdane, naren dene petate dajaze petandon, ul iltore petrete denon talv netora, ul ar talv dumva.

dena nasdate ir sedon ol felzur, ar urkis ol tavemer petate dameza fa sedon ol dena. dameza ar ez panize ol ziromon, ul arurkis vereze klamete dena. fa denon petandon ar ez nasdele, ul nasdandon tavir panize ar dunate ortemate denon.

dena demate olnare fir iltore, krezandon fir serdale, irkon ar fir urkis ol tavemer, ul jentize fir trameze. urzele panize jervete frenzate frenza talv olnare pral ortemandon ol lordeme.

nasde lendate denon fa chesdande, ul ar fa nasdandon. dena taurate arleis ol dena felzur umbaris tarnela ir dumva ol kalveza, ul dumva ar ez ol elte ol dena, ul dena talete ir sedon ol avandon ol dena.
