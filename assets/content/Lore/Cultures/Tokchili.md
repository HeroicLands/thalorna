Every household of the One Fire puts its hearth out once a year. On the morning of the New Fire, in the heat of midsummer when the first green maize is ready, the women rake the old ashes out of every house in every town from the river to the gulf, and runners bring a coal down from the fire on the great mound at [[place-hayopokta|Hayo Pokta]]. A household that takes the coal belongs to the [[affiliation-tokchiaska|Tokchi Aska]], the One Fire, for another year. A town that refuses it has left.

That is the whole of the nation, and the whole of its trouble. The Tokchili, the people of the fire, believe that the fire was carried down from the sun, and that a Sun—a member of the paramount house that claims the sun as its ancestor—keeps it on the great mound for everyone. When a Great Sun is strong and the harvests are good, towns ask for his coal and send their maize, their earth and their young men in return. When he fails, they light their hearths from someone else's fire, and the nation that looked like a kingdom comes apart into a hundred towns. The Tokchili call the first state a Gathering and the second a Scattering. They have lived through two Scatterings that they remember and are living now in the third Gathering, and every one of them argues about why the second Scattering came.

## The Three Gatherings

The story the Keepers of the Fire recite at the New Fire begins with [Ichasa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a woman of the river who climbed to the sun and came down carrying a coal in a pot of wet clay. The river was in flood when she came back, and she had her brothers carry earth in baskets until there was a hill above the water to set the fire on. That hill is [Pokta Hoshalo]{.name data-type="place/site"}, the Old Mound, upriver of Hayo Pokta, and the Suns are her daughters' children. Whatever happened in the deep past, the Old Mound is real: a grassed hill with oaks growing out of its summit, surrounded by the smaller mounds of a city that nobody has lived in for longer than the Keepers can count.

The **First Gathering** was the city around the Old Mound. The Keepers say it lasted forty Suns and ended in floods and a war with the people of the plains, and they say little else, because the line of Suns they recite from it is mostly names.

The **Second Gathering** is the one the Tokchili argue about. Its city stood on the river a day's paddle below Hayo Pokta, and at its height it held more people than the present seat. Its last strong Great Sun, [Lapali]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, ruled for forty years, won the coastal provinces by war and took their tribute in canoes, shell and young people. When he died, the Keepers buried him with the largest following any Sun has had: his wives, his retainers, his war chiefs who volunteered, and fifty-three young women of the coast towns, sent up the river as tribute and strangled on the mound so that the Great Sun would go to the sky attended. The count, with the volunteers, came to a hundred and eight. The Tokchili call it the [Funeral of the Hundred]{.name data-type="lore/history"}.

The coast never sent another canoe. Within ten years the shell towns had stopped paying, the eastern towns had lit their fires from a rival Sun on their own river, and the plains bands were raiding a valley no Great Sun could defend. Within a generation the city was empty. That was the **Scattering**, three lifetimes of small towns, each with its own fire and its own war.

The **Third Gathering** began with [Tamoska]{.name data-type="being/npc"} the Gatherer, a Sun of a minor line, and his mother [Hoyani]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the Sun Mother who chose him. They built Hayo Pokta on a new site, raised its great mound in one lifetime, and won back the coast without a war. Hoyani went down the river herself to the shell towns and made the [Shell Promise]{.name data-type="lore/law"} with their chiefs: no person of the coast will ever again be given to a Sun's funeral, and the shell towns will send their tribute in shell, fish and canoes and never in people. The coast took the coal again. The Third Gathering has lasted five Great Suns since, and the present Great Sun, [Opahiko]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, is old.

## Keeping the Fire Lit

Good is what keeps the fire lit, and evil is what lets it go out. The fire is meant literally, since a household that lets its hearth die between New Fires borrows a coal from a neighbor and is ashamed, and it is meant for everything a fire stands for. A good person feeds the people who depend on him, carries his share of earth to the mound, keeps his word to his clan, plays the stone game honestly, and fights when the town is attacked. A good chief keeps the granary full, the mound in repair and the town at peace.

Evil is waste and breaking. Stealing from a granary is worse than stealing from a house, because the granary feeds everyone in a bad year. Killing a clansman, breaking a marriage the clans arranged, or cheating at the stone game are all evils of the same kind: each breaks a bond that holds a town together. The worst thing a person can do is to put out another's fire, which in the Tokchili sense means leaving a household or a town without the means to live.

The Keepers add a second account. The fire came from the sun, and the sun is owed respect: the first maize, the first fish of a season, the morning greeting, and the attendance of the dead. To them impiety is an evil in itself, and a town that neglects its fire is inviting a Scattering.

## A House on the Mound

Success is a house on a mound. The Suns live on the great mound, the Nobles on the lesser mounds around the plaza, and the Mound Chief of each town on that town's mound, and everyone below them measures success by how close to the mound they stand. An ordinary farmer succeeds by keeping his household fed, his clan's fields sown and his name clear, and by earning a war name or a place on the mound's work crew. The real success open to a commoner is to be raised: a man who takes a captive in war, saves a Sun's life or wins the town's game in a famous match may be made an _amosi_ (Honored), eat apart from commoners at feasts and marry into a Noble line.

Failure is falling. A noble who loses a war, a game or a law dispute loses standing, and a commoner who cannot meet his share of maize or earth puts his household's place in debt. The final failure is to be left without a fire: a household that no town will give a coal to has nowhere in the nation to live.

## What Is Worth Carrying

The Tokchili most value the labor that builds something lasting. The great mound is the clearest case, raised basket by basket over a lifetime by people whose names nobody remembers, and the plain word for a good person is _amokilo_, a basket-carrier: someone who carries earth for a mound he will not live on. They value generosity, especially a chief's, since a chief who feeds his town in a bad year is remembered for generations. They value skill, and the copper-workers, shell-engravers and potters of Hayo Pokta are fed by the Great Sun so that they can work all year. They value composure: a Sun is never seen to hurry, and a man who loses his temper at the stone yard loses more than his wager.

They least value hoarding and loud claims. A noble who keeps maize in his own storehouse while his town is hungry is despised, and so is anyone who boasts of a deed before it is done. A promise made at the stone yard binds, so a man who wagers what he cannot pay is a figure of contempt.

## The Basket-Carrier

The most admired man of the One Fire is no Sun. He is a commoner who carries his share and more, fights well, keeps his temper at the game and dies with his debts paid. The Tokchili name him in the story of [Pakalo]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a war chief of the Second Gathering who was raised to Honored for taking captives in three wars. When Lapali died, Pakalo was named among those who would walk with the Great Sun to the sky, an honor that would raise his sister's children for three generations. He refused it. He said on the mound, before the Keepers, that a Sun who needs a war chief in the sky had not used one well on earth, and that night he took a canoe and nine of the coast women down the river, and only three were caught. The Keepers recite him as a traitor. Commoners and the shell towns tell the story as the best thing anyone did that year.

The admired man shares what made Pakalo admired. He works without being told. He keeps his word to his sister's children, who are his heirs, before his own pleasures. He fights for the town and not for loot. He wins at the stone yard without gloating and loses without complaint. And he will say no to a Sun when a Sun is wrong, once, plainly, and take the consequence.

## The Sun Mother

The most admired woman is a mother of a house, and the model is Hoyani. A woman of the Tokchili owns her house, her fields and her children, who belong to her clan and not their father's. The admired woman runs a household that never runs short, judges her daughters' marriages well, keeps the peace among her sisters and settles quarrels before they reach the Mound Chief. In old age she becomes the voice of her clan, and the clan's men do not go to war or to a council without her opinion.

Hoyani was all that at the top of the nation. As Sun Mother she chose her own son over two nephews with stronger claims, because she judged him the one who would not repeat Lapali's funeral. She then went to the coast in person, a journey no Sun had made in peace, sat at the Shell Chiefs' fires, ate their fish and made the Shell Promise. The Tokchili remember her as the woman who made a Gathering without a war. A daughter told to be like Hoyani is being told to be shrewd, to choose people well and to make peace that lasts.

## What the Towns Keep

The One Fire writes nothing, so its sacred things are objects and recitations.

- **The fire on the great mound.** The temple on the summit of the great mound at Hayo Pokta keeps a fire that is never allowed to go out. The Keepers say it is the coal Ichasa brought down, fed without a break since. Every household fire in the nation is lit from it at the New Fire.
- **The line of the Suns.** The Keepers recite the names of every Great Sun since Ichasa at each New Fire, with the length of his reign and the deed he is remembered for. A Sun left out of the recitation has been erased. Lapali is still recited, and his deed is recited as the Funeral of the Hundred.
- **The Shell Promise.** No object carries it. The Shell Chiefs recite it at every New Fire in their towns, word for word, before they take the coal, and a Keeper of Hayo Pokta has to stand by and say it is still kept.
- **The copper plates.** Each Sun's house keeps embossed copper plates showing the falcon dancer, the bird-man with wings spread and a war club in his hand. The plates are the Suns' standing made visible and are buried with them.
- **The town mound.** Every town's mound is its charter. A town with a mound has a fire and a chief; a town without one is a hamlet of the town whose mound it carries earth to.

## The Fire Every Day

An ordinary person's religion is daily and small. At sunrise the head of each household steps out of the door, faces east and greets the sun, and the household's first act is to feed the fire before anyone eats. The first maize of the season, the first fish and the first deer are given to the fire, and a little of every meal goes into it.

Men drink the black drink, a bitter tea of holly leaves brewed strong, before councils and before war, and vomit it up to come to the council clean. A man who cannot hold it down long enough for the speeches is mocked.

Most days the town's real worship is work on the mound. Every household owes its town so many baskets of earth a year, an _amoka_ at a time, and the work crews go up the ramp in the cool of the morning singing. The labor is a tax, a prayer and a social occasion at once, and a household that falls behind on its earth is talked about.

Twice a month, at the full and the dark of the moon, the Keepers hold rites on the great mound, open to anyone in the plaza below.

## The Year

The year is counted from New Fire to New Fire.

- **The New Fire,** _Tokchi Falisi_, at midsummer when the first green maize is ready. For four days every hearth is out. People fast, men drink the black drink, houses are swept and broken pots thrown out. Quarrels are settled and every crime short of murder is forgiven. On the fourth morning the Keepers light the new fire on the great mound, runners carry coals to every town, and the first green maize is eaten. The New Fire is what renews the nation: a town that takes the coal is in for the year.
- **The Planting,** in spring, when each clan's women sow its fields and the Mound Chief sows a field for the granary that the whole town works.
- **The Stone Season,** after the harvest, when the towns play the rolling-stone game against one another and the great matches are played at Hayo Pokta before the Great Sun. It is the busiest season of wagering, marriage-making and quiet politics.
- **The Shell Fair,** in late winter on the coast, when the heartland's traders go down the river with maize, copper and pottery and come back with shell, dried fish and carved wood.
- **A Sun's funeral,** when a Sun dies. A Great Sun's funeral stops the nation for a month.

## The Rolling-Stone Game

The _sakkowa_ is played on a long yard of beaten clay at the edge of the plaza. Two players run side by side. One bowls a polished stone disk the size of a hand ahead of them, and as it rolls each throws a long pole at where he thinks the disk will stop. The pole that lands nearer wins the point. The disks are owned by towns and handed down, and a famous one has a name.

The game is spectacle, gambling and politics. Whole towns travel to watch. Wagers run from a basket of maize to a copper plate to a man's own freedom, since a man who stakes himself and loses becomes his winner's servant until he is redeemed. Nobles settle rivalries through their champions, and a Mound Chief whose town loses the great match at Hayo Pokta comes home weaker. The Keeper of the Yard who holds the wagers and judges the throws is one of the most powerful people in any town, and one of the most often accused of being bought.

## Raised and Put Out

Rewards, from least to greatest:

1. **Praise at the fire,** a deed told aloud at a feast.
2. **A war name,** given for a first captive or a first kill in a just war, which replaces a man's child name.
3. **A gift from a chief,** a copper ornament, an engraved gorget or a share of the granary.
4. **Raising,** to _amosi_ (Honored), with its seat apart at feasts and its right to marry into a Noble line.
5. **A place in a mound,** to be buried in the flank of a town's mound, which the Tokchili believe sends the dead up with the sun.

Punishments, from least to greatest:

1. **Shaming,** a public scolding by the clan's elder women.
2. **Payment,** maize, copper or labor owed to the injured clan, set by the Mound Chief.
3. **Lowering,** the loss of a war name or of Honored standing.
4. **Putting out,** the gravest punishment short of death. The household's fire is put out and no town will give it a coal; the person must leave the nation or be taken as a servant.
5. **Death,** for murder that the victim's clan will not take payment for, and for treason against a Sun. The victim's clan carries it out.

The worst fate of all is to be made a captive and given to a Sun's funeral, and that is no lawful punishment at all. It falls on _ilampo_ (captives) taken in war, on servants who staked themselves at the game, and once on the coast.

## The Sun's Word and the Keepers'

The Great Sun is both king and high priest, and the One Fire has no notion that the two could be separate. His authority is the fire: he keeps it, and the towns that take it owe him. He settles disputes between towns, names the Mound Chiefs and the war chiefs, keeps the great granary, and leads the New Fire.

He does not keep the fire with his own hands. The Keepers of the Fire, a hereditary priesthood of Noble rank, live on the great mound, feed the fire, keep the temple where the bones of dead Suns are kept, recite the line of the Suns and conduct every funeral. The Great Sun cannot hold the New Fire without them, and they cannot choose a Great Sun. The Sun Mother chooses him, from among her sons and her sisters' sons. The three powers check one another. A Great Sun who quarrels with the Keepers finds the omens bad and the rites slow; Keepers who quarrel with the Sun Mother find their candidate passed over.

Below them, the Mound Chief of each town tries cases between its clans, sets payments and collects the town's maize and earth. The clan mothers settle what they can before it reaches him. The Keepers judge oaths and sacrilege. Wagers go to the Keeper of the Yard. Nobody writes down a judgment: the Keepers of each town remember what was decided, and a dispute between two memories goes up the river to Hayo Pokta.

## Up with the Sun or Down with the River

The Tokchili believe that the dead go one of two ways. The dead who are buried high, in a mound or on its flank, go up with the sun each morning and live in its light. The dead who are buried low, in a field or a riverbank, go down with the water to the country under the river, which is cold, dim and hungry. Suns go up as a matter of course, attended by those who die with them. Everyone else has to earn the high burial.

That belief builds the mounds. A household's earth-work for its town's mound buys its dead a place in the flank, and a town's Mound Chief keeps the account of who has carried enough. People who cannot carry, the old and the sick, have kin carry for them. A family spends its labor on the mound before its own house, and a widow who has kept up her husband's share can bury him high even if she has nothing else. A commoner raised to Honored is buried on the mound itself.

The same belief makes the funerals. A Sun's attendants go up with him and stay in the light, and the Keepers teach that the households of those who walk with a Sun are raised: a _tiwilo_'s sister's children become Honored for three generations. That is why there are always volunteers. A retainer with a failing body and many nieces can give them something no lifetime of carrying could, and some do it gladly. Captives are not asked.

Grave robbery from a mound is the one crime every Tokchili agrees deserves death, since it sends someone's dead down to the river.

## Feeding the Dead

The dead can be reached, and the living owe them food. At the New Fire each household sets a bowl of the first green maize on its dead's grave, or at the foot of the mound if the dead are in its flank. A dead person who is not fed is believed to come back hungry, in dreams, in illness among children, or in a fire that will not catch.

The Keepers carry messages both ways. A household troubled by its dead pays a Keeper in maize or copper to speak at the temple fire, where the dead Suns' bones are kept, and the dead Suns pass word to the lesser dead. A Keeper who reports that a dead mother is angry about an unfed grave can also name the price of making it right. Commoners grumble that the dead are always hungry when the Keepers' storehouse is low, and they pay anyway.

## What a Person Owes

A Tokchili adult can recite it:

> **I owe** my town my share of maize, my baskets of earth for the mound, and my body in war. I owe my clan my labor, my obedience to its mothers, and my care for my sister's children. I owe the fire its first food and the sun its greeting.
>
> **I am owed** a coal at the New Fire, seed from the granary in a bad year, the town's warriors when I am attacked, a judgment when I am wronged, and a high grave when I have carried enough.

The Great Sun owes the same, raised: the nation's fire kept, the great granary full, the towns defended, and a funeral that does not cost the towns more than they can bear. That last clause is not recited, and it is the one the nation argues about.

## The Mothers' Houses

The weak are first the clan's business, and the clan is the mother's line. A widow stays in her own house, which was hers before she married, and her brothers and sisters' sons work her fields. An orphan belongs to its mother's clan and goes to its mother's sister, who calls it her own child, since in Tokchili one word serves for mother and mother's sister. A destitute household is fed by its clan, and when the clan cannot, the Mound Chief opens the town granary. It is a duty, owed because the household carried its share in better years. A stranger with no clan is fed once as a guest and then must find a household to take him in as a servant.

## Those Who Cannot Carry

A person injured or sick past working is fed by the clan, and a warrior hurt in the town's war is fed from the town granary for the rest of his life; feeding him is the Mound Chief's visible duty, and a chief who neglects it loses men at the next muster. Kin carry the injured person's earth so that he will still be buried high. Lame men keep the yard, teach boys to throw the pole, or sit with the Keepers learning the recitations; the blind are often the best reciters in a town.

## When a House Fails

When a family cannot shelter, teach or defend its own, the clan does it. When the clan cannot, the town does. Clan houses take in the homeless of their clan. Boys learn war and the game from their mothers' brothers, girls learn fields and pottery from their mothers and aunts, and children with no adult kin are placed in a clan house by the clan's eldest woman. The town's young men turn out when the town is attacked. The palisade around a town's center and its mound is the last shelter, and in a raid the whole town crowds inside it.

Above the town is the Great Sun, whose duty to send help is the whole reason towns take his coal. A town attacked by plains bands that sends a runner to Hayo Pokta and receives nothing has learned something, and it remembers at the next New Fire.

## The Mother's Line

The family is the household of a mother: an old woman, her daughters, their husbands and their children, in houses round a shared yard, with fields that belong to the women. A man marries into his wife's household and works her fields, but his heirs are his sister's children. He teaches them, gives them his war name's honor, and leaves them his copper and his place at the yard. A child's father is loved; a child's mother's brother is obeyed.

Clans are counted through the mother, each named for a beast or a power: the Wind, the Bird, the Deer, the Bear, the Panther and many smaller ones. Marriage inside one's own clan is incest.

Rank passes through the mother too, and that rule shapes the whole nation. A Sun must marry a commoner. A Sun woman's children are Suns, but a Sun man's children are Nobles, a Noble man's children Honored, and an Honored man's children commoners. Every generation, the sons of the great sink a rung and the daughters keep their place. The Suns are therefore always marrying into commoner households, and a commoner family may have a Sun's grandchildren among its cousins. The nobility is never closed off from the people, and the people always have kin on the mounds.

## From Naming to the Mound

**Children** are named at the first New Fire after their birth, by the mother's brother. A child carries no earth and owes nothing; small children run through the plaza at the game and are fed by any woman of their clan.

**The young,** from about twelve, carry earth. Boys learn to throw the pole and to fight, and a boy becomes a man with his first captive or his first share of a war. Girls learn the fields, the pots and the house, and become women when they first marry, usually after their mothers have spoken to the other clan.

**Adults** carry the full share: maize, earth and war. A man's best years are spent in his wife's fields and his town's wars. A woman's are spent running her house and, as her mother ages, taking over her mother's place.

**The old** carry less and decide more. Old women are the voices of their clans. Old men sit on the mound steps and tell the young how the game was played properly.

**The dying** settle their earth-account. A dying man asks whether he has carried enough, and his sister's sons promise to carry the rest. A dying woman names which daughter has her house.

**The dead** go up or down, according to where they lie.

An ordinary day starts with the greeting to the sun and the feeding of the fire. The women go to the fields and the men to the river or the woods, or to the mound with the work crew, and in the afternoon the yard fills with boys throwing poles. An ordinary year turns on the New Fire, the planting, the harvest and the Stone Season, and in most years there is a war somewhere.

## The Shell Towns

The coast is the One Fire's other half, and the _koyoli_, the people of the shell, keep a different life. They have never farmed. Their estuaries are richer than any field: oysters, clams, mullet and sea turtles in such numbers that a town of two thousand eats without planting. Their towns stand on islands of heaped shell, raised over generations from what they ate, with dug canals for their canoes running between the heaps and out to the open water. They carve wood better than anyone in the north, masks, figureheads and animal heads with shell inlay for eyes, and they handle the gulf in dugouts that the heartland cannot match.

Each shell town has its Shell Chief, who collects tribute in shell, fish and labor from the smaller coastal towns around it and sends part of it up the river to the Great Sun. The shell towns take the coal, keep the New Fire and play the stone game, but they speak Tokchili with their own accent and consider the heartland farmers slow, pious and greedy. They remember the Funeral of the Hundred better than the heartland does, since it was their daughters, and they hold the Shell Promise as the condition of their staying. The Great Sun needs their canoes, which are the One Fire's only navy, and they know it.

## What the One Fire Gets Wrong

**The sin.** The Funeral of the Hundred was a crime the nation committed, and the nation knows it. Fifty-three of the dead were girls of another people sent as tribute and killed for a ceremony. The Keepers of the Fire recite it as Lapali's deed and do not call it a crime; the shell towns call it nothing else; most heartland people call it a wrong done by a Great Sun who was otherwise great. The practice it carried to its height continues. Every Sun is still buried with attendants, most of them volunteers and some of them captives, and every great funeral reopens the quarrel between those who hold that a Sun must go attended and those who hold that killing people for a ceremony is murder whoever it pleases.

**The blind spot.** The Tokchili believe that a Scattering is the price of a fire badly kept. The Keepers teach it, and most people believe it: Lapali's successors neglected the rites, and the sun withdrew. So each Gathering answers the fear of the next Scattering by binding the towns tighter, with more tribute, more earth for bigger mounds, larger funerals and longer wars to bring more towns in. That is exactly what drove the last Scattering. A town that lets its fire go out has decided that the Great Sun costs more than he gives.

Thoughtful Tokchili argue about it openly. Some Honored men, some clan mothers and nearly all the Shell Chiefs say that the fire goes out because the towns put it out, and that a Great Sun who asked less would be kept longer. The Keepers answer that the Third Gathering has lasted five Suns precisely because its Suns kept the rites. Both sides are now watching Opahiko, who is old, and the question of his funeral. The Keepers want him buried as a Great Sun should be, with a full following. The Shell Chiefs have let it be known that one coastal death, even a volunteer's, breaks the Shell Promise, and that they will put their fires out.

## The South

The One Fire is the northern nation most tempted to deal privately with the south. Its Suns prize what little comes north across the desert: cacao drunk at feasts, scarlet macaw feathers in a Sun's headdress, cast copper bells. All of it reaches them through the Mesa Towns, at the Mesa Towns' price. Some Suns and Nobles want more than goods. A Great Sun with southern steel would not need the towns' consent, and some of them have worked that out.

The shell towns hold a second road in their hands. The gulf runs south along the coast toward the southern lands, and some coastal captains claim to have sailed far enough down it to trade with people who had southern goods. Nobody has made the voyage both ways in living memory. The Great Sun's court would like to know whether it can be done, and the Shell Chiefs, who would own the route, have not said. Every other nation of the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] knows about the temptation and watches for it.
