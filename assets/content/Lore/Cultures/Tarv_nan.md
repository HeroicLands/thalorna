Tarvénia is not a country so much as a standing argument that has never been settled and is not going to be: three kingdoms, a free city, a free territory, and a patchwork of fiefdoms between them, bound by a shared tongue, a shared faith, and a shared conviction that nobody is going to tell a Tarvénan what to do. What holds a Tarvénan life together is his own name, the band or household he has sworn to, and the blade he is expected to be able to use.

## Courage as a Trade

The Tarvénan mercenary tradition is famous across the continent, and the important thing about it is that it carries no shame whatever. Tarvéni swordsmen and crossbowmen serve in armies from Vylaria to Tarûzel, and taking pay for arms is an honorable career here and not a mark of desperation—the thing a second son does because it is respectable, not because nothing else was left.

That fact organizes more than it appears to. It means a Tarvénan expects to leave and come back, and that a great many households are run for years at a stretch by women whose husbands are under contract three hundred miles away. It means money flows home from other people's wars, and that a village of no importance can be surprisingly well armed.

It also sets the measure of a man. A Tarvénan is judged on whether he is any good—visibly, demonstrably, in a trade where the judgment is made by people who will die of an error. Rank helps a man get a hearing; it does not get him kept on.

## Nobody's Tyrant

The Tarvénans are deeply skeptical of magic, and of the Ordo Arcanis above all. The Ordo keeps a few chapters in the larger cities and operates carefully, since a mage who throws his weight about in Tarvénia tends to end up with a knife between his ribs and the local authorities tend to be slow investigating it.

The skepticism is precise and not superstitious. Folk healers, hedge witches and village wise-women are tolerated and often respected: they are neighbors, they are answerable, and if one does harm the village knows exactly where she sleeps. What is not tolerated is institutionalized arcane power, which a Tarvénan regards as one more organization claiming the right to tell him what to do, and no more welcome than a tax-farmer.

The same instinct runs through the political patchwork. A Tarvénan does not want a stronger king; he wants a king weak enough to be argued with, and he will fund one against another to keep it that way.

## Honor, and the Public Word

Personal honor is the currency and it is transacted in public. An insult given before witnesses must be answered before the same witnesses, and the answer is expected to be proportionate and not lethal—a people this heavily armed has had to develop a grammar of escalation, and it is elaborate. The debt of honor that an insult leaves is the _orhéno_, and it is owed until it is answered.

A Tarvénan's word is meant to be good and is held to be good, and the mercenary trade depends on it: a company whose captain broke a contract does not get another. Contract-breaking is therefore the worst thing a Tarvénan can be known for, worse than cowardice, because cowardice is a fact about one man and a broken contract is a fact about everyone who marched with him.

## The Word Given

The Tarvénan answer to what is good is the word kept, and it is the same answer for a company, a lord and a householder. **Good is the word kept as given, courage shown where it can be judged, and power that is answerable to the people it acts on; evil is the broken contract, and after it the power that tells a free man what to do without having been asked.** The oldest and plainest words in the tongue are the oath words. A contract between a company and its paymaster is a _semnális_, a man under one is a _darándo_, a sworn man, and the oath itself is sworn on a stone older than the shrines, a _goitsára_. The man who breaks a contract is _khelmártho_, accursed, and the penalty is _kuthréza_ while the guilt, _damólis_, attaches to the company's name and not only to its captain's.

Success is to be any good and to be kept on. A man is a success when a captain keeps him in the company through the next season, when a household is held together through the long absence of its men, and when a company sings his name in the long poem after supper. The company's name for him, the _kiramhárlen_, is an earned name that a company gives a man. Failure is to be _anzirótho_, masterless, a soldier with no contract and no captain, or worse, to be struck from the company's roll, the _nehárlo_, the unnaming. The broken contract is the one failure the culture will not forgive.

A Tarvénan values most the word he gave, competence demonstrated, and freedom, _revótho_, the quality he most wants named in him. He values a plain statement: Tarvéni is a language of assertion in which indirectness reads as weakness or deceit. He values least the lord who claims a penny beyond the agreement, the tax farmer, the mage, the establishment that wants to regularize a village's festival calendar, and the man who writes down what a warrior should remember.

## The Man Admired

[[being-srdlvnthrvkknghtwstrnmrchs|Sir Dálven Thórvek]], a knight of the Western Marches, is the Tarvénan ideal at the scale of a life. He is the youngest son of a prominent house, and his elder brothers took the land and the titles. He served as squire under a famous knight, learned the ways of the sword and of command, and earned his reputation on the long campaigns that defended the border. What he made of himself he made by his deeds and by an unshakeable loyalty to his order, and the Western Marches know him as a knight of honor and integrity whose sword is at the service of his people and his king. He is stoic and a man of few words, and his words carry weight. He is compassionate toward those who cannot defend themselves, and he feels the protection of the weak as a duty and not as a pose.

The admiration has a price he knows. His adherence to the code is rigid, and his unwillingness to compromise makes him distant to those who do not understand his sense of honor. The culture admires him anyway, because rank got a younger son a hearing and it was his word that kept him: a man of no land who is trusted with the border is the Tarvénan answer to the question of what a title is for.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-althrtrvlth|Aléthra Torvaleth]], a priestess of Lîzêlâs in Nebrális, is admired for being what the village needs and answering for it. She was raised among fishing families, learned to fish before she learned formal prayer, and was nursed through a wound as a girl by an old priestess who saw her calling. She blesses the nets before the season, attends births and prepares the dead, mediates when two families quarrel over water or a boundary, and tends gardens and animals with the same attention. When a run of harsh years brought drought and hunger she fasted in solidarity with the village and nearly died of it. The communal assembly, where the free citizens of Nebrális decide matters of common concern, listens when she speaks, and she has never sought formal power.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the man's. Sir Dálven keeps a border by his sword, and Aléthra keeps a village by being present in the whole cycle of its life, in its fields and kitchens and fishing boats. She is the kind of neighbor a Tarvénan tolerates and respects, because she is answerable. The cost is in her too. Her work leaves no room for a household of her own, her graying and an old scar leave her village divided on whether she carries a blessing or a curse, merchant leaders want her informal work made orderly and accountable, and a bishop who visited was scandalized by what he perceived as an unstructured approach to her duties.

## What the Companies Keep

A Tarvénan can point to a handful of things that bind a company or a valley. **The contract** is the whole of the company's law. **The oath stone** (_goitsára_) is a standing stone older than any shrine, and the oath sworn on it is held to be the oldest kind. **The muster roll** (_theróna_) is the company's list of who marched, and the unnaming strikes a name from it. **The long poem** (_oldémo_) is the history of a company's deeds, sung after supper by someone who was there; the written chronicle is a different thing, and the two are not thought to be the same kind. **The charter** (_zaktéla_) is what a free city buys from the High King, and the Free City of Valthári pays tribute in return for its own courts and its freedom from feudal levies.

The order of the Sealed Word keeps the nearest thing to a national archive: the master register of every contested oath it has ruled on, held at Ólvestria. The Twinblade Order keeps the gates and thresholds, and the Twin Gate of Ólvestria is one of the most consequential in Aurèldía.

## Faith Without an Establishment

The Aurèldían pantheon is kept honestly and without much clerical supervision. Tarvénans favor Árdavon of gates and oaths, Karnavos of the wild, and the Sacred Forge, and they are cooler than their neighbors toward the grand temple establishments—the same objection as to the Ordo, applied to priests.

A village keeps its shrine, its own priest who is usually a local man, and its own festival calendar, and it resists any attempt to regularize any of the three. Hermit holy-men, wandering mendicants and local shrine-keepers are more common than formal temple priests. Under the gods of the pantheon the mountain villages keep an older one, the Mother (_menútsa_), who is kept in caves (_ikórrata_), where offerings (_itsárde_) are left at the mouth. Her wise-women (_tsingárra_) are the village healers a Tarvénan tolerates, and the Ordo's mage is the one he does not.

An ordinary Tarvénan's observance is plain and local. He swears on a stone, keeps the hearth fire (_karálzo_) and the household gods (_karalzándis_), leaves an offering at a wayside shrine (_nekháimo_) when he passes it, and goes to his village priest for the rites that mark a birth, a marriage or a death. He consults the wise-woman before a mage. Árdavon is revered above all among the warrior class and Lúsinía among farmers, and Brâzêmâs has a significant following among mountain communities and seafarers.

## The Year of the Valleys

Every village keeps its own festival calendar, _lordémo_ by _lordémo_, and resists every attempt to make it agree with the next valley's. The pantheon's festivals are kept in the local way. Árdavon's Feast of Thresholds at the turning of the year is the one that suits a people of oaths: lords and vassals publicly reaffirm their bonds, merchants renew their contracts and spouses their vows. Brâzêmâs's Thunderhead Vigil at the spring equinox sends the faithful up onto high places, the peaks and the towers and even the decks, to wait for the first storm of the season, and it belongs to the mountain communities and the sailors. The Sacred Forge's Festival of the Hearth at the winter solstice carries flame from the temple's forge to every household's fire. Mórváná's Waning Moon and Sélmoros's Silent Passage fall in late autumn and are the festivals of the dead.

The valleys keep their own: the midsummer fire (_ikéntsa_), the summer drive (_izúrka_) that sends the flocks up to the high meadows, and the autumn horse fair (_ratháuna_), where the races and the trading are the occasion. On certain nights the dead of a valley walk the roads in a line with a light before them, the _gatséra_. Each of these is for something concrete: the Thresholds for the oaths the year runs on, the Vigil for the storms, the drive for the flocks, the fair for the horses, and the procession for the dead.

## Answer, Penalty and Unnaming

The ladder of reward is the ladder of a company. A man is kept on, and then trusted with a rank, a _zirótho_, one step of the company's order. He is given an earned name by his companions. He comes home from a contract with money in hand, the _velzóra_, a homecoming the empire's speech keeps for a general's triumph. And he is sung. For the household the reward is the return of its men, the sound holding and the deed that goes into the long poem.

The ladder of penalty is the grammar of escalation. The lightest is the answer to an insult before the same witnesses, proportionate and not lethal. A lord who claims more than was agreed is not obeyed. The contract's penalty, the _kuthréza_, follows a breach, and the oath-hearing of the Sealed Word follows a breach that civil courts cannot reach. The law's heaviest sentence is banishment (_venthódis_), and the worst blow to a name is the unnaming, because it strikes the name from the roll. Where the oath-breaker is a powerful lord, the Sealed Word's brothers may offer trial by combat on the petitioner's behalf.

## Where the Village Ends and the Order Begins

Authority is local and several. The High King is elected by a council of the major lords at Astúrath, calls the ban, and adjudicates disputes between lords, and he has no standing army and no central bureaucracy. A lord governs from a hilltop castle. A town council of merchants and master craftsmen governs a free city or a coastal town, and in the free city and the free territory women hold office outright. The mountain clans follow their own ancient customs. Across Tarvénia there is a tradition of communal assembly in which free men decide matters of common concern.

The faith is neither rival nor master. A village priest is a local man. The Great Temple of Árdavon at Polthámis trains priests in theology, law and combat together, and sends them out to mediate disputes and bring corrupt officials to judgment. The prelate there confirms the arbitrations of the faith's traveling priests under seal, and the commissions carry the protection those cases need, which holds only while the work never reaches the royal court. Where the civil courts cannot reach an oath-breaker, the order of the Sealed Word convenes a hearing. Its rulings do not formally bind a kingdom's courts, and in practice they are honored because the order refuses petitions it cannot substantiate and rules without favor on those it can. The crowns license it, and some have grown reluctant to renew the license.

Who tries a case depends on the case. A matter between neighbors goes to the village assembly or the lord; a matter between lords goes to the High King; a broken oath goes to the Sealed Word. Who collects is the lord or, in a free city, the council. The tax farmer is no more welcome than a mage. Who keeps the record is the order for oaths, the scribe for contracts, and the poet for deeds. When these conflict no rule settles it. The answer is a negotiation, which the Tarvénans prefer to a settled hierarchy.

## What Becomes of the Dead

The Aurèldían faith teaches that the soul, _díravo_, passes first through the spirit realm and that the rites of the god receive it there, and the village priest says them. Under that the mountain villages keep an older account. On certain nights the dead of a valley walk the roads in a line with a light carried before them, the _gatséra_, and a house sets a lantern for the dead, the _ezkóbe_, on its threshold, the _belártsa_, so that they pass by. The dead of a house are _zekúdi_, the old ones, and in song the river runs like the blood of the ancestors, the _gárevárren_.

That belief changes an ordinary Tuesday. A Tarvénan keeps the contract he swore, because the man who breaks it is accursed and the guilt falls on everyone who marched with him. He does not sell the household's good name for a better price. A mercenary under contract in a distant war thinks about the homecoming, and a company that has lost men far from home sees to their rites, since a field where the dead were left unreceived is a danger to everyone who passes it. A householder lights the lantern on the right night and does not argue the point. He pays the village priest and leaves an offering at the cave mouth where the Mother is kept. He lays his dead among the old ones of his house. And he swears on the stone, not on a document, when the matter is the one that matters.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the village priest, who says the rites, through the lantern on the threshold, which asks the procession to pass by, and through the offering at a cave mouth. A wise-woman lifts the evil eye (_udúrtsa_), and a charm against the dead (_motsáre_) is to be had by a house that wants one. Nothing in the faith lets the living question the dead, and Sélmoros opposes anyone who raises them, so the institutions that charge are small: a priest paid in the village's coin, a wise-woman paid by the household she serves, and no establishment that sells answers.

The dead reach the living by walking. On their night they go by the lantern and no further, and a house that sets the light has done what is asked. The old ones are not consulted. They are kept at a distance that both sides honor.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Tarvénan what a person owes, and he answers: the word he gave, first and before everything, because it is the only thing about him another man can rely on. Then the household, then the band, then the village.

A lord is owed what was agreed and not a penny more. A Tarvénan will fight for a king he dislikes if the terms were met, and walk away from one he admires if they were not, and he does not regard the two as inconsistent.

In return a captain owes his sworn men their pay and their contract's terms. A household owes its absent man the holding kept and his place held. A village owes a free man a voice in its assembly, and a free city owes its citizen the courts its charter bought. The sum is short.

## The Weak

Care is a duty of the household before it is a kindness of the village. The household absorbs cousins, fosterlings and veterans of a dead brother's company, and the word that makes a stranger a member is _aipséna_, the taking in. A widow runs the holding and holds the contract, since women manage property in the absences of the men. An orphan is taken in like any other claimant. The village priest and the wise-woman tend the sick of the village, and the temples of Lîzêlâs and Mórváná keep houses of healing and hospices in the larger cities.

The limit is the edge of the household and the village. A stranger who has made himself useful is kin. A man who has no company, no household and no village is masterless, and he is the weak person the culture notices least.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A man wounded in a company's service is held to be kin, for a man who has fought four seasons beside another is kin in every way that operates day to day. The company's surgeon (_rithezándo_) closes the wound, and a household that has taken in a veteran gives him a corner. The contract states what is owed, so an injured man owes what the contract says and no more, and the rest is the household's. The old tell the long poem after supper, since a company's history is told by someone who was there. The dying are the village priest's.

In a lean year the money that comes home from other people's wars is the buffer, and the household that has sent a son abroad is better supplied than the one that has not. Where there is no buffer the village assembly decides what is shared, and a priestess may fast with the village as Aléthra did.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household's, the company's camp and the village's. A man who has fought four seasons beside another sleeps under his roof. Teaching is the company's and the household's: a herd boy's first weapon is the sling (_lírkheza_), a young man goes abroad under contract as a rite of passage, and the Great Temple at Polthámis teaches law and the sword together. The village priest teaches the rites and the wise-woman the herbs.

Defense is every free man's. A Tarvénan village of no importance may be surprisingly well armed, every free man in Astúrath is expected to bear arms, and the High King calls the ban when a foreign threat requires it. The Twinblades hold the gates and the borders, and the Sealed Word answers when a king breaks his word.

## House, Band and Name

The household is the unit, and it is more elastic than the Aurèldían norm. It absorbs cousins, fosterlings and the occasional stranger who has made himself useful. Blood matters and is not the only claim: the tongue uses _sorethándis_, those of one blood, of people who share none. A household is run by whoever is at home, the _zorkhándo_, and the word takes no notice of whether the person is a husband or a wife. Marriage (_kámbeza_) is arranged but not dictated, a dowry (_gébrina_) goes with the bride, and a Tarvénan who forced a daughter into a match would be talked about. Women commonly keep their birth names after marriage, and in some kingdoms use a matronymic rather than a patronymic. A name is often a patronymic, a place-name or an epithet earned through a deed.

The ladder of a life follows the ladder of the valley. A child is the household's. A herd boy has a sling in his hand early, and a young man goes abroad under contract, where he is judged by whether he is any good. A woman runs the holding through his absences, manages its property and holds its contracts. The returned man takes a place in the assembly and, if he is lucky, a chair at the fire where the long poem is sung. The old teach and tell, and the dying are the priest's and the lantern's.

## What the Hills Will Not Be Told

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Tarvénans' is the freedom that shelters what it should refuse. A people who will not be told what to do will also not be told to stop. The hill-baronies around Ólvestria have, for political reasons, declined to suppress the Black Flame, and the Ólvestrian cell of the Order of the Ashen Vow, that order's largest, operates under their protection. The Magister of the Sealed Word at Ólvestria died of causes ruled natural, days before he was to issue a major writ against a baron sympathetic to the Black Flame, and the death is still talked about as something less than natural. The Aurèldían crowns maintain standing orders for the order's suppression, and the hill-baronies of Tarvénia are the qualified exception.

The culture's other blind side runs through the household. Women manage property, hold contracts and run holdings through the long absences, but in the three kingdoms they do it through a husband's name while everybody knows who is actually deciding. Thoughtful Tarvénans dispute whether a people so jealous of every claim on a free man's will can leave so much of its practice unwritten.
