The Áelendan are a human people of the high interior of [[place-elavendre|Élavendre]], the People of the Pact, and they are deeply conservative, almost to the point of it defining them. They descend from the Proto-Pelwar band that came over the eastern pass, they keep [[affiliation-theoldway|the Old Way]] and the [Kindred Pact]{.name data-type="lore/law"}, and they hold that they are the guardians of the thin places, of the fae and of the numinous. The [[lore-flksinale|Sinale]] love them and keep them apart. The [[lore-elavendriclt|Élavendri]], their modern descendants, regard them fondly. Where they live and how their tribes are governed are in [[lore-aelendnppl|the Áelendan People]] and [[affiliation-aelndntrbs|the Áelendan Tribes]].

## Human, and Knowing It

The Áelendan are human and not of the numinous, and they know it. Their descent is Proto-Pelwar: they are the last of a line pushed west camp by camp ahead of [[lore-pelwarmigr|the Pelwar Migration]], and they reckon their people from the night their chiefs died in the far grassland and a herdsman, [Dhargan]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, told the survivors to take the children and the seed and go ([[lore-nelgancross|The Crossing of Nelgan]]). They teach their children that the people were driven, not invited. Every flock in the highlands is held to descend from the lamb that Dhargan's daughter [Nela]{.name data-type="being/npc"} carried over the pass in her coat.

Their closeness to the Sinale does not blur the line. The wood was given to them by a people who are older, taller and stiller than they are, and the account they tell of it begins with a man sitting at its edge with his hands open, because a human cannot walk into it as of right.

## The Unchanging {#the-unchanging}

The Áelendan change slowly and on purpose. Custom, _ornalon_, is the whole of what is done because it has always been done, and a thing is not wrong for being old. Their language and their scripts have not changed in centuries. A new thing from below gets a word coined from old roots, and a lowland word said in council is said once and corrected ([[skill-aelendlng|Áelendi]]).

They carve in the [[skill-thrvrdhscrpt|Thurarth]] runes, the rune-row the settlers brought over the pass, and they write in the [[skill-clthndscrpt|Veldikko]], the Sinale hand, which they took from their neighbours and use for their own tongue. They write little. The Pact is deliberately unwritten, since a thing committed to stone stops being renewed in the mouth. What is cut in Thurarth is wards, boundary and grave markers, old magic and poetry; what is written in the Veldikko is the rare page an elder or a student of the art sets down, in an older and plainer hand than the Sinale now use.

The conservatism is the people's strength and their argument with the world. The houses of the lowlands came down out of the hills Áelendan by descent and stopped being Áelendan by habit ([[lore-firstcrown|The First Crowning of Élavendre]]), and the tribes count that as the thing that does not happen to them.

## The Pact and the Old Way {#the-pact-and-the-old-way}

The Áelendan's spiritual tradition is their own. It is the Old Way, the shamanic faith their forebears carried over the pass, with [[affiliation-firstgods|the First Gods]] above, the Bright and the Dark, and [[lore-thekindred|the Kindred]] between them and mortal life. Within it the Áelendan keep the Kindred Pact, the compact between the people and the land, the fae and the older powers that dwell at the thin places. The Pact is not a metaphor to them. It is the agreement by which their communities stay in good standing with the land and its spirits, and the [[affiliation-alndnwrdns|Wardens]] guard its terms.

The Pact was first sung at the lowest pool of [[place-longstair|the Long Stair]], in the generation after the crossing, by the shaman [Silor]{.name data-type="being/npc"}. The song was the band's own, in the old forms and the people's own tongue, with the Sinale names set in it and said half wrong, and the Sinale stood apart from it because it was not theirs to make or hear ([[lore-lowestpoolpct|The Pact at the Lowest Pool]]). That is the shape of the whole tradition. The Sinale taught the band the wood, and the band kept its faith.

The Sinale influence lies in two reverences:

- **The spirit realm,** which is part of [[lore-flksinale|the numinous]], and the spirits and totems within it. The Kindred of a ridge, a corrie or a hollow are venerated, appeased or warded against, each as its nature requires.
- **The land:** the mountains, the forests, the lakes and the rivers. The Áelendan believe each has its parallel in the spirit world, so a peak or a pool is the near side of something that also stands on the far one.

The Sinale teach the Áelendan the outer fringe of the numinous and no more. The Áelendan hold the concept without being able to grasp it whole, because the rest is closed to humans, and their words for it are Sinale loans for the fringe alone. Their shamans, who bargain with [[lore-kiltasspr|Kiltas]] over his levy, ask [[lore-ossendaspr|Óssenda]] whether the dead have gone, read the weather through [[lore-tipelspr|Timel]], ward against [[lore-urnelspr|Urnel]] in winter and venerate him in summer, and approach [[lore-uronspr|the Uron Tekhi]] rarely and formally, practise the Áelendan's own Pact tradition. None of them practises the Sinale's inner ways.

## Land That Is Not Owned {#land-that-is-not-owned}

The Áelendan revere the natural world and have no idea of owning land or territory. Land is revered, not held. A pact is asked for: the land is not taken and not earned, and a people may sit at its edge and ask. The wood came to them as a gift that was never the giver's to give, and when an outsider asks by what right they are there, the first thing they say is that it was given and not won ([[lore-firesnelgan|The Fires on Nelgan]]). The lowlands and the maps speak of Áelendan country and Áelendan-held passes, and the Áelendan themselves claim none of it.

Certain places are sacred, and those they defend fiercely. A trespasser who blunders onto sacred ground is warned once and escorted out, and one who returns or desecrates is dealt with harshly by the Wardens, whose judgment there is final. The Tree-Line oath, sworn at every Élavendren crowning, is the crown's promise to stop where the tribes' sacred ground begins.

The Áelendan recognize no borders, and some of their sacred sites lie in the mountains of other realms: [[place-hydravenmnt|Mount Hidraven]] and [[place-sirmenne|Sirmenne]] in the north of [[place-provenzrgn|Provenzia]], the high groves and springs of [[place-toravarrange|the Toravar]] in [[place-tarvenirgn|Tarvénia]], and the peaks of [[place-vylariargn#the-cervaron-spine|the Kervaron Spine]], which the chancery of [[place-vylariargn|Vylaria]] lists as Vylarian. Defending those sites brings the Áelendan into friction with those realms from time to time: a Provenzian survey turned back from Hidraven's springs, a Tarvenan logging concession stopped short of a sacred slope, a Vylarian expedition repulsed in the Kervaron passes.

Provenzia tolerates the defense as a matter of policy rather than sympathy. The sites lie in mountains and remote forest that the crown has little use for, and tolerating the tribes there keeps its relations with Élavendre smooth. Tarvénia and Vylaria are considerably less tolerant.

## Keepers of the Thin Places

The Áelendan hold that they are the guardians of the [[lore-aralwen|aralwain]], the thin places, and of the fae and the numinous that cross at them. Their oldest pledge is the watch that Dhargan set on the fog-hollow the Sinale named [Talve]{.name data-type="place"}: two to sit by it through the night with spear and dog, to turn what comes through by song, fire or stone, to retire when it will not turn, and to sing from the hall on the nights it wins. The Wardens began as that watch, and their charge widened to the passes and the groves.

Not every sacred place is a thin place. The Long Stair is sacred because the river-mother [[lore-salendaspr|Sálenda]] is seated there, the Owl Hollow because Óssenda is, and Ésalosa because its hearth is [[lore-tenaspr|Tena]]'s. The thin places are a charge of their own, and a thin place left unwatched is the one failure the tradition does not forgive in itself ([[lore-blacksnow|The Black Snow]]).

The guardianship sets the Áelendan against [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|the Thordimalon]], which comes up the valleys to study what the tribes exist to keep. The Áelendan count the Order's thefts from the sacred sites among the gravest wrongs done to them ([[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan People]]).

## Loved and Apart

The Sinale love the Áelendan, and the bond is the closest between the Sinale and any human people. The Sinale deal with the world through the Áelendan and nowhere else, at [[place-ethalosse|Ethalosse]], and the Áelendan carry what is said exactly and add nothing. The Tradition Keepers hold the songs the Sinale have given freely, [[lore-theasking|The Asking]] among them. Áelendi is full of Sinale words for the wood, the water below the snow line, the veil and the rites, and the Áelendan do not count them as borrowed: they came with the keeping of the wood, and a thing kept is part of the people who keep it.

The Áelendan are nonetheless apart from the Sinale, and they know where the line runs. They do not speak Sinale. They write their own tongue in the Sinale hand, which is not writing Sinale. They keep the Old Way and not the Sinale's ways, and the numinous is open to them only at its fringe. Marriage, fosterage and long friendship happen at the edge where the two peoples meet, rarely and quietly. Why the Sinale opened themselves to the Áelendan and to no other human people is a question neither side has answered, and the Áelendan do not discuss it.

## The Élavendri {#the-elavendri}

The Élavendri are the Áelendan's adaptable modern descendants, and they regard the Áelendan fondly and respectfully as the ancestor people who keep the old ways. Every noble house's prestige rests on a claim to Áelendan blood, and the right to the throne is the right of a house to say it comes from them. The crown protects the tribes' sacred ground by law, and its courts act against anyone who encroaches on it.

The Áelendan return the regard with affection and unease. They understand Élavendri well and take nothing from it into their own speech. They acknowledge the kingdom without submitting to it, govern themselves by their own customs and answer to their own elders, and they hold that the cities traded something away. Both sides are courteous about it.

The Élavendri do not keep the Pact tradition. They worship the gods of the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]] in the Aureldian manner, and they follow the philosophy of the [[affiliation-hexhodai|Zemor Hodhis]], the Six Ways. They have very close ties with the Thordimalon, and that bond is a real point of tension between the two peoples, beside their regard. The Áelendan actively dislike the Order and do not tolerate its meddling in their sacred sites, and they count the kingdom's permission for it, more than any single theft, as the offense.

## What Is Good, and What Succeeds

**Good is a thing kept: the wood as it was given, the watch at the thin place, the name a child inherits and the song held whole. Evil is a thing taken that was not offered, and a door left open.** The Áelendan do not weigh a deed by its outcome alone. A deed done by driving is suspect even when it works, and a deed asked for in the right form is sound even when it costs. The gravest religious offense is a shaman's power turned to private ends: cursing, private gain or coercion.

Success is a name carried well. An inherited name comes with what its bearers did, and a person succeeds by adding to it without breaking it. Success is a voice heeded at the council of elders, a household that keeps its herds and its guest-right, a place among the Wardens or the Tradition Keepers, and in the end a name the Keepers recite. Failure is a broken word that leaves its weight on the name, _dhámolis_; a forfeit the kin pays for one of its own; a curse that puts a kinsman outside the Pact; and at the bottom, Kinlessness.

The Áelendan value most continuity, the keeping and the asking. They value least haste, novelty, a lowland word in council, and any claim to own what was given.

## The Man Admired

Dhargan, the first, is the man the Áelendan hold up, and they are particular that he had never led anyone before. He had kept sheep. He led the band over [[place-nelgan|Nelgan]], went back into a river in spate for one more child, and sat on the seed sacks when the hungry men looked at them and told them to eat the sheep, the dogs and himself before the seed. At the wood's edge he sat for three nights with no cup, no fire, no spear and no bread, his hands open on his knees, and asked to be called in or not to come in at all. He set the first watch with his own sons. The Sinale named him **Pilme**, the one who sat, and the Áelendan say the name to children by the hearth and at his cairn, where a birch came up through the stone.

What they admire is the asking. He did not drive, he did not take, and he waited to be told what the wood needed. His cost is in the telling too: the river took his wife, the fog took his son, and he spent each spring after at the wood's edge.

## The Woman Admired

[Mogha]{.name data-type="being/npc"} is the woman admired, a Tradition Keeper in the line of Nela, who was the first. In the killing winter of [[lore-blacksnow|the Black Snow]] she held the right song whole, and nobody asked her. When her brother, the chief, had felled the silver birch and pulled the watch from the hollow, and something came through, the hall made him Kinless. Mogha brought the people up to the wood's edge in the cold, the young and the old with their hands bare, and they sat three days as Dhargan had sat. She told the Sinale that the people had broken the wood and had come back to ask to keep it again, and that they would fail and ask once more. She was answered, "Keep it."

Her admiration is not a lesser form of Dhargan's. He asked for the wood, and she held what was known about it and owned the people's failure aloud. Her cost is the lesson of her story: the lore was in her keeping, and it was not consulted until it was too late.

## What the Tribes Keep

An Áelendan can name the things that hold the people in place. **The Kindred Pact** is unwritten, sung and enacted through the seasons, and held whole by the Tradition Keepers, one chosen child in each generation trained for decades. **The genealogies** are recited by the same Keepers, and a name entering a lineage for the first time is an event they record. _The Asking_ is the Sinalean lay of the band, held by the Keepers. **The Long Stair's rules** are taught to every child in order, the first being that nobody climbs it by daylight. **The birch law** forbids anyone to fell a standing silver birch, for any reason. **Dhargan's cairn and its birch** are the one human place the Sinale visit as they visit no other. **The Felled Stand,** the ring of birch stumps nearest the Lodge, is where young Wardens are brought to see what was done.

Each person carries smaller things: an inherited name, the protective songs for the ground a person must pass, and the knowledge of whose territory must not be entered.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is a set of relationships, and most of them are easy. A household venerates the hearth-spirit that keeps the children warm and the river-mother whose water is clean, and leaves the small standing offerings at the stones where they are owed. Offerings are simple and specific: food, drink, blood, breath, song, fire. The Áelendan take from the land what it offers and give back what the Kindred have shown, through long tradition, that they require. They are careful speakers after dark near the Owl Hollow, because Óssenda listens and remembers.

The rest of the day is the herds, the high pasture and the walled field, the lodge and its kept fire, and the guest. A guest under the roof is owed what the lodge has, and the order of seats is kept. The shamans advise and the elders decide, and an apprentice learns the songs, the lineages of the tribe's Kindred and the uses of fire, smoke, plant and water across many years.

## The Round of the Year

The year turns on the Two Powers: the solstices, the equinoxes, and the liminal nights between them. Each tribe adds the occasions of its own Kindred.

- **The great seasonal rites of the Pact** are held at [[place-eshalshldg|Ésalosa Lodge]], in the one hall large enough for the gathered people. They are for keeping the people's standing with the land.
- **The spring offering at the Long Stair** is made at the lowest pool at first light, in silence, and it is a thing the giver made and would miss. It is for Sálenda, and for the pact that was first sung there.
- **The summer rite in the high corries** venerates Urnel between the thaw and the frost, and it is among the larger occasions of the year. It is for the high pasture, the honey and the warning of avalanche.
- **The equinox rites at the Owl Hollow** are kept by the nearest tribe and are the only times numbers come into the hollow at once.
- **The autumn fair at [[place-elanmere|Elanmere]]** brings tribal families down to camp on the lake shore rather than enter the walls, and is for trading face to face with the lowlands.

## Standing and the Kinless

Reward is standing. A name heeded in council, a place among the Wardens or the Keepers, a household that keeps its guest-right, and a deed sung afterward by the Keepers are what an Áelendan works for. The monarchs invited up to Ésalosa are remembered in an honor-song, and so are the Áelendan whose deeds are kept.

Penalty runs in order. A lowland word in council is corrected. A broken word leaves _dhámolis_ on a name. A forfeit, _kuthresa_, is paid by the kin for one of its own who broke his word. A curse, _khelmaron_, puts a kinsman outside the Pact and its protection. Outlawry, _enthodis_, walks a person past the tree line. Worst of all, a tribe's hall makes a person Kinless: no hearth, no shade, no water, no name and no friend, owed no hospitality and no vengeance. A shaman who turns the power to private ends is exiled. A trespasser on sacred ground answers to the Wardens.

## Elders, Shamans and Wardens

Authority is shared among three bodies that do not overlap. The Council of Elders governs each tribe, and leadership is earned through wisdom, skill in the old ways and service; a chieftain holds by descent and consent together and loses the place when either fails. The tribes meet in common council, whose Speaker keeps its peace and nothing more, and whose delegates may argue and may not bind. The shamans keep the people's standing with the Kindred and advise the elders without commanding them. The Wardens keep the sacred sites, and on sacred ground their judgment is final over the councils, the crown and everyone else.

A case is tried by the elders in council, whose judgment is _nasdis_; the Lawkeeper arbitrates where two tribes' customs differ, and the Keeper of the Feud reckons blood owed and blood paid. Nothing is written, so the records are the Tradition Keepers' memory and the Keeper of the Feud's. When the answers conflict, the elders decide, except on sacred ground, which is the Wardens'.

## What Becomes of the Dead

A funeral-rite commends the dead to the river-mother who carries them on, or to the stone-warder who keeps their bones. The dead lie under cairns, and a valley keeps its field of cairns at its edge. Beside the cairn the Áelendan plant a tree as the Sinale do for their own dead, _sélposalpu_, and sing the lament, _nélthapa_. A spirit that will not go on lingers, and the tribes keep two or three binders in every generation to settle such a spirit and do not speak of them. The ones who sat down on Nelgan in the crossing are said to be sitting there still.

That belief makes an ordinary day. A child receives the name of an honored ancestor and is expected to carry what came with it, so a person's conduct is the dead's as much as his own. The genealogies the Keepers recite are the dead in the one place where they are kept, and a name broken is a line shamed. A family's wealth goes to the offerings, the cairn and the tree, and to the guest, not to the land, which belongs to nobody.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the Death-Speaker. A Death-Speaker goes to Óssenda at [[place-owlhollow|the Owl Hollow]] to ask whether the recently dead have gone, which is a different question from asking the dead anything. The nearest tribe permits this and charges nothing, on the ground that the answer is Óssenda's to give and not theirs to sell. The asking is done at night at the beck, and Speakers have waited three nights and gone home without an answer.

The dead reach the living through the thin places and the lingering. What the fog-hollow kept in the first generation was not dead and was not returned, and a spirit that will not go on is settled by a binder. The Pact makes no promise that the dead answer.

## What a Person Owes

Ask an Áelendan what a person owes, and the answer is the keeping first: the wood as it was given, the watch, the birch, the sacred sites. Then the kin, whose name the person carries. Then the guest under the roof. Then the council's judgment, which is accepted once given.

In return a kinsman is owed the household's protection, its feud and its share: hearth, shade, water, a name and a friend, which are exactly what the Kinless lose. A person is owed a hearing in council, a guide's knowledge of the routes, and the Wardens between the people and whatever comes through.

## The Weak

The household is the first resort. A dependent is sheltered by a household without being of its blood: a client, a widow's family, a taken-in stranger. An orphan is the kin's, and a guest is owed the lodge's fire. The Kinless are owed nothing, and that is the whole weight of the sentence.

In a bad year the seed comes before the hungry, as Dhargan sat on the sacks and would not rise, and the birch is not fuel even in a killing winter. The tribes learned the second rule at the cost of three children and a chief, and they teach it as a story about the winter rather than about him.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot work remains a kinsman and keeps a kinsman's share, since membership is by blood and not by labor. The old are the memory of custom and precedent, and an elder's recollection settles what the young dispute. A person who cannot go to the high pasture keeps the lodge, the fire and the children, and a person who cannot keep those is fed by the household that holds the name.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household, then the lineage, then the tribe. Teaching begins in childhood and lasts decades: a child who shows the perception is taken as apprentice by an elder shaman, a Tradition Keeper is chosen as a child and trained across a lifetime, and the Wardens are drawn from the tribes by family line or selection and trained from youth. Every child learns the Long Stair's rules in order. Defense is the Wardens on sacred ground and the common muster when a threat concerns every tribe, called by the Host-Caller.

## The Kin at Every Age

The kin is the unit. A household is a tent or a hall under a householder; a lineage is the households of one blood under its elders; a clan carries its founder's name with the ending that means "those of", and a tribe is the clans of a valley or a range. A child is given an ancestor's name and learns the rules of the sacred places before anything else. A child who dreams true dreams or has an unusual rapport with animals is marked for the shamans. A young person herds, apprentices or trains for the Wardens. An adult keeps a household and speaks for it at the moot. An elder holds the memory of custom. The dying are attended by the shaman and the kin, and the dead go under stone, are given a tree and a lament, and are recited among the genealogies.

Membership is by blood, by marriage and by a household's taking-in, and it is lost only by the hall's sentence of Kinlessness.

## What the Tribes Do Not Say

The Áelendan's blind spot is the conservatism that defines them. A people that changes nothing keeps everything, the wrong songs with the right ones, and the tribes have no means within custom of asking whether a custom is wrong. Thoughtful Áelendan argue whether refusing every lowland word keeps the people whole or only keeps them small, and whether the Élavendri cities traded something away or found something the highlands declined to look for.

The sin is the Black Snow, and the Áelendan tell it as the people's and not the chief's. In a killing winter Ghalmir said the silver birch should be cut, the people let him, and nobody spoke against it. The watch came off the fog-hollow for one spring because hands were needed to haul and split, and the people thought so too. The right song was held whole by a Tradition Keeper, and nobody asked her. The Áelendan say that the thing in the fog came in not through the fog but through a door the people left open themselves, and the shape of every telling after it is failure, return and asking.
