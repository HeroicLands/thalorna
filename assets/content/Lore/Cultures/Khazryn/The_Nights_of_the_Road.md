**The Nights of the Road**, _Tarlimnurmesh_, is the collection of tales a caravan-master tells on [[lore-celestialroad|the Celestial Road]], one to a halt, to a factor on his first crossing. The master in this telling is **Tarlimay**, a [[lore-khazrynclt|Tellumi]] of [[place-kethramir|Kethramír]] who has crossed from the March to Tānvür and back more times than he says; the factor is a young man of a Byzarian backing house, unnamed, who wrote the tales down in the rendering at the end of the crossing, in the order the master told them. No two masters tell them in the same order, and every master tells them to explain a thing the factor has just seen: a bend in the road, a halt within sight of water, a price paid without argument. At [[place-qisomrktcmp|Qiiso]], where the road changes hands, the master gives the fire to other tellers, and their tales sit inside his.

The collection is the caravan peoples' own form, told in a master's reckoning voice, and the places in it are the road's: the first toll-camp, an owned spring of [[place-wlqtrrgn|Völketör]], the fort at [[place-roqid|Oroqid]], the hand-off at [[place-yemokwell|Yemök]], the Long Dry, the towers of [[place-jilaq|Jilaq]] seen lit, the market-camp, the halt within sight of [[place-wemaq|Vömek]], and the hand-off at [[place-sowidwell|Sovid]]. The tellers who take the fire are a guide of the [[affiliation-rwlstrb|Uruvolur]], **Uruvolun**, a rider of the [[affiliation-shrzrtrb|Shirzâri]], **Khilodtoliz**, and a Tānvüri silk-broker who gives no name. What each tells of [[place-wilud|Vülüd]], [[place-qimod|Qiimod]] and Jilaq is what a teller of that people sells at a fire, and the master says so.

: The Nights of the Road {#nights}

The seventh day. The first toll-camp. A hundred and twenty miles from the last caravanserai of the March, and the first fire the factor has sat at that was not under a roof.

## The Seventh Day: the Arithmetic

"You have paid a price today and you did not like it," the master said. "You counted it in your head while I counted it into the dovaq's hand, and you made it come to a month of your father's rent, and you were right. Now hear what it bought, and then hear what it is for.

"Silk costs a hundred weights of silver at the customs-house on the Tānvüri pass. It sells for four hundred at Karvashion, five in a good year, six if a fleet has gone down. The difference is what we are walking across. Ten weights of that go to the tribes of Idvaqor, at the far end. Seventeen go to the tribes of the sands, because the sands are the longest stretch and a well lost there cannot be replaced. Ten go to the tribes here on the grass. Fifteen go to riders, water at the named wells and fodder. Five go to the March on the way out. Fifteen go to the dead, which is to say to the losses spread over the whole fleet, about one string in eight. That lands a load at Karvashion for a hundred and seventy, and it sells for four hundred, and that is the road. Everything you will see between here and the pass is somebody collecting a part of that two hundred and thirty, and everything you will be tempted to do is a way of keeping a part of it that was not yours to keep.

"The one thing. Learn it tonight, because tomorrow it is too late to learn it. In this country everything has an owner, and a stranger who has bought nothing is fair game. The spring that looks empty belongs to a tribe camped two ridges off, and so does the grass your horse is cropping, and so does the hare you were thinking of shooting this afternoon. I saw you look at it. What we paid the dovaq today bought three things and no more: water at her tribe's named wells and nowhere else, that rider sitting over there with the braid, who will go with us to the edge of her range and answer for us with his life, and her word, which is a promise that her people will make it good if they rob us and will raise a feud on our behalf if anybody else does. It did not buy safety. Passage buys vengeance. That is why forty of these men carry bows.

"A master I knew, a Tarûzeli, argued the first price. He had a list from his father that said eight weights at this camp, and the dovaq said twelve, and he said eight, and she said twelve, and he said eight and turned his string to go round her wells to the next tribe's. The rider on the ridge that afternoon was hers. He paced the string for an hour and went away. Nothing happened that day, and nothing the next. On the fourth day the Tarûzeli came to the next tribe's spring and found the well-stone on the shaft and no one, and tied his cloth to the skull-pole as the custom is, and waited, and at first light a dozen riders came in and the eldest sat down at the water and waited to be offered tea, and then told him that no tribe on the grass would sell him a rider this year, because the first dovaq had sent word along the wells that he was a man who argued the price. He went back to the March with his cargo and sold it at a loss and I bought a third of it. Four weights. He had saved four weights. Go to sleep."

## The Owned Oasis

Between the springs of the grass range, in the second week, the factor saw the master halt the string a bowshot short of a dozen palms at dusk, with the camels pulling toward the water, and get down, and walk, and tie a strip of cloth to a pole with a horse skull on it, and sit at the edge of the sand with his hands on his knees. Nobody unloaded. Nobody drew. In the morning twelve riders came in from every side at once.

"That was my first crossing," the master said that night, at the owners' fire, with the price paid and the tea drunk. "I was younger than you and I knew less. My master was a Tellumi of Ashkarad, dead now, a careful man, and he had hired a guide at the last well, a rider of a tribe whose name I did not catch, and the guide was the one who tied the cloth. I asked him why we did not drink. He did not answer. He looked at the ground. He found the well-stone, a flat slab over the shaft with a sign cut into it, and the sign cut in the oldest palm higher than a camel can rub, and the skull-pole facing the way we had come, and he said, 'All three. They are close.' Then he tied the cloth and sat down, and told us not to light a fire, and we sat in the dark with the camels groaning at the smell of the water and did not drink.

"They came at first light. I counted eleven. The guide said twelve, and he was right; one was on the ridge behind us with a bow. The eldest came down and sat at the water and we gave him tea, and he drank it and asked my master his name and his house and his city, and my master told him, and the eldest asked what we had drawn, and my master said nothing, and the eldest sent a boy to look at the mud at the pool's edge, and the boy came back and said nothing, and the eldest named a price for the night. It was a blade. My master gave him a good one. Then the eldest said that his tribe's range ran three days east and that one of his riders would take us across it for a price, and named that too, and my master paid it, and the rider took us by a track no caravan uses, past a seep I have never found again, and handed us to the next tribe at a well that was not on my master's list, and my master put it on the list that night. It is on mine. It is where we drank yesterday.

"What it cost: a blade, the passage, and a night without water within smell of it. What it bought: a well. The man who camps and drinks pays for every skin and leaves a man behind as surety until it is paid. The man who grazes loses the beasts. The man who fouls the pool or cuts a palm or draws steel pays in blood, and the ones who live are thralls. The man who ties the cloth pays a blade. You saw which I was. Now you know why."

## Oroqid

The string watered at the fort on the low ridge on the sixteenth day, and the factor saw a square of dressed stone with a tower at each corner and a courtyard swept clean, and paid for water at a price that made him look at the master, and a larger price when he asked to see the headquarters, and did not see it.

The rider of the tribe that waters there told it, and the master gave it as the rider gave it. "The ones who built it were not driven out. They were called away. A force that leaves a fort with its roofs on and its pay in the strongroom and its standards wrapped in oilcloth means to come back for them, and we keep the gate clear and the yard swept against the day, and we do not go into the house in the middle, because it is theirs. We have swept it for longer than anyone counts. The water is ours, because the water was here before the fort and will be here after, and we sell it.

"There is a door under the house in the middle, iron, shut, with a stair down to it. A Byzarian came three years ago who had paid for the water and paid for the yard and paid to go into the house, and he had a paper that said what was in the strongroom, and he went down the stair with a lamp and two men. The stair has a stone in it that is not fastened. Everyone of ours knows which. He did not ask us, because he had paid to go in and thought that was the whole of the price. The stone went, and a slab came down from the wall above it, and we heard it in the yard. We do not go into the house in the middle, so we did not go in. His two men came out. He did not. The door is still shut. The Byzarian is still on the stair, under the slab, and the next man who pays to go in will step over him, and will not ask us either, because the ones who pay to go in never do. The price of the water is what you paid. The price of the house is what you paid. The price of the stair is not for sale, because nobody has asked."

"What it cost him," the master said, "was the whole of what he brought, and his life. What he carried out was nothing. The standards are still wrapped. Mark that the rider told it for nothing. He will sell you anything on the grass, and he told that one free, and he looked at you while he told it, and that was the price."

## Yemök: the Oath

The thirtieth day. Five hundred and fifty miles. Tamarisks over a stone trough long enough to water forty camels, and a well-head cut with a dozen signs, and two sets of riders squatting on either side of the trough with the master between them laying out his string of tokens on the stone, one by one, a tally of cord or clay for each range crossed, each cut with a tribe's sign. The riders of the sands turned each one over and asked a question about it. Then both sets of riders laid their hands on the well-head stone and swore that the caravan had come through whole, and the master paid again, a month's profit, and paid it without a word.

"You wanted to argue that," the master said. "I saw you. Hear why not. Every tribe sells its own stretch, and nobody sells the road. The riders of the grass brought us this far and they go no farther, because east of those tamarisks is the sand and the sand is not theirs. The riders of the sands do not take a caravan on without the tokens, because the tokens are what says we kept the road-peace: that we hunted nothing, drew at no unnamed water, fouled no well and sent no armed men off the track. Each of those tokens is a tribe's word that we did. The riders read them. If they read them clean, they take us on and swear it on the stone, and the grass riders swear it, and go home with their pay and a witness's word. The oath is why the well has its name. The price is the second highest on the road. The only one higher is at Qiiso.

"A master came in here, eight years ago, with tokens on his string from two tribes that were not on his road, and swore he had been robbed and the tokens were the robbers' marks that he had taken off a dead man. The riders of the sands laid his string out on the stone and looked at it for a long time and said: wait. The truce binds every tribe whose range touches the well, so nobody touched him. He waited. His camels ate the fodder he had bought for the Long Dry. His drivers drank his water. The tribes whose signs were on the two tokens were sent for, and one came in nine days and the other in a season, because its range was far and its dovaq was at a feud, and when they both sat at the trough and looked at the tokens, they agreed that the tokens were theirs, and that they had been given to a rider of theirs who had died on the grass that spring, and that the master had taken them off the rider, who was not dead when the master found him. They did not kill the master. The truce held. They sold him nothing, and nobody east of the tamarisks sold him anything, and he is a drover now in the March, which is the only work left for a man who has crossed and cannot cross again. What it cost him was the road. What he carried out was two tokens, and he did not even keep those."

## The Long Dry

The thirty-second day to the thirty-eighth. No tale. The master did not speak of the wells ahead until he saw them, and the factor learned not to ask. The camels carried skins for the horses and walked with their heads low and their eyes half shut through the heat, and the string marched before dawn and halted in the worst of the day under cloth, and marched again toward evening. On the fourth day a budaq came over the string and hung, a bird with a wingspan of nine feet, and followed, and on the fifth it settled on the oldest camel, the one with the torn ear, and the drivers looked at the master and the master looked at the camel and said nothing. The camel reached the well. The bird went away. Some do.

## The Towers of Jilaq

On the forty-second night the factor saw a row of yellow lights on the northern horizon, low and steady and as even as a necklace, and the camels lifted their heads toward them, and he asked the guide why the string had turned south at the last cairn. The guide did not answer. The master said he would be told at the fire by someone who had a better tale of it than he did, and on the forty-fourth night, three days south of their line, a Tānvüri silk-broker who had joined the string at Yemök told it.

"In my country," the broker said, "a lamp is lit for the dead, and the dead do not light their own. Those are the towers of the lantern city, which the lamp-league built when the road was theirs and lit every night from dusk to dawn so that a string could march in the cool and steer by them as a ship steers for a harbor. A dozen towers stand there over a ring wall a mile across. The lamp-chambers are empty and black. On still nights and on the night of the sack they light themselves, and bells ring in the yards though the bells were carried off and melted, and ranks of figures form and break in the harbor court at a bowshot's distance and never nearer. The city was killed in a day and a night by the first banner when it refused tribute, and nobody laid its dead out, because they were strangers to every rite the riders kept, and they are a field now of the kind that kills. The road bends three days round it. The detour is worn deep. Every tribe whose range it crosses charges for it.

"Now the tale, which is about glass. The league ground lenses for its lamp-chambers, clear as water and nearly flawless, and stored them in straw in the counting-house vaults, and one lens is worth a caravan. A factor of my acquaintance at Qiiso, a Byzarian, will pay a fortune for one and will not say what it is for, and I know what it is for, and I will not say either. He hired a party. They went north from the last cairn with an Uruvol guide as far as the Uruvol would go, which was not far; the hidden-water bands will sell a path south of the wall and not one north of it at any price. The party went on alone. None came back. He hired a second party, better paid, with a man in it who had been inside the fort at Oroqid and lived, and they went north, and one man came back to Qiiso.

"He had a lens. It was wrapped in his coat. He walked into the market-camp in the middle of the ten days with sand scored in wide arcs round every step he had taken, which the riders saw and spoke of, and he gave the lens to the factor and took the money and did not count it, and then he sat down on the north side of the camp facing the way he had come, and did not move. He was there when the market broke up. He was there when I left. The riders fed him. He would not say what the glass was for and he would not say what had happened to the others and he would not turn round. The factor has the lens, and has not sold it, and has hired no third party, and when I asked him what the glass was for, he looked at me the way the man on the north side looked at the horizon. That is the tale. What it cost: two parties, and one man's face. What he carried out: one lens, and the money, which he did not count."

"The Tānvüri tell it well," the master said when the broker had gone to his tent. "They have lamps for the dead, so it goes hard with them. Mark the one true thing in it, which is the Uruvol who would not go north of the wall. The hidden-water bands sell everything, and they will not sell that. When a people who sell everything will not sell a thing, that is the price."

## Qiiso: the Price

The sixty-sixth day. Eleven hundred and fifty miles. A ring of black stones round a deep well, and in ten days a city of felt tents and rope corrals round it that held fifteen thousand people, four thousand camels and every kind of silver anyone had ever struck. The western factors pitched on the west side and the Tānvüri and Vedyari on the east, the Tellumi and Ātárzád houses on the south, and the furs of the north came in on the north, and the tribesmen camped in the gaps, and the factor found his own house by the color of the cloth on the tent-pole. The master sold the cargo here and did not go on whole, as most do not, and bought Tānvüri silk off an eastern string to carry back, and the factor's house would go on east with another master; so the master gave the fire to others for three nights, and said that the factor would need their tales more than his.

"No polity keeps this," he said first. "The tribes whose ranges meet here hold a moot before each season, and the chiefs swear a peace on the well-stone, and the peace holds for the ten days. No blood is shed in the ring, no weapon is drawn, a quarrel is carried to the moot and priced. A man who breaks the peace is killed outside the ring by the tribe he wronged and the tribe that holds his water pays the blood-price. You saw the man at the gate of the corrals yesterday with the bound stumps. He stole a camel. The water here is the dearest on the road and the well is lidded in stone and watched day and night, and the moot sells everything it can: stands, water, grazing, the services of its riders, and its word. Now hear a guide."

### The Guide's Tale: the Street Sold Twice

The guide was a woman of the hidden-water bands, Uruvolun, small and quick, with a face burned nearly black and a cord of tokens round her neck longer than the master's. She sat down at the fire as if she had been invited and the master poured her tea as if she had.

"You know the Wonder," she said. "You do not. It is nine tower-tops standing out of a sea of pale dunes in the sand range, a long march from any well that is not guarded, and under them is a city of ten thousand that the dunes drowned when its well failed. It is not a blood land. Nobody was killed. They walked away and the sand walked in. The same wind that buried it uncovers it, and every generation or so a sand-front goes over a quarter of the city and leaves a street open to the sky, walls whole to head height, doorways clear, cellars full of fine sand, and in a few years the next front covers it again. The sand lends the city and calls the loan in without notice. We are the first to know when a street opens, because we are the only ones out there, and we sell the news.

"Four years ago a street opened on the eastern side, a street of shops, with the lintels showing and a counting-house door at the end of it. I rode to Qiiso. I sold the news to a Byzarian factor on the west side in the morning, for a price, and I told him I would guide whichever buyer reached the Wonder first. He asked if there was another buyer. I said there would be by noon. I crossed the ring to the east side and sold it to a Tānvüri collector for the same price, and told him the same. They both paid. That is the custom: the news is one thing and the guiding is another, and I had sold the news twice and the guiding once. The Byzarian hired camels and left that night. The Tānvüri hired better camels and left at dawn and overtook him on the second day, and I was waiting at the edge of the sand for whichever came first, and it was the Tānvüri, and I guided him.

"The Byzarian came up a day later and found us at the street with the Tānvüri's men already in the counting-house door, and he had paid for the news, and the news was true, and he had paid for nothing else. He asked me what he had bought. I told him: the news. He asked what else I had to sell. I said: the position of a second street that had opened on the western side in the same front, which I had not sold to anyone, for the same price again. He paid it. It was true. That street held a potter's quarter and a household's silver in a jar under a hearth, and the Tānvüri's street held seals and weights of the counting-house and lamp-glass from a fallen tower, and both of them went home content and neither of them has spoken to me since, and both of them will buy from me again, because nobody else knows when a street opens.

"What it cost: three prices. What they carried out: what I have said. What I carried out: three prices, and the knowledge of where both of them keep their silver, which I have not sold, yet. A party that finds an open street without a guide is a competitor, and I know where everyone in the sand range is, and I sell that too. You did not know I was here until I sat down. Think about that when you are out there."

### The Rider's Tale: the Silent Market

The Shirzâri rider, Khilodtoliz, sat down the second night and did not take tea until he had been paid for the tale, and the master paid him, and said afterward that the Shirzâri had never put out a warning that was not for sale and that this was the cheapest way to buy one.

"Four days south of here on the spur," the rider said, "where the track to the belt dips through a gap in the black rock, there is a town with its walls whole. Mud-brick twenty feet high, a gate that stands open, a gatehouse with its roof on. Inside, three hundred houses and a square, and in the square the stalls are up: poles, awnings, a weighing-beam hanging from its hook. It looks like a town that stepped out for the afternoon. It has looked like that for a hundred and seventy-three years. The third banner rode down the spur in the week of the autumn market and the gate was open for the fair, and the town was full of horse-dealers from the belt and spice men from the south and a wine-agent from the west and silk-brokers from the east and a hundred of our own herdsmen come to sell, and by nightfall nobody in the walls was alive, and the host rode on at dawn with the stock and left the people where they lay. Each of them had a rite and none of the rites was done. The belt men had no priest. The southerners had no conch. The eastern men had no shrine. Ours were not laid out and not named.

"Here is what it is. The wind goes through the lanes and makes no sound. A coin dropped on the stone falls without ringing. Ten paces off, a man's voice goes flat and small, and across the square you see his mouth move and hear it as through felt. No bird nests in the walls. No fly comes within a bowshot. No fire stays lit: a wick catches and the flame stands up and lies down and goes out, and a spark dies in the pan, and the cold and the dark come at the same moment. And at night the hunters come into that. You cannot hear them coming. The first a man knows is the hand at his throat and the silence of his own shout. The belt factors who have tried it were found in their blankets in the morning with their mouths open and a cold on them that was not the season's.

"We hold the well. It is in the square and it is sweet. We draw it by rope from outside the gate and we sell it at the mouth of the gap and we take a string past the walls in daylight and we do not stop. We take nothing from inside, because nothing anyone has taken from inside has stayed his. The shops are stocked. The counting-house on the square has a barred door and a vault under it and the vault was never opened, and the fair's deposits are in it, silver in bags with a clerk's tally sealed in wax, and a house in exile in Amradad wants the tally and not the silver, and has said so to me, and I have named a price, and it has not been paid.

"On the fair's anniversary, from outside the gate, you can hear the stalls. Haggling. Faint. I have heard it. A priest of one of the peoples who lost men there wants to hold a market in the square on that day with a priest of each of the others, every rite at once, and thinks it might hold the silence back. He asked me what it would cost. I told him. He is still finding the money. That is the town. What it costs to go in is what you have. What you carry out is nothing, because it does not stay carried. What it costs to go past is what you paid me. I would sooner have the second price than the first, and so would you."

### The Master's Tale: the Water Nobody Camps At

The third night the master took the fire back, because the factor had asked him on the road why the string had halted within sight of a stand of heavy palms the day before and gone on thirsty.

"That was the best water on the road," he said. "It comes up cold through white sand into a pool the size of a threshing floor, and the palms round it are thicker than your waist and so heavy with dates that the trunks lean, and the grass is knee-high in a country where grass means a good year. There is no skull-pole and no well-stone and no sign in a palm, because it is the one water on the road that belongs to nobody, and it belongs to nobody because the second banner killed a tribe there over a morning, four thousand of them, and forbade the laying-out, and the dates fell on the bodies, and the next spring the grass was knee-high and it has never been less.

"It does not kill by itself. It does not need to. A string that fills its skins by day and is gone before the light turns yellow takes no lasting harm. A man who lingers by day hears the grass move against the wind and a low wet sound from the reeds like men breathing. A man who sleeps within sight of the palms dreams the morning, clear, and the dream does not end when he wakes, and a man who sleeps there three nights hears it in every quiet for the rest of his life. The dates stay on the palm. What falls is left to lie. The guides will not eat them. The sweetness is what gets into the dreams.

"There is a string there now. A merchant's string, in good order, tents pitched, animals fat and sleek and glassy-eyed, and the drivers asleep in their blankets and breathing, and none of them has woken in four days. The tribe whose range it is has offered a price to anyone who will go in by day and carry them out before dusk without lingering. Nobody has taken it. I did not tell you yesterday because you would have wanted to look. You can look on the way back. By day. From the saddle. We will not stop."

## Sovid: the Rider

The ninety-sixth day. Seventeen hundred miles. The last dune ended in a wall of black rock split by a cleft three camels wide, and the cleft ended at a cistern the size of a threshing floor with a stone hall beside it roofed with turf, and the wind that came down the cleft at evening smelled of snow for the first time in weeks. The riders of the sands brought the string in and the riders of the stone desert were waiting in the hall, and the master, who was going back west with the silk and was handing the factor's house on, laid the factor's master's tokens on the stone floor one by one and the Idvaqor riders picked them up and asked more questions than the riders at Yemök had, because the tribes of Idvaqor deal with the Empire and the Empire's customs-house examines the cargo, and they checked the tokens against a count of the camels. A string that arrives with fewer beasts than it left with is asked where they died.

"The price here is the highest on the road," the master told the factor, outside the hall, with the cold coming down. "The cistern fills with snowmelt in spring and has to last the year, and a string that comes late finds it low and the price of water doubled. The hall is open to any rider, and the tribe that holds the cleft will shelter a man with a token for the night, and will not feed him. In the loft are riders who lost their caravans on their own stretch. A rider who loses his string is not taken back by his tribe, and some of them come here and stay, and sell what they know of the stone desert to anyone who will pay, and some of what they sell is true. One of them knows a cistern in the hills that no tribe has found. He has known it for six years and he is still in the loft. Think about why.

"Now. You have asked me nine times since the March for my list, and I have said no nine times, and here is the tenth. A master's list is which dovaq he has paid and which he owes, which rider is whose cousin, which two ranges are at feud this year and must be crossed in a particular order, which well on the grass is not on anybody else's list, which Uruvol sold a street twice and to whom. It is the whole of what a master is. It is not written, because a thing written can be taken off a dead man at a well and sworn to. It is held in the head, and it is handed to a son, or to a factor who has become a master, when he can hold it. You cannot hold it. Not because you are a fool. Because you have crossed once, and the list is a thing you cross into. Every tale I have told you from the first toll-camp to this cleft is a line of it. The Tarûzeli who argued four weights. The blade for the night. The stone on the stair. The two tokens off the rider who was not dead. The lens and the man who faced north. The street sold twice. The town with its stalls up. The string asleep at the sweet water. The man in the loft who knows a cistern and is still in the loft. Those are the list. When you can tell them back to me in the order of the road, with the mile and the day and the price, and tell me what each one cost and what each man carried out, then you can hold the list, and I will give it to you, and you will be a master, and you will be afraid every day of the crossing for the rest of your life, as I am, which is the only way to come back from it.

"Go on east. The riders of Idvaqor will take you from here, six or eight or ten of them, each good for his tribe's stretch and not a yard further. Pay every price promptly. Argue none of them. Count your camels before they do. Sleep nowhere within sight of a thing a guide will not sell you a path to. I will see you at Qiiso in the autumn, if the budaq sits on somebody else's camel, and you will tell me the tales back, and if you have them right, I will tell you the ones I did not tell you this time, which are the ones that cost more."
