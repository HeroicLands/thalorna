**The Recitation of the Breaking**, _Turemtsemhat_, is the fixed text the [[lore-khazrynclt|Tellumi]] recite of Turem, the war in which the [[affiliation-tribestrzd|Atarzád]] took four of their seven cities. It is a _tsemhat_, a recitation: couplets, but plain ones, with no cloth and no reed, spoken standing by the eldest of a house at a princely funeral, by a Delkoshekh on the first day of the five-day festival, and at every formal ceremonial of the exile courts at Amradad, and spoken behind closed doors in the subject quarters of the four held cities, where it names the magistrate's own people. The Tellumi insist that it is not a lament and not a grievance: it is a case being kept open, and its form is the form of a case, each claim followed by the tablet that proves it. A reciter who departs from the words is corrected from the floor.

The rendering below is the text as the House of [[place-zargandur|Zargandur]] in exile recites it, which gives the second year its full weight because the war-house died in it; the other houses lengthen their own years and shorten this one. It names seven riders of the war-house, **Kireshay**, **Budishay**, **Pedunay**, **Garvanay**, **Perimay**, **Hageshay** and **Lirunay**, as the house's tablet names them, and leaves the princes to the tablet, because the recitation may not read a prince's name in a city where reading it is forbidden. The Atarzád tell the same twelve years as the War of the Twelve Years and the keeping of a Promise, and their account is set out in their own note; this one reports their claim in order to refuse it, which is what a case does. The field outside Zargandur's north wall, [[place-henhulpedin|Henhutpedin]], is where the recitation ends, and what it says of the lamps in the Tellumi quarter is the recitation's own account of a custom the quarter explains to nobody.

: The Recitation of the Breaking {#recitation}

```poetry {form=tsemhat lang=en}
This is the case of the seven cities, and the case is open.
Stand for it. It is not long. Nothing in it is spoken
that is not on a tablet, and the tablet will be named,
and where no tablet holds a thing, the thing will not be claimed.
Seven cities, and the order of them, as the first tablets give:
Shirvan, under the pale cliff, where the great spring broke, to live;
Ashkarad, of the workshops; Zargandur, of the horse;
Kethramír, where the spur comes down; Vahurdash, where the course
of the last stream fails; Mernagord, by the salt; Ushtra-ber, by the ice.
Seven springs. Seven houses. Seven tablets. Say it twice.
Seven. We came down from the mountains to them. That is the first claim,
and no one who has taken a city of ours has taken that from the name.

Now the guests. The tablet of the settlement says this:
in the ninth year before the war, in the month of the first melt,
there came to the edge of the belt
a people out of the west and the south, with their herds thin and their water spent,
twelve tribes under twelve chiefs, and they asked for the thing a stranger asks,
bread, and water, and shade, and no question, and we did not set them tasks.
We gave it. The tablet of Shirvan says the princes gave them the marginal ground
north of the gardens, where the streams give out, to graze on, and they found
it good, and stayed, and the tablet of Kethramír records the tithe they paid,
and the tablet of Zargandur records the horses they bought, and the oaths they made.
We gave the neyin's right to twelve tribes. We have not taken it back.
A stranger in extremity is fed. That is what we are. That is the track
the case runs on, and the Atarzád know it, and it is why they were inside the belt.
Say it plainly, because the record does: we fed them. It is what we felt
and what we did, and we would do it again, and that is not a boast.
It is the claim. A people who feed the stranger have the stranger's oath. At most
the stranger has their bread. The bread was given. The oath was not kept.
That is the first finding. Now the years. There are twelve. We have not slept.

The first year. They rose. The tablet of Zargandur gives the day:
the twelfth of the month of the high melt, when the herds were far away
on the marginal ground, and the twelve tribes came in from the grazing armed
and took the weirs north of the war-city, and the city was not alarmed,
because a guest does not take a weir, and the prince sent to ask them why,
and the one who answered said: the land is promised. That was the whole reply.
The land is promised. We will hear that sentence at every gate in the case.
It is their claim, and this is ours: that no god gives one people's place
to another people. The Flame is a god, and keeps the hearth, and is honored in his seat.
He is not a sovereign who grants a city. The tablet of Shirvan is complete
on the order of the gods, and has been for two thousand years, and he is in it,
and he grants no kingdoms in it. That is the finding. We do not need a minute
more on their theology. They had the weirs. The war had begun.
Twelve years. The first was the taking of the water. The second was the sun

going down on Zargandur. Hear this one slowly. It is ours.
The second year, the last morning, before the heat, in the first hours,
the war-house of Zargandur rode out of the north gate to meet the host
on the pasture, because the host was on the pasture, and the house was most
of what the belt had in the way of horse, and it was the strongest, and it went,
and it was killed to the last rider, and the princes with it, and the day was spent
by noon, and the city fell by noon, and that is the whole of the battle.
We do not sing the battle. We name the riders. They are on the tablet. Settle.
Kireshay, who held the left of the line, and did not give the ground.
Budishay, who carried the house's banner, and did not drop it; the staff was found
standing in the trench with the cloth rotted off it, and he beneath.
Pedunay, the drill-master, who taught the house its strokes, and died with his teeth
set, the tablet says, which is the only word it gives on how any of them died.
Garvanay, who bred the gray mares the Zhoqarod ride today, and rode one.
Perimay, who kept the gate-fires, and was the youngest, and was somebody's son.
Hageshay, who held the right, and was found with the princes, and had held.
Lirunay, who was a freed man of the house, and need not have ridden, and was felled
beside Hageshay, and the tablet says of him only: he was free, and he went.
And the princes, whose names the tablet holds and this recitation does not. They are lent
to the tablet until a Delkoshekh stands on the north grass and reads them where they lie.
That is the second year. It is not finished. The house stands for it. Eyes dry.

The Atarzád gave their own dead to the flame that night. That is their rite, and they kept it.
Our dead were left on the pasture. No Delkoshekh was let beyond the wall. He had not slept,
the quarter's Delkoshekh; he stood at the north gate with the house's tablet in his hands
and asked to go out and read the names, as the rite is, as every Tellumi understands
the rite to be: the name read from the clay, the lamps lit, and then the dead are received.
He asked. The gate was theirs. The worship of the Keeper was already forbidden. He believed
they would let him, because he was a priest. They did not let him. The lamps were not lit.
Three days the war-house lay on the north grass. On the third day the Zhoqarod dug a pit
and drove the bodies into it with their horses, and filled it, and the grass on the trench
is shorter than the grass on either side, and has been since, and the tablet gives the length.
The names were not read. The lamps were not lit. That is the second finding.
It is the one the case turns on, because the others are about land, and this is binding
in another court than theirs or ours. A man whose name is not read is not received.
The war-house is not received. A hundred years ago that would have been believed
by no one in the belt, that a Tellumi house could lie unread outside its own wall.
It lies there now. Hooves go over it every night at the hour the sortie rode. That is all
the tablet says about the field, and the quarter says the rest, and the Zhoqarod keep no picket there when the light has gone.
And this is why there is a lamp at every door in the Tellumi quarter from dusk to dawn:
they are the lamps that were not lit that night. We light them every night instead.
We have lit them for ninety years. The quarter does not explain it. The recitation does. Go ahead,
ask a chandler of Zargandur why she sells so many lamps. She will not tell you. Now you know.
That is the second year. The war went on for ten more. The other houses will tell you so.

The fifth year, Ashkarad, of the workshops. The tablet of Ashkarad gives the gate
and the hour, and the name of the man who opened it, which the House of Ashkarad does not state
in the recitation, because his line is still in the city and makes good tools.
Ashkarad fell by treachery and not by horse, and the Teghumod hold it, and the rules
of the workshops stand, and the craft councils sit, and the tribe buys what is made,
and the water-tunnels are family property shaft by shaft, and the tax is paid.
The eighth year, Kethramír, of the priests. The tablet of Kethramír gives the count
of the Delkoshekhs killed in the yards, who would not flee, and it is a large amount,
and the recitation gives the number when the House of Kethramír recites it, and we do not,
because it is theirs to give. The Tozhirod hold the city and rule it like scholars, by rote
and precedent, and the quarter has its Delkoshekh and its courts, and the magistrate reads the clay
and sometimes agrees with it. That is the eighth year. The ninth and tenth we do not say;
the tablets of those years are the siege of Shirvan, and the siege is a long account
of water, and who held the basin, and who held the cliff, and the amount
the city paid for grain, and nobody recites it, because it ends the same way.

The eleventh year, Shirvan. The first city. The holiest. The great spring under the cliff. The day
the Awakening count gives is the first of the month of the low water, and the wall
was opened at the basin gate, and the city was taken by nightfall, and that is not all.
The temple of the Keeper of Harmony was pulled down. Its stones are in the fire-temple now.
The great shrine of the Mother of the Oasis at the spring was pulled down, and the fires they vow
never to let go out were built over the holy water itself, and burn there, and the Tellumi do not drink
on that side of the basin, and have not said why to any Atarzád, and will not. Think
what the archive was. Two thousand years of clay, without a gap, in the temple's rooms:
the calendar, the kings, the treaties of the seven, the order of the gods, the tombs,
the water-hours of every household, and the commentary. It burned.
Clay does not burn well. That is the one mercy in the case, and it is not earned:
what the fleeing households carried out is scattered through the exile courts,
and what fell in the rubble is under the lower basin, in the mud, in the lower courts
of the spring, and comes up when a channel is dug, and the water-masters take it away before the overseer sees.
The tablets of the treaties between the seven are somewhere under the water-masters' keys.
That is the eleventh year. The twelfth was the hunting of the clergy, and the end.
Four cities taken. Three standing. One empty then, and settled since. We do not pretend
Vahurdash is a victory. It is a town among its own stones that drinks from two wells.
It is the one city ever recovered, and the recitation says so, and the tablet tells
the rest. Seven. Four. Three. That is the count. That is the state of the case.

What is held. The subject quarters. The old districts, the lanes too narrow for a cart. The place
a Tellumi lives in a city his grandfather governed. No land outside the quarter. No arms.
No public worship of any god but theirs. And the water-tax, which falls on Tellumi heads of household alone, and harms
by its name more than its weight, because the water is ours, and the engineers are ours,
and the canals do not run without us, and both peoples know it, and the city endures
on that knowledge. We keep the receipt. We keep two calendars. We keep the clay, and the clay is ours.
What is owed. Four cities. Four palaces. Two temples. One spring. The names unread. The lamps. Not flowers,
not compensation, not a treaty, not a quarter. When a thing is returned it is struck from the recitation,
and the recitation is shorter by that line, and that is the only end of it we want. Return. Not restoration
by another's grace. Revun. The word is on the tablet of every exile house, and under it the list.
This is the case. It is open. The reciter sits. The house stands. Nothing is dismissed.
```
