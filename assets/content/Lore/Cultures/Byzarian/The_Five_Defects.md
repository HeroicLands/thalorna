**The Five Defects** is the telling the clerks of the Merchant Hall of [[place-khryshteikhos2|Khrýshteíkhos]] give the city's new apprentices once a year, on the afternoon of [[lore-marendasdty|Mârendâs]]'s Dedication of the Apprentice, after the priests have blessed them and before their masters take them home. It is the [[affiliation-byzarianlg|Byzarian League]]'s foundation legend in the form Khrýshteíkhos tells it: a prose telling, given in the Hall itself by the oldest clerk who can still stand, with a fixed declaration at its end that the apprentices say back to him, line by line, and are expected to know by the next Dedication. The other four cities tell it too, each to its own apprentices and each to its own credit, and the telling below notes where their tellings part from this one.

It is the story of [[lore-heladcompact|the Compact of the Five]], sworn in 157 BF at the plateau's edge the year after the steppe host died between the springs ([[lore-heladwells|the Battle of the Wells]]). The four things the Compact bound the cities to are history, and the telling keeps them as the history does. The rest is the telling's own: five envoys who could agree on nothing for three days, the commander of the springs who had them declare what each city lacked before any of them sealed, and the order of the seals that put the bond, and later the Hall, where the last seal went down. The envoys are **Ánevkâr** of [[affiliation-nesthiros|Nêsthiros]], **Teíkhazûr** of [[affiliation-argyrteikhos|Argýrteíkhos]], **Órashazûr** of [[affiliation-orashion|Órashion]], **Tchîrnâlash** of [[affiliation-karvashion|Karvashion]] and **Agórankâr** of Khrýshteíkhos, as the clerks name them, and no city's record gives other names for them or confirms these. The commander of the springs is Strátimos Ghéranîdês of the pass-city, whom the record of the Wells names.

## The Clerk's Opening

Sit, apprentices, on the floor where you are, the floor of the Hall. You will stand on it a great deal before you are masters and sit on it only today. The priests have blessed you and your masters have signed your indentures, and before you go home with them you will hear why this Hall stands where it stands, which is a thing a clerk of the Hall is bound to tell and an apprentice of Khrýshteíkhos is bound to know. It was told to me on this floor by a clerk who was told it by a clerk, and I tell it as I heard it, and you will tell it as you hear it, adding nothing. At the end you will say the Declaration back to me. Listen for it.

## The Springs

You know that the east is dry. You know that the foothills below the passes have few springs, and that they run lowest at the end of summer. Learn now that a host knows it too.

In the year before the Compact a host came out of the steppe and over the passes at the end of summer, when the springs were lowest. It was a great host, more riders than any city of ours had men, and the east does not remember who led it or what tribe he was, because a host that does not go home leaves no one to say. It came over the passes knowing that it could not carry its water, and meaning to take ours.

The five cities each sent what men they had, and they put them under one man, because there was no time to quarrel about who should command, and the man was Strátimos Ghéranîdês of the pass-city, which you call Argýrteíkhos. Remember his name. It is the one name in this telling that every city tells alike.

Strátimos did not go out to meet the host on the grass, where every horse it rode would have counted against him. He walled the springs. Every spring in the foothills he ringed with a fort of stone and turf, and he put men from two cities in every fort, so that no fort was any one city's, and the host came down out of the passes and found every water it could reach under the arrows of men who could not be bought by any one city's quarrel. It went at the forts for eleven days and took none. Its horses died at the walls of arrows, and in the ground between the forts of thirst, and the field where they died is the field the March garrison keeps the jar at, which you will hear about if your master trades east. What was left of the host went back the way it had come.

Here is the first lesson, apprentices, before the telling begins: a host does not die of its strength. It dies of what it lacks.

## The Winter of the Garrisons

The host was gone, and the forts stood on the springs with the men of five cities in them, and the winter came, and the forts had to be fed.

Who would feed them? Nêsthiros said the springs were in the foothills, a long way from Nêsthiros, and that a harbor city had no business paying for soldiers on a mountain. Argýrteíkhos said the springs were below its walls, and that it had held the passes alone for longer than anyone could count, and that it was time somebody paid it for doing so. Órashion said it had made the arrowheads and was owed for them. Karvashion said that its caravans could not cross the foothills with garrisons in every fort charging a toll for the water. And Khrýshteíkhos, apprentices, said nothing, because Khrýshteíkhos was a mule-yard.

Hear that, and do not be offended at it; we are not. In the year of the Compact, Khrýshteíkhos was a town of mule-yards and toll-houses at the top of the last escarpment, where the lowland road from the straits comes up onto the plateau and the bales change from the ships' carts to the mules. It had a well, a market, a wall of mud brick with a gate in it, and a council of mule-masters and toll-farmers. It had nothing to sell but the place it stood in. Every bale that went east from the sea came through its gate, and every bale that went west from the mountains, and it lived on the toll, and it had no say in anything, because nobody had ever needed to ask it.

All winter the councils sent letters, and the letters crossed in the snow, and nothing was paid. In the spring Strátimos sent a letter of his own, the same letter to all five councils, and the clerks have kept its words, which are few: _I walled your springs. I will not hold them for five cities that will not pay me. Send a man to the top of the road, where the roads meet, before the barley is cut, or I will bring the garrisons home and the next host may drink where it likes._

He named the top of the road because it was the one place on the map that belonged to no city that mattered, and so no envoy could say he was being made to travel to a rival. That is how Khrýshteíkhos came to be chosen, apprentices. Not for its wealth. For its lack of weight.

## The Five at the Top of the Road

They came before the barley was cut, five envoys, each with his scribe and his servants and his city's die, and they lodged in the toll-houses of the mule-yard town, and the town's council gave them bread and wine and roast kid before it asked them anything, because it was the custom of the mule-masters to feed a caravan-master before naming him a price, and the custom of the mule-masters is the custom of the Hall.

From Nêsthiros came **Ánevkâr**, a ship-owner, who had the wind in his name and the sea in his walk, and who did not like the mules.

From Argýrteíkhos came **Teíkhazûr**, a captain of the wall, who had stood on it through the eleven days and did not let anyone forget it.

From Órashion came **Órashazûr**, a master of the forges, black in the creases of his hands where no washing reaches, who spoke slowly and meant everything.

From Karvashion came **Tchîrnâlash**, a caravan-master, who had a bell on his belt as caravan-masters do, and who smiled at everything and agreed to nothing.

From Khrýshteíkhos, which was the town they were sitting in, came **Agórankâr**, the warden of the mule-market, who was the youngest of the five and the poorest, and who poured the wine.

And Strátimos came down from the springs with two soldiers and sat at the end of the table, and said he was not an envoy, and would not speak until he was asked, and would not leave until they had sealed.

## The First Day: Who Speaks First

On the first day they could not agree who should speak first.

Ánevkâr said Nêsthiros should speak first, because it was the oldest city and the largest and its ships had brought the first settlers up from the straits. Teíkhazûr said Argýrteíkhos should speak first, because it had held the passes, and without the passes there would be no cities to speak. Órashazûr said that the city that made the arrows should speak first, since without arrows the passes would not have been held. Tchîrnâlash said, smiling, that he would gladly speak last, if the others would only agree that the caravans should pay no toll at the springs, and then he would not need to speak at all. Agórankâr said nothing, and poured the wine.

They argued until the lamps were lit, and nobody spoke first, because each of them knew that whoever spoke first would say what he wanted, and the others would know it, and would not give it to him. They went to bed. The mule-yard town fed them supper.

## The Second Day: Who Writes

On the second day they agreed to let every man speak at once, through his scribe, and to have each scribe write down what was agreed, so that the five copies could be compared at evening.

The scribes wrote all day. At evening the five copies were read aloud, one after the other, in the mule-yard toll-house, and they were five different bonds. Nêsthiros's copy had the springs paid for by the cities nearest them. Argýrteíkhos's copy had the passes held by Argýrteíkhos and paid for by the other four. Órashion's copy had the arms bought from Órashion at a stated price. Karvashion's copy had no toll at the springs and a great many clauses about caravans. And Khrýshteíkhos's copy, which Agórankâr's scribe had written, was the only one in which all five cities paid something, and it was the shortest, and nobody listened to it, because it was Khrýshteíkhos's.

They argued until the lamps were lit. They went to bed. The mule-yard town fed them supper. The clerks say Agórankâr paid for the suppers out of the market's own chest, and that the market's council asked him how long it was going to go on.

## The Third Day: Who Seals First

On the third day, tired of arguing, they agreed on a text. They did it by striking out of all five copies every clause that any one of them would not accept, and what was left was so short that it said almost nothing: that the five cities were friends, and would discuss the springs.

Then it came to the seals, and nobody would seal first.

Apprentices, you will meet this every day of your working lives, so learn it now. The man who seals first is bound before the others are. Until the last seal goes down he is holding a bond that binds him and nobody else, and the others may read it over, and think again, and ask for one clause more, and he has nothing to bargain with, because he has already given his word. So nobody seals first who can help it. Ánevkâr would not, nor Teíkhazûr, nor Órashazûr, nor Tchîrnâlash, and Agórankâr was not asked.

They sat at the table with the short text between them and their dies in their hands, and nobody moved, until the lamps were lit and the town's servants came in with supper, and still nobody moved. Then Strátimos Ghéranîdês stood up at the end of the table, and said he had been asked, though nobody had asked him, because he had heard enough.

## What Strátimos Said

This is what the commander of the springs said, as the clerks keep it, and you will hear it again at the end of your indenture, and at your examination for master, and when you sit on a council, if you ever do.

"I have sat here three days," he said, "and I have watched five men hide a thing from one another, and I know what it is, because I watched a host die of it last summer. The host did not die of our arrows. It died of thirst, because it lacked water and hid that it lacked it, and came over the passes as if it carried its own. If it had said at the top of the pass, we have no water, it would have bargained for it, or gone home. It said nothing, and it died between the springs.

"Each of you lacks something, and each of you is hiding it, and that is why none of you will seal first. You think that if you seal first the others will find out what your city lacks and make you pay for it. They will. They will find out anyway, the first winter. A bond sealed with a defect hidden is a bale sold with the rot at the bottom, and it holds until the rot is found, and then it is a quarrel, and the quarrel will be fought on my springs.

"So declare it. Each of you, before he seals, will say aloud in front of the others what his city cannot do without the rest. Then nobody is bound blind, because everybody knows what everybody else lacks, and nobody can be made to pay more for his lack than the others for theirs. A defect declared is no defect in the bargain. I will hear each of you, and I will not leave this table until all five have spoken."

## The Declarations

They did not want to. The clerks say there was a long silence, and that the lamps were trimmed during it.

Then Órashazûr of Órashion, who spoke slowly and meant everything, put his die on the table in front of him and spoke, because, the clerks say, a smith is used to saying what is wrong with the iron before he is paid for the blade.

"Órashion has iron and no road. Our arms go nowhere without your mules and your ships. If the passes close, or the road, we can make swords until the mountain is hollow and nobody will carry one of them away. That is what we lack."

Then Teíkhazûr of Argýrteíkhos, the captain of the wall, put his die on the table.

"Argýrteíkhos has the pass and the silver, and no bread. Our mines feed nobody. If the plateau does not send grain up the road we can hold the pass with silver for one winter, and the second winter we will open the gate to anyone who brings bread. That is what we lack."

Then Tchîrnâlash of Karvashion, who smiled at everything, stopped smiling and put his die on the table.

"Karvashion has the caravans and no wall that the steppe respects. Our bazaars are open on every side, because that is what a bazaar is. If a host comes down the road to us, we will sell it water until we have none, and then it will take the rest. That is what we lack."

Then Ánevkâr of Nêsthiros, who had the sea in his walk, put his die on the table last of the four, and the clerks say he did it as a man lays down a card he hoped to keep.

"Nêsthiros has the sea and no plateau. Our ships end at the harbor. A galley cannot carry a bale up the escarpment or hold a pass. If the road above us closes, we are a harbor full of ships with nothing to carry. That is what we lack."

Then they looked at Agórankâr of Khrýshteíkhos, the youngest and the poorest, who had poured the wine for three days and had not been asked anything, and Strátimos said: "And you."

Agórankâr put the mule-market's die on the table, which was a die of plain brass with a mule cut in it, and the clerks have it still.

"Khrýshteíkhos has nothing but the place where your roads meet. Without your roads we are a mule-yard. That is what we lack, and it is everything."

## The Four Bindings

When the five had declared, the clerks say the room was easier, because there was nothing left in it for anybody to find out, and they wrote the bond again from the beginning in one evening, through one scribe, and read it aloud once, and it was the Compact as the Hall keeps it.

They bound themselves in four things, apprentices, and you will learn them now as the history keeps them, and you will learn them so that you could say them in your sleep:

- **Common tariffs**, so that no city undercut another at the passes.
- **A joint muster**, so that the force raised for the next host would not be raised in the middle of it.
- **The passes held by a council**, in which each city sat, and no city alone.
- **Disputes between cities brought to that council**, and not carried to the field.

Hear what each binding answers. Órashion lacked a road, and the tariffs kept the road the same price for everyone. Argýrteíkhos lacked bread, and the muster brought bread up with the men. Karvashion lacked walls, and the passes held in common kept the host off its bazaars. Nêsthiros lacked the plateau, and a council in which each city sat gave the harbor a voice on the mountain. And Khrýshteíkhos lacked everything but the place, so it was given the one thing a place can hold: the table at which the others sat.

## The Order of the Seals

Then they sealed, and they sealed in the order they had declared, because that was what Strátimos asked, and nobody had the face to argue with him after the declarations. Órashion first, because Órashazûr had declared first. Then Argýrteíkhos, then Karvashion, then Nêsthiros. And Khrýshteíkhos last.

Learn this too: the last seal closes the bond. Until it goes down the others are bound and the bond is not; when it goes down, the bond is whole, and it stays where the last seal was struck, in the keeping of the one who struck it, until the parties agree otherwise. The bond stayed in the mule-yard town, in Agórankâr's keeping, because his seal went down last, and the four did not agree otherwise, because no one of them would let another take it home. So it stayed. They built a room for it, and then a hall around the room, and then the five councils met in the hall, because that was where the bond was, and that is the Merchant Hall, apprentices, and you are sitting on its floor.

That is how Khrýshteíkhos, which had nothing but the place, came to hold the table. Not by being first. By declaring that it had nothing, and sealing last.

## The Republic's Envoy

You will want to know what the Republic did. It was the Republic's country, after all, in those years, or it thought so.

In the summer after the Compact an envoy of the Republic came east with a small escort and a large letter, and the five cities received him in the new hall, which had a roof by then, and he read the letter aloud. It said that the cities had made a league without leave, and that the Republic required them to dissolve it and submit their passes to the Republic's officers.

Agórankâr, who held the bond, fed the envoy first, because that is the custom, and then asked him how many officers the Republic could send to the passes that year. The envoy did not say. The cities knew, because the Republic had its own factions to put down that year and its Riders to train, and no men to spare for a war in the east. So the five offered him a yearly payment instead of the passes, and named the sum, and declared the defect in the offer before he could find it, which was that the sum was less than the passes were worth to the Republic and more than a war would cost it that year. The envoy took the payment, and the Republic took it every year after, and left the eastern cities free of its officers and its garrisons for three and a half centuries.

That is the League's foreign policy, apprentices, from that summer to this one. Be worth more paid than conquered. Declare the defect in the offer. Feed the envoy first.

## Where the Other Cities Part

You will hear this telling in other cities, and you will hear it told otherwise, and you will keep your mouth shut, because a guest does not correct his host's history at his host's table. But you should know what you are hearing.

In Nêsthiros they tell it with Ánevkâr declaring first, and declaring nothing at all, because the sea lacks nothing, and the others declaring their lacks to him. In Nêsthiros the bond stays in Khrýshteíkhos because Agórankâr would not give it back.

In Argýrteíkhos there are no envoys in the telling to speak of. Strátimos does everything. He walls the springs, he writes the letter, he calls the meeting and he makes the five declare like schoolboys, and the five are schoolboys in that telling, and the bond is his.

In Órashion they tell it almost as we do, and they are proud that Órashazûr declared first, and they are right to be.

In Karvashion they tell it with a sixth defect. Tchîrnâlash, they say, declared the bazaar's open walls and kept back one more thing that Karvashion lacked, and has never said what it was, and the caravan-masters of Karvashion smile when you ask. We do not believe them, apprentices. A defect kept back is not a joke. It is the thing the whole telling is against. Ask your master what a caravan-master's smile is worth.

## The Reading

The chair of the League's council opens every session in this Hall by reading the Compact aloud, and the clerks know the old text well enough to hear a clause go by that is not in it. Whether the copy he reads is the one the five sealed at the top of the road, or a copy made since, the Hall has never said, and the clerks say it is the same weight, which is what a clerk says.

When you are masters, and stand on this floor on a session day, you will hear it read. Listen for the four bindings. You will not hear the five defects in it. The defects are not in the Compact, apprentices. They were said before it was sealed, which is why it holds. They are in you, and here is where they are kept.

## The Declaration

Stand up now, and say it back to me, a line at a time. You will know it by the next Dedication, and you will say it to your own apprentices when you are masters, and you will say it to yourselves on the morning you are tempted to seal a bond with the rot at the bottom.
