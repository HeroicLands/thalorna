**The Life of Kitvarion of the Gate** is the Life the monks of the eastern rite at [[place-argyrteikhos2|Argirteikhos]] read aloud in their refectory on the day their gate-stander died, and lend, with the house's seal, to any monastery of the rite that asks to copy it. It is a Life in the high style, written in the long sentences of the rite's chroniclers by a monk of the house who did not know the saint, and it says so; he wrote it from the gate-book Kitvarion kept, from what the house remembered, and from what the garrison told. It is the house's account of [[lore-heladcitadel|the Two Winters]], when the Silver Citadel held against the legions, and of the man the house holds to have outlasted them.

The house keeps the gate-book still, in the cell above the east gate where it was written, and keeps beside it a second book that it does not open, which the Life describes and does not quote. **Kitvarion** is the name the house gave him at his taking-in: _kitvar_ is a ledger, and the name says what the brothers already called him. The name his mother gave him is in the city's register, and the Life does not use it. The legate who took the city and the prefect who first sat with its council are left unnamed, as the house leaves them. The Life calls the commander of the Citadel by his name, Mathirkar Gheranikot, and calls the steppe peoples beyond the pass only that, since it names no one it cannot place.

## The Prologue

Brothers, the life of a holy man is a debt the house owes, and this is the house paying it late. We who write it did not know him. We know the book he kept, which is in the cell above the east gate where he kept it, and which any brother may open who washes his hands first; we know what the old brothers told the young ones, and the young ones told us; and we know what the garrison says on the night of his day, which is less than we know and more than they will write. Of the rest we know nothing, and where we know nothing the Life says so, as he would have said so. A Life that is fuller than its sources is a bale weighed heavy. He would not have it, and we do not offer it.

Hear, then, the life of Kitvarion of the Gate, the counter of the trains, the gate-stander, the exact; who ate his portion and not less; who wrote the true count when the false one would have comforted; who sat with the second winter's dying and wrote them down; who read the clause aloud at the foot of the mountain; and who outlasted the legions, though he died before they went, because the book outlasted them, and the book was his life.

## His Birth and the Sign in It

He was born in a spring-fort of the foothills below the pass, in a snow, the son of a muleteer of the road and of a woman of the mining villages above the city, and the fort's soldiers stood his mother by the fire and sent to the city for a midwife who could not come up the road until the snow was off it. So he was delivered by the fort's surgeon, who had delivered mules and men and no infant, and who wrote in the fort's daybook, after the night's count of the trains, one boy, alive. That is the first entry in his life, and it is in a ledger, and the brothers do not think it an accident.

His father led trains of the League's mules up from the plateau and over the pass to the outer caravanserais and back, and the boy went with him from the time he could sit a pack-saddle. The sign that was on him from the beginning was the count. He counted the mules of every train that passed them on the road, and the bales on every mule, and the bells on every train, and he was never wrong. The muleteers made a game of him, and then a use, and a caravan-master who had lost a mule in the dark would send to the boy rather than walk back along the road, and the boy would say how many had passed the third spring at dusk and how many the fourth at moonrise, and the master would know where the mule had gone. The old brothers say he counted a train once in the dark by its bells alone, standing in a fort's gateway with his eyes shut, and gave the count of mules and the count of bales and the house of the mark burned on them, and that the fort's officer went out with a lamp and found each of the three exact. We set this down as the brothers set it down. It is not in the gate-book, which begins later.

The second sign was the one the house holds the greater. When he was a boy of nine or ten years, a steppe trader came in under the truce to the outer caravanserai with horses, and the League's collector counted the horses at the gate and set the toll, and the boy, who was standing by, said that the collector had counted one horse twice. The collector struck him. The boy said it again. The collector counted the horses over in front of the steppe trader and his men, and found one horse less, and set the toll again, and the steppe trader went in paying for the horses he had brought and not for one more. He did not thank the boy, and the boy did not wait to be thanked. He had declared a defect in his own people's count, to a foreigner's profit, at the cost of a blow, and he did not think he had done anything but count. Brothers, the whole Life is in that afternoon. What came after is the same afternoon kept for sixty years.

## His Taking-In

His father died on the road when the boy was fourteen, under a rockfall at the narrows of the pass road, and the mules with him; the boy was at the caravanserai and was not taken. His mother sent him to the house, because the house takes the sons of the road who have no trade, and because the collector who had struck him had told the gate-wardens about the horse, and the gate-wardens had told the house.

The abbot of that year asked him what he could do. He said he could count. The abbot asked him whether he could count what was not in front of him, and he did not understand the question, and the abbot sent him to the kitchen. He served in the kitchen for two years, and the cellarer's accounts for those years are the cleanest in the house's chest, which the cellarers since have not forgiven him. In the third year the abbot put him to the scriptorium to learn his letters, and he learned them in a winter, and in the spring the abbot gave him the name he is known by, because the brothers had begun to call him the ledger already and the abbot did not hold with a name that is used behind a man's back and not to his face.

There was a fast in the house that year, kept by some of the brothers beyond the rule, and the young brother Kitvarion would not keep it. The brothers who fasted thought him greedy. The abbot asked him why he ate when his brothers did not, and he said that the rule stated a portion, and that a fast which is not in the rule is a term bent, and that if he ate less than the portion he would be saying that the rule had weighed his bread heavy, and he did not think it had. He ate his portion, every day of his life, to the crumb, and not less, and not more. The brothers who fasted beyond the rule are not remembered. This is the paradox of his abstinence, and we ask you to sit with it: his fast was exactness. He took what was his and left what was not his, and in sixty years of eating he never left a crumb on the board and never asked for one more.

## The Gate

The house of the east gate has kept the record of what crosses the pass since the house was founded, and keeps it still, a book at a time, one brother to a book. A brother keeps the gate-book until he dies or can no longer climb to the cell, and then the abbot names the next. When the brother who kept the book in Kitvarion's young manhood went blind, the abbot named him, and he kept it from that day until the day before he died, and his books are the longest the house has.

What he did was this. He stood in the east gate from the first light to the last, in every weather, and he wrote what crossed: every train, its master, its count of beasts and bales, the marks burned on the bales, the toll paid; every soldier of the March, going out and coming in; every steppe envoy under the truce, with his escort and the gifts he carried and the gifts he took away; every monk of the house who went down to the caravanserais to speak with the peoples beyond; and every dead man carried in. He wrote standing, at a desk the house's carpenter built to his height, with a hood of oiled felt over it against the snow. The soldiers of the gate called him the gate-stander, and so do we.

Brothers, do not think it a small thing. Holy men of other rites go up onto pillars and into caves to be nearer the gods. He stood in a gateway to be nearer the count. He stood there in the snow of the high pass, which falls from late autumn to the spring, and in the dust of midsummer, which comes off the steppe and fills the mouth, and he stood there on the feast days when the gate was shut and nothing crossed, and wrote at the head of the page the day and the words _nothing crossed_, because a page left blank is a page on which anything may afterward be written. That was his asceticism. Not hunger, not the scourge, not the cold for its own sake: the page kept true from the first light to the last.

He learned the steppe tongue at the gate, from the envoys and the horse-traders and the drovers of the winter camps, and spoke it well enough that the abbot sent him down to the caravanserais with the brothers who went to speak with the peoples beyond, and the steppe elders learned his face. They called him by a word in their tongue that the house wrote down and that means, the old brothers said, the one who does not add. A steppe elder who came in under the truce in his old age told the abbot that the clans would take a monk's word at the gate because one monk at the gate had once told a collector that he had counted a horse twice. The house holds this the first miracle of his life: that a blow taken at ten was repaid at sixty, by a stranger, in the stranger's own coin.

## The Coming of the Legions

When the legions came over the mountains and the five cities were cut from the sea and then from one another, the Citadel did not open its gates. Its commander, Mathirkar Gheranikot, shut the west gate and cut the road below it, and the legions came up the road to the cut and could come no further, and began to build. They built their ramp up the mountainside below the walls, and two camps beside it, and the city above them watched the ramp grow by the width of a hand in a day, and knew how long a hand is and how far the wall is, and did the sum.

The east gate stayed open a little while, and then it was shut too, because a gate that is open to the steppe while the legions are below the west wall is a gate that a garrison cannot watch with half its men. Kitvarion wrote at the head of the next page the day, and the words _the gate is shut by order of the commander_, and below it _nothing crossed_. The brothers asked him what he would keep now that the gate was shut. He said that the city was a gate too, and that he would keep what crossed it.

Here begins what the house holds the greater part of his life, and the part the city keeps.

## The First Winter

The city was fed through two winters with its road cut, and the mines are why. The silver mines run in tunnels under the mountain, and some of the tunnels come out a long way from the walls, and the grain and the water came in by them, and some say the sick went out. Which of the tunnels carried the grain, and from where it came, and whose it was, is not in the gate-book, because he did not write it there. He wrote it in another book, which we come to. The house has never said, and the house has been asked by every prefect and every governor since.

The grain came in, and the grain was counted, and he was set to count it. The quartermaster of the garrison asked the abbot for a brother who could keep the city's grain-book, and the abbot sent the one brother whose books no one had ever found a fault in. Kitvarion went down from the gate to the granaries under the citadel and kept the book of what came in by the tunnels and what went out to the barracks and the kitchens and the houses, bushel by bushel, from the first snow of the first winter.

In the middle of that winter the grain was short. The tunnels had brought less than the quartermaster had promised the commander, and the quartermaster came to the granary at night and told the brother to write a fuller count than there was in the bins. The garrison was afraid, he said, and the city was afraid, and if the commander read them a short count at the morning muster the gate would be opened from inside by men who would sooner eat in a legion's camp than starve in their own city. Write the bins full, he said, and we will fill them when the tunnels bring more, and nobody is harmed by a number.

Brothers, hear what he answered, because the house has kept it word for word, and it is the center of his life:

"A bin written full and standing half-empty is a bale with the defect hidden. You are not asking me to comfort the garrison. You are asking me to sell it a short weight and call it a mercy. The garrison will eat what is in the bin and not what is in the book, and on the day the bin is empty and the book is full, the garrison will know that the book lied, and after that it will believe nothing that is read to it, and that is the day the gate is opened from inside. Write the count that is in the bins. Let the commander read it at muster. Let the city know what it has, and halve the portion, and hold."

The quartermaster went to the commander, and the commander came down to the granary himself with a lamp, and counted the bins with the brother, bin by bin, through what was left of the night. In the morning Mathirkar Gheranikot stood up at muster and read the true count aloud to the garrison, and halved the portion of every man in the city, his own first. The garrison did not open the gate. The house holds that it was the true count that held it shut; the garrison says it was the commander who halved his own bread first. Both are true, brothers, and the second could not have been done without the first.

He halved his own portion with the city's, to the crumb. It was the only change in his eating in sixty years, and it was not a fast; it was the new portion, and he ate it exactly.

## The Second Winter

The second winter was worse, and the city does not say how much worse. Its own account of the siege is that the mines fed it and that the terms it took were the terms it would have asked for on the first day, and it gives no number. The number is in a book.

When the second winter came the sickness came with it, which comes into a hungry city as the snow comes into a pass, and the dying lay in the barracks and the houses and the passages of the citadel, and there were more of them than there were priests or physicians or brothers to sit with them. Kitvarion left the granary-book to a brother he had trained, and went among the dying, and sat with them.

He did not heal them. He was not a physician and he did not pretend to be. What he did was this: he sat by each one who was dying, and asked his name and his house and his trade and his guild, and what he owed and what was owed him, and wrote it down; and he read it back to him, so that the dying man heard his own account read aloud before witnesses, as a man settles his accounts on his bed, calling in what is owed and owning what he owes. The dying of the second winter had no beds of their own and no witnesses of their house and no keepers paid for them, and many had no heirs inside the walls. He was their witness. He wrote their accounts and he read them back, and he said over each the words the brothers say, that the account is heard; and when they died he wrote under each account that the man had died, and the day, and the line _he was kept_, which is the line we write. Brothers, he wrote it for every one of them, before anyone had paid for their keeping, because he knew it would be paid.

The house holds the second miracle of his life to be this. He sat with the dying by a lamp, because the passages under the citadel are dark at noon, and the lamp was filled once from the house's oil, at the beginning of the second winter, and it was not filled again because the house had no more oil to send down, and it burned all that winter. It did not burn brighter than a lamp burns, or longer than a lamp burns in a night; it burned every night of that winter on the one filling, as a lamp would burn if its oil were exactly as much as the winter needed. On the morning the terms were sealed it went out, and when the brothers looked into it, it was dry. We do not say the god filled it. We say the oil was exact.

The book of the second winter's dead is the second book in his cell. He sealed it with the house's seal on the day the terms were read, and wrote across the seal that it was to be opened when every man in it was kept, and not before. It has not been opened. Every year on his day the house pays a sum to the keepers at [[place-nykhtion|Nikhtion]] for the keeping of the second winter's dead, all of them, by the book's count, and the keepers have never asked the count, because the house's seal is on the receipt. The governors have asked. The house says: when every man in it is kept. That is why the city does not say the number, brothers. The number is in a book the city does not open, and the city knows exactly where the book is.

## The Clause at the Foot of the Mountain

At the end of the second winter the commander sent down to the legions to ask for terms, and the legate gave terms, and the commander sent the terms down again to be written fair and sealed. The legate's scribes wrote in the tongue of the empire and the city's in its own, and the commander asked the house for a reader who could read both aloud before the seals went down, as a scribe reads a bond aloud before it is sealed, so that no man sealed what he had not heard. The abbot sent Kitvarion, who had learned the imperial letters from the papers of the western merchants that crossed the gate before the war.

He went down the ramp the legions had built, which the children of the city now call the Vylarian Steps and play on, and into the legate's camp, with the commander's envoy and two soldiers of the garrison, and was given the two copies, and read them aloud: first the city's, in its own tongue, and then the legate's, in the empire's, clause by clause, to the legate and the envoy and the officers of both sides, standing.

The city's copy said that the city kept its walls and its own garrison. The legate's copy said that the city kept its walls and a garrison.

He stopped reading. The legate asked why he had stopped. He said that the two copies were not the same weight, and read the two clauses aloud one after the other, so that every man in the tent heard the one word that was in the one and not in the other. A garrison is any garrison. Its own garrison is the city's. He said that he would not read further until the copies matched, because the seals would be going down on two different terms, and a bond sealed on two different terms is a quarrel sealed, and the quarrel would come due at the worst hour for both sides.

The legate's scribe said it was a fault of the copying. The legate looked at the scribe, and then at the monk, and then he had the scribe write the word into the legate's copy in front of them all, and Kitvarion read the clause again, from both copies, and they were the same weight, and he read on to the end. The seals went down. The city kept its walls and its own garrison, and an imperial prefect of the pass sat with its council and spoke for the Orzas, and the prefect's seat is the military governor's seat now, on the council of the League, beside the merchant-princes. The garrison says the governor sits there because of one word read aloud at the foot of the mountain. The house does not say so. The house says the word was in the city's copy, and that he only read it.

He came back up the ramp and went to the cell above the east gate, which had been shut two years, and opened the gate-book, and wrote at the head of the page the day, and the words _the terms are sealed; the gate is opened by order of the commander_, and below it the first train that crossed, which was a train of grain from the plateau, with its count of mules and its count of bales and its marks.

## The Long Keeping

He kept the gate-book for more than thirty years after the Two Winters, and the house has every one of them. He wrote the imperial collectors going out to the caravanserais and coming in with the tolls, and the province's soldiers going out to build the forts of the pass road, which the League holds now and calls its own, and he wrote nothing about the forts that he had not seen from the gate. He wrote the steppe envoys coming in under the province's truce as they had come under the League's, and the steppe elders still asked for him at the caravanserais, and still took his word.

He outlived the legate, who went west and died in another province. He outlived the first prefect and the second. He outlived the abbot who named him and the abbot after that one, and he outlived every soldier who had stood muster on the morning the true count was read, and at the end of his life the garrison sent its new conscripts up to the cell to look at the old man who had kept the book through the Two Winters, and he would tell them the count of the bins on that morning, bushel for bushel, from memory, and they would go down again and say that he was exact.

Brothers, the legions were the strongest thing in the world in his youth. They took the five cities in five years and held them for three centuries. They are gone now, and what is left of them on the mountain below our walls is a ramp the children play on and two flat places where the garrison drills. The book is in the cell. He did not outlive the legions in his body. He outlived them in the book, which is his body as the rite counts it, and that is what we mean when we say he outlasted them. Endurance is the victory of the saints, and there is no other kind of victory that lasts the length of a book.

## His Death

When he could no longer climb to the cell, he asked the abbot to name the next brother to the gate-book, and the abbot named one he had trained, and he gave the book into the young brother's hands at the desk in the gateway, with the hood of oiled felt over it, and showed him how to write the head of the page.

Then he settled his accounts, as every man of the five cities settles them, aloud, before witnesses, on his bed. The house came to hear, and the abbot, and men of the garrison, and the gate-wardens, and a steppe elder who was at the caravanserai under the truce that month and came up to the city when he heard. Kitvarion called in what was owed him, and it was nothing; he had lent nothing, and no man owed him a crumb. He owned what he owed, and it was the house's portion of bread for sixty years, which he said he had eaten, and the oil of the second winter, which he said the god had measured and he had only burned, and one thing more. He said he owed the city the number of the second winter's dead, and that he would not pay it, and that he declared the debt so that it would be known to be owed and not forgotten: when every man in the sealed book was kept, the house should open the book and read the number aloud in the Hall, and then the debt would be paid.

He asked who would keep him. He had no house of his own and no heirs, and a monk's keeping is the house's, but the house's endowment at the city of the dead is a small one, and covers its brothers in a common chamber, and he did not ask for more. The garrison asked for more. The soldiers of the gate, who had stood in it with him in the snow, gathered a sum among themselves, every man of them, and bought him a keeping of his own at Nikhtion, a small chamber in the district of the common markers, with a perpetual endowment in the name of the garrison of the east gate of Argirteikhos, and they brought the receipt to his bed and read it to him aloud. The house holds this to have been the last thing he heard. He asked to see the receipt, and they gave it to him, and he read the sum, and said that it was exact. Then he died.

The endowment is in the garrison's name still, and the garrison pays it still, every year on his day, from a chest the conscripts put a coin into when they first stand guard at the east gate. They do not say why. The house says why.

## His Miracles

The house holds three miracles of him after his death, and sets down only these, because these are the ones it can show.

The first is the gate-book. The page on which he last wrote, which is the page before the young brother's first, has never yellowed with the pages around it, and its ink has not browned, and the place is found at once by the whiteness of the leaf.

The second is at his chamber in the city of the dead. The priests who keep the chamber say that a man who stands in it and states a false count, of anything, cannot finish the sentence; and the house holds that a merchant of the plateau, accused at the caravanserai of counting a toll short, was taken to the chamber by his accusers and asked to state his count there, and stood with his mouth open and could not, and paid the toll, and then stated the true count without trouble, and went home. The priests do not say this is a miracle. They say it is a chamber the garrison pays for.

The third is the steppe elders. On his day, which falls in the snow, when the pass is shut and nothing crosses, there is always a steppe envoy at the outer caravanserai under the truce, though no truce-day falls then, and the envoy asks to go up to the city, and is taken up, and stands in the east gate for a little while in the snow, and goes down again. The gate-book records it every year, under the head of the page: _nothing crossed; an envoy of the peoples beyond stood in the gate._ The house has asked the elders why. The elders say he did not add.

## The Prayer

Brothers, we have told you what we know, and where we know nothing we have said so. Pray to him for the true count, and for the portion eaten to the crumb, and for the courage to read aloud the one word that is in one copy and not in the other. Pray for the second winter's dead, whom he kept before they were paid for, and for the day the sealed book is opened, which is the day every man in it is kept. Pray for the garrison of the east gate, which pays his keeping without saying why. And when you stand in a gateway in the snow, and nothing crosses, write at the head of the page that nothing crossed, and leave no page of yours blank, so that nothing may afterward be written on it that you did not see.

Here ends the Life of Kitvarion of the Gate, the exact, the gate-stander, the one who does not add.
