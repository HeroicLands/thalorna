Provenzia is rich, clever, and entirely convinced that being both is a moral achievement. Its cities are marble and aqueduct and amphitheater, half of it inherited from the Vylarian Empire and half built since out of trade money. What holds a Provenzian life together is less the crown, which shares the kingdom with a Council of Peers and with cities of chartered rights, than three things: the city he belongs to, the patron above him and the clients below, and the exhausting and genuinely serious business of being seen to have taste.

## The City Is the Country

Each city is its own polity with its own character: some are ruled by hereditary nobility, some by elected councils of wealthy citizens, and a few by guilds of merchants or artisans who dispensed with the pretense some generations ago. A city's council is its _kurtema_, and the open square where the city does its business in public is its _rembalis_. Inside the bound of the city, the _tirutrima_, no blade is drawn.

A Provenzian's first loyalty is to his city and it is not close. He names it before his family, defends it against a neighboring city with real heat, and regards the man forty miles up the coast as a foreigner with a funny accent. Regional feeling exists chiefly when a Vylarian or a Nordman appears, at which point it materializes at once and dissolves the moment the threat leaves. On the eastern frontier the same energy takes the form of a family quarrel with the Tarvenans, kept below the level of a war.

The consequence is obvious and the Provenzians live with it cheerfully: nothing here is ever decided once. Every treaty is provisional, every alliance is a season's arrangement, and a merchant house that cannot read the standing of four cities at a time will not last a generation.

## Patronage

The relation that actually organizes daily life is patron and client, and it runs from the top of a city to the bottom without a break. A patron, the _daimela_, provides money, protection, introductions and the use of his name; a client provides attendance, loyalty, votes where there are votes, and the visible fact of being one more person in a great man's train. The client's daily attendance at the patron's door is the _pramolza_, the morning call.

Neither side finds anything demeaning in this. A man with no patron is not free—he is unattached, _anzirote_, which in Provenzia is a description of vulnerability.

Artists live inside the arrangement and not beside it. A painter, sculptor, playwright or natural philosopher works for a patron, and the patron's standing rises with the work's reputation, and Provenzia therefore produces a great deal of art and very little art critical of anyone who could afford to commission it.

## Courtly Love

The tradition of courtly love is an elaborate social code governing romance, patronage and artistic expression, and its influence has spread across the whole Vylarian Sea region. Foreigners consistently make two mistakes about it.

The first is to think it is about love. It is about conduct—the precise management of admiration, obligation, discretion and public regard—and a great deal of it is transacted between people with no romantic interest in one another whatever.

The second is to think it is frivolous. The code is how patronage gets negotiated without either party having to say the ugly part aloud, and a Provenzian who plays it badly loses real money and real access. A visitor who treats it as a parlor game is indulged, and then quietly excluded.

## Learning as Standing

Education is valued and the valuing is sincere. The academies at the great cities draw students from across Ankaris for natural philosophy, mathematics, rhetoric, law and the magical arts, and a Provenzian family that can afford it will put a son through one whatever he intends to do afterward.

The result is a society where a merchant quotes philosophy at dinner and means it, and where a man's ability to speak well is a legible asset. Rhetoric is the skill the culture actually rewards most: a Provenzian will admire a well-made argument he disagrees with, and remember it longer than the conclusion.

## Manners

Provenzian manners are ornate, indirect and fast. Compliment is a medium of exchange, a direct refusal is a discourtesy, and a plainly-spoken foreigner is charming for about one evening and tiresome afterward.

None of this indicates insincerity, and Provenzians are irritated by the accusation. The ornament is the vocabulary: a Provenzian can say something exact and cutting inside it that another Provenzian hears perfectly, and outsiders who cannot hear it assume nothing is being said.

Appearance matters and is not thought shallow. Dress, table, house and the company a family keeps are the public record of its standing, and a family that lets that record slip is understood to be in trouble before anyone has seen the books.

## Placed and Unplaced

The Provenzian answer to what is good is conduct done well: **good is the duty discharged in its order and with grace, the favor given without a price named, and the secret kept; evil is the patron who abandons his clients, the confidence betrayed, and the blade drawn inside the bound of a city.** The language has one word for duty, _jerval_, and it is a list: the house, the patron, the city, the clients, in that order. A patron who abandons his clients has destroyed the only thing that made him a patron, which is why the culture calls the abandoned client a wrong and not a misfortune. The private name a friend or a lover is given is not spoken in public, and using it there is a serious breach of intimacy. Sincerity, _tennairsa_, is the truth of a heart as a lyric claims it, and the culture holds the claim and the fact to be separate questions.

Success is to be placed. Precedence, _zirotal_, is worked out before anyone sits down, and a Provenzian is a success when he is found on the right step of it by people competent to judge. The marks are legible: a patron's train that is long, a house whose dress and table and company are in order, a name the city has given as an earned name after a deed (_kiramarle_), and a school or a bridge or a bench that carries the family's name. Failure is to be unplaced. The family that lets its record slip is in trouble before the books are opened, and the worst failure is the unnaming (_nearle_), the striking of a name from a city's rolls.

A Provenzian values weight, _solvima_, the grave bearing proper to a person of standing; rhetoric and craft; a library and the school that keeps it; the beautiful made to last; and the patron who funds all of them. He values least the plain statement that offends nothing and persuades nobody, the unattached man, the foreigner who cannot hear the ornament, and the kind of art that criticizes its commission.

## The Man Admired

[[being-ordnbrylnd|Ordan Brilonde]], a dealer in bullion and cut stone, is the Provenzian ideal at the scale of a trade. He heads one of the minor noble houses of Lizela, rich enough to matter in the district and not landed enough to matter at Jalnasdal, and buys gold, silver and gems where they come ashore, weighs and values them himself, and sells to the goldsmiths of Válaren and the households of the capital, where his weight on an ingot is taken without a second scale. He has never sold short weight and never overvalued a stone for a buyer who wanted it overvalued, which has cost him custom and made the custom he has. He teaches the young traders of the coast, holds his seal from the Merchants' Guild of Jalnasdal, and keeps his house out of the long quarrel between the Crown and the Council of Peers.

The admiration sits in a culture of compliment, where a valuation is also a courtesy, and the households of the court send to him for the one that will not flatter. Its cost is visible too. His house is small, sound and uninteresting to the great, he has a nephew who wants otherwise, and a Peer who asked for a valuation and did not get the one he wanted has not forgotten it. The culture admires him because the patron's whole worth rests on the weight of his word, and Ordan is the man whose word is the weight.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-arnelkrsvr|Arnela Kórisvar]] of Lizela is admired for what a patron builds. She inherited a solvent, unremarkable estate from a cautious father and used its modest income to found a small academy of navigation, natural philosophy and commercial law for the sons and daughters of merchants and tradesmen. In a kingdom where noble prestige is measured by patronage, the move was shrewd, and it was also sincere: she reads widely, writes to scholars at the chapterhouse of the Epistémion (the Academy of Knowledge) in Durvalis, and spends evenings debating with her instructors when she should be reviewing accounts. She brokered the fishing compact between Lizela and Kalveza, which is now a teaching text in commercial law, chairs the harbor commission, and represents her town at court.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the man's. Ordan earns his standing by weighing truly, and Arnela by making a school that will outlast her. The culture says that funding a school is the most reliable way a wealthy person has of being remembered well, and in Arnela it is plain that the shrewd act and the sincere one are the same act. The cost is visible in her too. A traditionalist lord blocks her proposals on the charter council because educating commoners weakens the order of society, the syndics of Kalveza resent the compact, and she carries the fear that her father was right and that keeping one's head down is the only safe strategy.

## What the Kingdom Keeps

A Provenzian can point to a handful of things that belong to a city or to all of them. **The Charter of Rights** won in the War of the Charter limits the monarch, and a **city charter** (_daktela_), a grant written and sealed, gives each city its chartered rights and its self-governance on local matters. **The notary's seal** (_tizaro_) makes a contract an act. **The marble** that the empire left, the aqueducts and amphitheaters and columns, is a civic inheritance every city keeps in repair or lets be called resting. **The songs** of the _sílador_ tradition are the culture's canon, passed on by singers and recited by patrons' poets, and a song is what memory keeps. **The libraries and archives** of the academies hold the learning that makes the cities famous. The **reva**, the channels that carry river water along the valley terraces, are the working infrastructure of the countryside.

The nearest thing to a shared register is a list nobody keeps. The blood-fields of the kingdom have no register, and Provenzians name the ground and not the battle, so that what survives in common speech is where it happened and what the place is like now.

## Temples That Are Also Galleries

The [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian pantheon]] is the faith of the kingdom, and its temples tend toward the grand and the beautiful, doubling as galleries, concert halls and centers of community life. Marendas of knowledge and Ólvenia of prosperity and pleasure are the best-loved gods here, which is a fair summary of Provenzian priorities. The Sacred Forge is honored by the region's very considerable population of skilled craftsmen, who are better paid and better regarded than their equivalents anywhere west of them.

An ordinary Provenzian goes to the temple as he goes to a gallery: to be seen in a good place, to hear the music, and to give what a man of his standing gives. He keeps the hearth fire of his house, the _karalze_, and the household gods, the _karalzis_, which are "those of the hearth", and he passes a wayside shrine, a _nechaime_, with a coin. Before an important piece of business a merchant may spend a night at a shrine of Ólvenia, praying for charm, eloquence and a favorable outcome. The festivals are where the faith is most itself: solemn ritual combined with carnival. A priest of Sélmoros attends the dying and is paid for the funeral and the tomb.

The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Thordimalon]] works openly here under royal oversight, and Provenzian society treats magic as another art and another scholarship. Mages answer to the crown as well as to the Arcane Order.

## The Year Kept in Public

The festivals that matter are the ones that put the culture's values on show. Marendas's week-long Festival of Illumination at the spring equinox fills the temples with public lectures on philosophy, theology and natural science, awards scrolls and manuscripts to the winners of philosophical contests, and ends with students and teachers swearing to pursue and pass on knowledge. The Dedication of the Apprentice at the beginning of autumn presents the young entering their trades to Marendas's priests. Ólvenia's Festival of the Golden Harvest in late summer, three days of music, wine and open feasting when the merchants close their shops, is the most openly hedonistic celebration in the pantheon's calendar. Patrons commission singers and actors for the temple squares, and the priestess blesses new stalls and consecrates new trading companies. The Sacred Forge's Festival of the Hearth at the winter solstice carries flame from the temple's forge to every household's fire, and the Lanterns of the Night at the spring equinox turn a city's streets into a river of light.

The Silent Passage of Sélmoros in late autumn puts black candles in windows and the names of the year's dead on every tongue. The villages beside a recurrent blood-field keep a night that is the opposite of a festival: on the anniversary nobody goes out. Each of these is for something. Illumination and Dedication are for learning, the Golden Harvest is for the market, the Hearth is for the house, the Silent Passage is for the dead, and the night nobody goes out is for the ground.

## Favor, Fine and Exile

The ladder of reward runs from the private to the public. A client's work earns his patron's favor, and the favor leaves a debt (_orfeate_ is to stand under one) that is real though no court enforces it. A long record of favors earns a place in a patron's household, a recommendation and a post; a deed done for a city earns an earned name, a charge for a year (_usal_), a share in the city (_verosal_), a knight's place in its mounted order (_gulmale_). The release (_liskaima_) that frees a client from his patron is the settlement of a long account in both directions.

The ladder of penalty runs the other way. The lightest is the quiet exclusion that the visitor who plays the code badly receives: indulged for an evening and then not invited. A lost patron is the next, and for a client the loss of his patron's door is the loss of his place. A court (_nasdal_) gives a judgment (_nasdis_) and a penalty (_kutreza_), and the learned law offers an appeal (_felavis_). The gravest sentences are banishment from the city (_ventodis_) and the unnaming, which takes the name from the roll. On the eastern frontier the Winter Assize sits at Durvalis from the closing of the passes to the thaw and hears what the campaign season had no time for, and it is called the most honest court in Provenzia because everyone in it is snowed in together until spring.

## Where the Crown Ends and the Temple Begins

Authority is shared among the crown, the Council of Peers and the cities. The monarch's power is limited by the Charter of Rights, and the Peers hold significant authority; the major cities keep chartered rights, among them self-governance on local matters. The result is a political culture of negotiation and consensus, more stable than a factional scheme and no less intricate. At Jalnasdal the Crown and the Peers have spent decades in a long maneuver over prerogative, taxation and appointment that neither has won, and the contest is conducted through offices, marriages and loans. Great houses place themselves in the gap and cautious ones keep their heads down in it.

The faith is not a rival. The temples are cultural institutions, the Arcane Order works under the crown, and the priests of Sélmoros hold the competent authority over the one thing nobody else will touch, the dead and the ground that holds them. Who tries a case is the city's court; who keeps the record is the notary and the archive; who collects is the city's treasurer and the crown's. When the answers conflict the settlement is a negotiation, and the weight goes to whoever has the most to lose from a refusal.

## What Becomes of the Dead

The Aureldian faith teaches that the soul, _dirave_, passes first through the spirit realm and that the rites of the god receive it there. The Silent Judge Sélmoros keeps the gate, and his priests attend the dying: the dying person speaks a final testimony, confesses wrongs and affirms goods, and the priests keep the record. The dead of a Provenzian house are received by the rites their own people keep. A death said as seldom as it can be, _cholvazis_, is a death spoken of in a lyric as _selvore_. The dead lie outside the walls, in the _demrolara_, the tombs along a road out of a city.

The belief explains the ground. A battlefield the dead were taken from in order leaves a battlefield, and a battlefield they were left on, unburied, unnamed or unjudged, leaves a blood-field, a place where the boundary with the spirit world tore and did not close. A skirmish does not make one, and a raid on a farmstead leaves a sad place and no more. So an ordinary Provenzian family fetches its dead from the frontier before it does anything else, carries them home to the rites and pays the priest, because the only fields the kingdom fears are the ones where nobody did.

That belief changes an ordinary day. A Provenzian does not walk a named field after dark, and a farmer who works a quickened one does not raise it at table. A family writes a will (_lemvisa_) and, when a line runs short, takes an heir by adoption (_aipsea_). A rich man funds a school or a bench because it is the most reliable way of being remembered well, and a merchant house pays the priest of Sélmoros for the funeral and the tomb.

## Reaching the Dead

The living honor the dead with the funeral, the tomb and the names spoken at the Silent Passage. The clergy of Sélmoros hold the competent authority over a blood-field, and their rite is a settlement and not a cleansing: an acknowledgement that the dead were not judged in order and a formal placing of them in the god's keeping. It is performed where a field is troublesome, it is expensive, and it works perhaps half the time. The priests are candid about the proportion, which does not endear them to the districts that pay.

The dead reach the living through the field. A recurrence repeats the battle on its night, with lights along a treeline and ranks at a distance, and a quickening makes the ground grow too well. Nothing in the faith lets the living question the dead, and Sélmoros opposes anyone who raises them. The Black Flame wants a field, since a field is a permanent reservoir of violent death that can be worked on until it consumes a district; that is what has happened at Yarvalen. Nobody keeps a field by office. The institutions that charge are the priests, who charge for a rite that may not work, and the districts that decline it.

## What a Person Owes

A Provenzian owes in order: his house first—by which he means the reputation of his name, not the building—then his patron, then his city, then the clients who depend on him. That last is stated as a real debt and not a courtesy, because a patron who abandons clients has destroyed the only thing that made him a patron.

Below those he owes the gods their festivals and the academies their endowment, and he will say with a straight face that funding a school is the most reliable way a wealthy man has of being remembered well. He is not being cynical. In Provenzia those are the same sentence.

In return the patron owes his clients money, protection, introductions and the use of his name. The client owes attendance, loyalty and the visible fact of being one more person in a great man's train. The city owes a citizen the chartered rights that its charter writes down, and the crown owes the city the Charter of Rights. A host owes a guest the welcome (_ellevirsa_) and a guest owes a host the courtesy of being placed. The accounts are meant to be recited.

## The Weak

Care in Provenzia is a duty of patronage before it is a kindness. The unattached are the weak, since a man without a patron is unprotected, and the system's whole answer to destitution is that nobody should be without one. A patron carries a client's burial and keeps his widow in the train. A household takes in its orphan, and the line that runs short adopts one. The city gives grain to its poor, the dole called _uzmela_, and an office exists whose holder, the _luse_, speaks for the commons in council. The temples of Lizelas and Morvana keep houses of healing and hospices in the larger cities, where the poor are tended, and the priests of Sélmoros bury paupers and the unclaimed without fee.

The limit is the edge of the train. A free man of another city living in this one, a _palvete_, has the city's courts and no patron to open them, and the Élavendren of Bataren, who are subjects and have been so for generations, have quarrels over land, rights and tradition that the crown does not recognize and that grind without being decided.

## Those Who Cannot Work

An injured client is a patron's matter. A patron who drops a client has damaged the thing that made him a patron, so the table keeps the one who cannot attend, and the physician (_ritezande_) is paid from it. The aged find a place as a companion who rides beside a patron, an _olvete_, or as a tutor, and those with a house teach its young. In a lean year the city's chest feeds the poor and the patron's table feeds his train, and the patron who cannot do so falls with his clients. The system's risk is that the hand it relies on is a purse, and a patron whose purse fails has nothing to give.

On the frontier the arrangements are different and more brutal. The march musters its levy at Durvalis every spring and disperses it in autumn, and the border families bear their own wounded and bury their own dead. Pirnebris is built so that it can be shut, and its people bring the flocks and the outlying families inside in an afternoon.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the patron's and the house's. A great man's household shelters his train, and a family of standing keeps a townhouse in the city (the _tevaime_) and a villa in the hills (the _komrela_). Teaching is the academy's: a family that can afford it puts a son through one, the academies of the great cities draw students from across Ankaris, and the Dedication of the Apprentice sends the young into the crafts under a priest's blessing. A craftsman is taught by a master at the bench (_gardema_) and holds his place by his work.

Defense is the city's and the march's. A city has its wall, its watch (_zorala_) and its guard, and the crown calls a levy. The frontier with Tarvénia is a season of raid and counter-raid that neither crown calls a war, held by the border families and by the garrison of Durvalis. The passes close in winter, and the war has a calendar.

## The House and the Name

The unit is the house, and the word means the reputation of the name more than the building. A Provenzian carries a given name, a house name (_arleis_), and sometimes a name a city gave him for a deed. Married women commonly keep their own names, a practice unique among the Mídhalión cultures, and the people of the tongue grant a friend or a lover a private love name that is never used in public. A marriage (_kambeza_) is a contract between two houses, and an adoption (_aipsea_) is an honorable way to get an heir. Membership is the name, the patron and the roll of the city. The founder (_derte_) is the one the house is named for, and a great house (_telvaral_) is a line of standing in its city.

The ladder of a life follows the ladder of the train. A child is the house's. A son of means goes to an academy. A young man or woman without a house of standing attaches to a patron and makes the morning call; a sound client rises to a companion; a successful one becomes a patron to others. A woman manages an estate, a school or a seat on a charter council as her house allows. The old are patrons, tutors and companions, and the dying are Sélmoros's. At every rung the question is the same: whom do you stand behind, and who stands behind you?

## What Is Not Said at Table

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Provenzians' is the courtesy that closes a subject. The quickened field at Kivarel is the case. It grows the best fruit in the barony, it is farmed thoroughly by tenants who inherited the holdings and did not choose them, and a good share of the finest fruit conserves in Ankaris come from a battlefield and are sold under no particular name. The priests of Sélmoros have twice offered the rite of settlement and the tenants have twice refused it, courteously, because nobody knows whether settling a quickened field would end the quickening and the district cannot afford to find out. The yields can be discussed with any factor. The field cannot.

The sin sits beside it. The Talote lives on a war it does not call a war. Pirnebris sells the frontier its boots and carts and is richer than a cherry crop explains, and nobody there imagines a lasting peace would be good news. The only blood-field still being added to is Malnebris, where no rite of settlement has ever been attempted because neither crown will guarantee the other's priests safe conduct. [[being-vornantlgn|Vornan Tolgen]], a carter's son who taught himself philosophy from an unclaimed book of dialogues, lectures in the taverns of Pirnebris and asks, plainly and repeatedly, what a town that needs the war to go on makes of itself.

The same habit runs through the frontier of the north. At Bataren, which faces the open ocean, the settled Élavendren and the crown quarrel over land and rights, and both crowns treat it as a domestic matter because their friendship depends on not looking closely. Thoughtful Provenzians dispute whether the courtesy is how a civilization keeps from looking at what it lives on or how it keeps a kingdom of independent cities from falling apart, and they argue it from opposite ends.
