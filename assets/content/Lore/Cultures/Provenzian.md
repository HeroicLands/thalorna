Provènzia is rich, clever, and entirely convinced that being both is a moral achievement. Its cities are marble and aqueduct and amphitheater, half of it inherited from the Vylarian Empire and half built since out of trade money, and a Provenzian will say the second half is the better part. What holds a Provenzian life together is not a realm, since there is no single realm, but three things: the city he belongs to, the patron above him and the clients below, and the exhausting and genuinely serious business of being seen to have taste.

## The City Is the Country

Each city is its own polity with its own character: some are ruled by hereditary nobility, some by elected councils of wealthy citizens, and a few by guilds of merchants or artisans who dispensed with the pretense some generations ago.

A Provenzian's first loyalty is to his city and it is not close. He names it before his family, defends it against a neighboring city with real heat, and regards the man forty miles up the coast as a foreigner with a funny accent. Regional feeling exists chiefly when a Vylarian or a Nordman appears, at which point it materializes at once and dissolves the moment the threat leaves.

The consequence is obvious and the Provenzians live with it cheerfully: nothing here is ever decided once. Every treaty is provisional, every alliance is a season's arrangement, and a merchant house that cannot read the standing of four cities at a time will not last a generation.

## Patronage

The relation that actually organizes daily life is patron and client, and it runs from the top of a city to the bottom without a break. A patron provides money, protection, introductions and the use of his name; a client provides attendance, loyalty, votes where there are votes, and the visible fact of being one more person in a great man's train.

Neither side finds anything demeaning in this. A man with no patron is not free—he is unattached, which in Provènzia is a description of vulnerability.

Artists live inside the arrangement and not beside it. A painter, sculptor, playwright or natural philosopher works for a patron, and the patron's standing rises with the work's reputation, and Provènzia therefore produces a great deal of art and very little art critical of anyone who could afford to commission it.

## Courtly Love

The tradition of courtly love is an elaborate social code governing romance, patronage and artistic expression, and its influence has spread across the whole Vylarian Sea region. Foreigners consistently make two mistakes about it.

The first is to think it is about love. It is about conduct—the precise management of admiration, obligation, discretion and public regard—and a great deal of it is transacted between people with no romantic interest in one another whatever.

The second is to think it is frivolous. The code is how patronage gets negotiated without either party having to say the ugly part aloud, and a Provenzian who plays it badly loses real money and real access. A visitor who treats it as a parlor game will be indulged, and then quietly excluded.

## Learning as Standing

Education is valued and the valuing is sincere. The academies at the great cities draw students from across Ankaris for natural philosophy, mathematics, rhetoric, law and the magical arts, and a Provenzian family that can afford it will put a son through one whatever he intends to do afterward.

The result is a society where a merchant quotes philosophy at dinner and means it, and where a man's ability to speak well is a legible asset. Rhetoric is the skill the culture actually rewards most: a Provenzian will admire a well-made argument he disagrees with, and remember it longer than the conclusion.

Ménérva of knowledge and Vénusia of prosperity and pleasure are the best-loved gods here, which is a fair summary of Provenzian priorities. The Sacred Forge is honored by the region's very considerable population of skilled craftsmen, who are better paid and better regarded than their equivalents anywhere west of them.

## Manners

Provenzian manners are ornate, indirect and fast. Compliment is a medium of exchange, a direct refusal is a discourtesy, and a plainly-spoken foreigner is charming for about one evening and tiresome afterward.

None of this indicates insincerity, and Provenzians are irritated by the accusation. The ornament is the vocabulary: a Provenzian can say something exact and cutting inside it that another Provenzian hears perfectly, and outsiders who cannot hear it assume nothing is being said.

Appearance matters and is not thought shallow. Dress, table, house and the company a family keeps are the public record of its standing, and a family that lets that record slip is understood to be in trouble before anyone has seen the books.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Provenzian what a person owes, and he answers in order: his house first—by which he means the reputation of his name, not the building—then his patron, then his city, then the clients who depend on him. That last is stated as a real debt and not a courtesy, because a patron who abandons clients has destroyed the only thing that made him a patron.

Below those he owes the gods their festivals and the academies their endowment, and he will say with a straight face that funding a school is the most reliable way a wealthy man has of being remembered well. He is not being cynical. In Provènzia those are the same sentence.
