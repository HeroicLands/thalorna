**The Head of the Roll** is the opening of the Archivist's rolls, the genealogies on which every House of the [[affiliation-mtrrchybth|Matriarchy of Bethua]] rests its standing, and it is read aloud in the [Council of Houses]{.name data-type="affiliation/governmental"} at [[place-bethura|Bethura]] each time a Dómissa takes her House's seal. The new Dómissa stands in front of the Archivist's table while it is read. When the head is done, the Archivist reads her own House's line down from it, daughter after daughter, until she reaches the woman standing there and says her name, and the Dómissa answers with the name of her House and her own. The head is the same for every House. A Lesser House and the oldest Great House hear the same words, and only the reading-down differs.

It is the Houses' account of the first years of the colony that [[affiliation-pelagora|Pelagora]] planted on this shore ([[lore-daughtercts|the Daughter-Cities]]), and of the year the realm calls [the Dry Year]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, when [Ánevimia]{.name data-type="being/npc"} set the first seal on the first cistern and kept the first roll of those who drank from it. It is the reason the realm gives for why a seal is a woman's and why a man is written in the roll under the name of the woman who answers for him. The Archivist reads it in the feminine register, in the long sentences of the rolls, and the rendering keeps their length.

Pelagora's account of the founding is a council's: it sent its younger sons over the sea when the valleys were full, appointed the officers who kept the colony's harbor and collected its dues, and has no record of a Dry Year. The head of the roll does not dispute that Pelagora kept the harbor. The harbor-quarter of Bethura has its own telling, in which the men who sailed on the night of the cistern went to fetch water from the mother-city and were refused it there, and the Archivist does not read that one. [House Khalath-Mara]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} holds that the ships first beached at [[place-ashira|Oshira]], and the temple of [[affiliation-lusinia|Lusinia]] at Bethura holds that the cistern under its oldest court is the one Ánevimia sealed. The court is kept shut, and the cistern under it holds rainwater still.

## The Opening

Hear the head of the roll, you Houses, before the seal goes into a new hand; hear it as your mothers heard it standing where this woman stands; and hear, before her own name is read, the names that every name in this hall is read down from, because a House that does not know where its roll begins does not know what its seal is worth.

The roll does not begin with a ship, though there were ships, nor with a war, though there have been wars, nor with a goddess, though every word of it is said under the goddesses and in their hearing. It begins with a cup of water, counted, and with a name written beside it, and with a woman sitting on a stone lid in the dark. Everything the Houses hold they hold because that cup was counted. Hear how it was counted.

## The Ships

The ships came from Pelagora over the sea, in the season when the sea is quiet, and they came because the mother-city's valleys were full and its younger sons had no land to stand on. The council of Pelagora says that it sent its sons, and the roll does not quarrel with the council: it sent its sons. But a son with no land does not go over the sea alone to a shore no one has farmed, and the sons brought their wives, and their sisters who had no dowry, and their mothers who would not be left, and the daughters of those mothers, and the seed. The council counted the sons, because the sons were what it could tax. The roll counts the women, because the women were what it could not have lived without.

They came to a shore with a good bay and no river. The roll gives the bay as this one, and the court of the temple as the place where the first fire was lit, and every House that holds otherwise has said so in this hall and been heard. There was green along the shore where the sea-wind keeps it, and olives growing wild on the slope, and a spring at the foot of the hill that ran sweet in the wet season and ran slow in the dry. Behind the slope the country turned the color of straw and stayed that color to the edge of sight.

The council in Pelagora sent an officer with the ships to keep the harbor, as it sent one to every colony it planted, and he kept it. He built the mole of the harbor with the men, stone on stone out into the bay, so that the council's ships could lie safe when they came for the dues, and he was good at it, and the mole stands under the quay of this city to the third course. The roll gives him that. It does not give his name, and you will hear why.

## The Cistern

While the men built the mole the women dug. They dug because they had stood at the spring in the first dry season and watched it run slow, and had counted the jars it filled in a morning, and had counted the mouths, and the counts did not agree. A woman who has kept a house knows that sum before a man knows there is a sum to be done, and the women of the ships had kept houses.

The woman who put the spade in first was Ánevimia, a widow of Pelagora who had come with her five daughters and no son, and the roll does not say who her husband was, because she did not. She chose the place in the rock above the spring where the rain off the hill runs together in the wet season, and she and her daughters cut it, and the other women came and cut beside them when their own work was done, and in two seasons they had cut a cistern into the hill as deep as three women standing on one another's shoulders and as wide as a threshing floor. They lined it with lime and pitch. They cut a channel to it from the slope so the rain would run in. They made a lid of a single stone, which took forty of them to set in its place, and the men came up from the mole for one morning to help them lift it, and went back down.

In the next wet season the rain ran down the slope and into the channel and into the cistern, and filled it, and the women stood around the lid and listened to it filling, and Ánevimia said that the water was the goddesses' and the work was the women's and the cup would be the House's. That is the first time the roll gives the word House as the realm gives it now. She did not mean a building.

## The Dry Year

Then came the year the roll calls the Dry Year, and the colony had been on the shore three years.

The wet season did not come. The clouds stood out over the sea through the whole of the season they should have come in, and looked at the land, and went west, and the storm-priests on the hill kept their vigil night after night and saw no lightning. The spring at the foot of the hill ran slow, and then it ran in drops, and then a woman could put her hand over it and hold it shut. The barley came up in the first rain of the year before and stood in the field a hand high and went brown. The council's ship that came for the dues that autumn took the dues, which were oil, and sailed home with them, and the officer of the harbor sent with it a letter asking for water, and the ship did not come back that year.

The cistern was full. It was full from the wet season before, and it was all the water there was.

Ánevimia went up to it on the morning the spring stopped, and she took with her a lump of clay from the potter's pit and her own ring, which had a vine cut in it, and she pressed the clay over the join of the lid and the rock and pressed her ring into the clay, and it dried in the sun. That was the first seal. The seal of every House in this hall is that seal, and when a House sets its seal on a letter of credit or a contract or a warrant, it is saying what Ánevimia said with the clay: this is closed, and it opens when I open it, and not before, and not for anyone who comes to it in the night.

## The First Roll

Then she did what the roll is named for. She sat down by the lid with a board of whitened wood and a stick of charcoal, and she sent her daughters through the colony, house by house and hut by hut and down to the mole, and they brought her every person on the shore by name. She wrote each name on the board, and beside each name she drew a mark for a cup, and each morning every name on the board came up the hill and was given one cup from the cistern, and she drew the cup through. No one drank who was not on the board. No one on the board drank twice.

She wrote the women first, each under her hearth, a mother and then her daughters in the order they were born, because the women had dug the cistern and the women knew who belonged to whose fire. Then she came to the men, and she asked each man: who answers for you? And a man with a mother on the shore said his mother's name, and was written under it; and a man with a wife said his wife's, and was written under hers; and a man who had come over the sea alone, a younger son with no one, stood there and had no name to give. Then Ánevimia asked the women standing round the lid: who answers for him? And a woman would say: I do. An old woman with no sons said it for two young men who had none of their own, and fed them from her own hearth all that year, and the roll gives her name, [Lomaris]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, with theirs written under it. So every man on the shore was written under a woman, so that he could drink, and that is why a man is written in the roll under the woman who answers for him to this day. It was done so that he would not die of thirst. Hear that, you Houses, when you hear it said in foreign ports that the roll was made to hold men down. It was made to hold them up the hill to the cup.

Some of the men were not written. The officer of the harbor would not stand at the lid and be asked who answered for him, because he was the council's, and he said so. The men who had come over with him in the council's pay would not stand there either, and some others stood with them who had wives and would not say their names. They took their cups from the harbor-jars of the council's ship, which had been left half-full, and from what they could find, and they did not come up the hill. The roll does not say they were refused. It says they did not come.

## The Night at the Cistern

Forty days the cup went round, and on the fortieth the harbor-jars were dry.

On the forty-first night the officer of the harbor came up the hill in the dark with the men who were not on the board, and they carried oars, the long oars of the council's ship, because there was nothing else on that shore heavier than a spade, and they meant to break the seal and fill the jars and carry them down to the boat that was left at the mole and row it out of the bay toward Pelagora, where there was water, and leave the shore to the dry.

Ánevimia was sitting on the lid. She slept there every night of the Dry Year, on the stone over the water, with the board beside her and a lamp, and her daughters slept on the ground around the stone. When she heard them on the path she sat up and turned up the wick, and she did not stand.

The roll gives what she did next exactly, and the Archivist reads it slowly. She took up the board and read it aloud. She read every name on it from the top, the women under their hearths and the men under their women, and as she read each man's name his woman answered from the dark, wherever she was sleeping on the hill: here. She did not read the names of the men on the path, because they were not on the board. She read past them. She read the whole board through to the bottom, by the lamp, in the hearing of the men with the oars, so that each of them heard every name on the shore that was not his.

Then she said: There is a cup for every name on this board, and there is room on this board. Who answers for you?

The roll says that a man put down his oar on the path and said his wife's name, and his wife came up out of the dark and stood beside him, and said: I do. And Ánevimia wrote him under her. And another man put his oar down and said his mother's name, and she was not on the hill, she was at the mole, and another woman said: I will, until she comes. And one by one the oars went down on the path, and the men came into the lamplight and were written. The roll does not give how many came. It gives that the board was full to its edge by morning, and that Ánevimia wrote the last of them on the back.

The officer of the harbor did not put his oar down. He said that he was the council's officer and that the council had not given her the water, and she said that the council had not given anyone the water, and that the council's ship had taken the oil and not come back. He said that he would go home and tell the council what she was. She said that he should, and that he would not need an oar for it, because the boat at the mole had its own. He went down the hill in the dark with the men who had not put their oars down, and before light they rowed the council's boat out of the bay with what water they had found in the town, and the roll says that the boat went over the edge of the sea, and the roll does not say whether it came to Pelagora. The roll does not hold them, and so it does not hold their names. They are the first who are not in it.

The seal was not broken. The Houses keep that night as the night the seal held, and it held because she read the board aloud and did not stand up. No spear was raised on that hill and no woman struck a man. Hear that too, you Houses, and remember what it cost: a woman sat on a stone in the dark with forty days of thirst behind her and read out names to men holding oars, and she was one woman, and they were many, and she did not know what they would do until they did it. Every seal in this hall rests on that night, and on the knowledge that it might have gone the other way.

## The Dream

The roll does not say the goddesses did nothing. It says that on the night after, the forty-second, Ánevimia slept on the lid and dreamed, and that [[lore-zunelasdty|Zunelas]], the Veiled Dreamer, came to her with her face covered, and showed her the hill from the inside, as a weaver sees the back of a cloth: the rock in its layers, and among the layers a dark thread running down from the high country that was not rock, but water, moving in the stone where no one could see it.

In the morning she took her daughters and went up the hill to the place the dream had shown her, where a fig tree grew out of a crack in the rock in a year when nothing else on the hill was green. They dug at the roots of the fig, and dug for nine days, and on the ninth day the eldest daughter's spade came up wet. They had found the vein. They followed it in with their hands and their spades, and cut a channel from it to the cistern, and it was a thin water and a slow one, but it did not stop, and it ran into the cistern a cup at a time while the cups went out of it, and the count began to agree.

The rain came at the end of that year, at the season it should have come the year before, and the storm-priests on the hill saw their lightning and cried out, and the Thunderer was honored for it. The roll gives him his honor. It gives also that the cistern had held until he came, and that the vein under the fig had fed it, and that the cup had gone round every morning to every name on the board, and that in the whole of the Dry Year eleven people on that shore died, and the roll gives their names, every one, with the women who answered for them, and none of them died of thirst. They died of the fever that comes in a dry year and of their years, and they were tied off in the roll in their places.

The fig tree stands in the court of the temple of Lusinia that is kept shut. The temple says so.

## The Naming

Ánevimia kept the seal of the cistern until she was old, and the board became a roll, and the roll grew, because every child born on the shore was written in it under her mother, and every man who came over the sea and wished to drink was written under a woman who would answer for him, and the colony grew into the city. When the council's next ship came for the dues the officer of the harbor was not on it. The council sent another officer, and he kept the harbor, and the women kept the water, and the roll says that this was the arrangement for twelve centuries: the harbor was Pelagora's, and the water was ours.

When Ánevimia came to her end she did what every Dómissa in this hall will do, and what the woman standing here today has sworn to do. She named the one who would hold the seal after her, in public, in the court above the cistern, in front of the whole shore.

She did not name her eldest daughter, [Pédhoria]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, whose spade had come up wet on the ninth day. She named her third, [Lofortha]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who had kept the count of the cups on the board through all forty days and the year after without one cup wrong. The roll gives her reason in her words: the spade finds the water once, and the count keeps it every morning.

Pédhoria was passed over in front of the shore, and the roll gives what she did. She did not leave. She took the keeping of the vein under the fig, which was a lesser office then and is a great one now, and she kept it until she died, and she was the first of the sisters of the water, from whom the [Order of the Waters]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"} reads its line. She waited, and she served, and the roll honors her waiting as it honors her sister's seal. Hear that, you daughters who are passed over. The first of you found the water.

## The Five Lines

These are the daughters of Ánevimia, from whom every line in this hall is read down, and the Archivist reads them in the order they were born:

Pédhoria, the eldest, who found the vein and was passed over, from whom the sisters of the water read their line;

[Sothimia]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the second, who answered for more men in the Dry Year than any woman but Lomaris, and fed them, and whose line holds the orchards of the coast;

Lofortha, the third, who kept the count and took the seal, from whom the line of the first seal runs, and from whom the Metrissai of the realm are read down;

[Nikhtana]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the fourth, who carried the lamp on the night at the cistern and held it steady, whose line keeps the rolls themselves, and from whom every Archivist is read;

[Ibetha]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the fifth, the youngest, who was a child in the Dry Year and was given the first cup every morning because she was the smallest, and who lived the longest of them, and whose line went inland with the water, and holds the grain.

Every House in this hall reads itself down from one of these five, and the Archivist knows which, and has the rolls to show it. A House that cannot read itself down from one of the five is not a House of this hall, and does not stand where this woman stands.

## The Reading Down

So the head of the roll is read, and now the line of this woman's House is read down from it, from the daughter of Ánevimia from whom it comes, mother to daughter, to the woman who stands here.

_(Here the Archivist reads the line of the House, each Dómissa's name in order, and the name of each daughter who was passed over beside the name of the sister who was named, and comes to the name of the woman standing at the table.)_

The seal is hers. The roll holds her. Let her hold the House as the lid was held, and open it when she opens it, and not before, and not for anyone who comes in the night; and let her read the names aloud when the night comes; and let her name her daughter in public, in front of the Houses, when she comes to her end.

_(The Dómissa answers with the name of her House, and then her own, and says: I hold the seal. The Archivist sets the seal in her hand. The Council answers her.)_

Harmony.
