**The Testimony of Rísakos** is the final testimony of [Rísakos]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a digger of the tunnel-wells (_katelun_) who worked for the [Order of the Waters]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"} for most of his life, spoken on his deathbed to a priest of the [[affiliation-selmoros|Faith of Sélmoros]] and written down by the priest's scribe in his words. The Threshold Keepers keep the original in the temple's records of the dead, and it is entered there, as every man's testimony is, under his own name. It is one of the few places in a Bethuan man's life where he speaks for himself, and this one is spoken in the plain speech of the works, with the Khelathi words of the trade in it.

The Order of the Waters asked the temple for a copy, and reads it aloud at the head of every new mother-well, the first and deepest shaft of a tunnel-well, on the morning the first spade goes in. The gang stands around the mark on the ground with its tools, the engineer-priestess who has sighted the line stands at the mark, and the eldest sister of the Order present reads the whole of it, including the name, which the Order says aloud for no other man. Then the spade goes in. The Order keeps the reading because the testimony says what the work costs, from the bottom of the shaft, more plainly than anything the Order has written itself.

The tunnel-well came to [[affiliation-mtrrchybth|Bethua]] with the engineers [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Au'Khelathu]] sent after [[lore-bethuasep|the Separation]], and Rísakos was a boy when they came. The line the testimony is proudest of is the one that brought water to [[place-sehela|Sehela]] in the dry southern interior, the village held by [House Khalithon]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"}. The collapse in its third shaft is entered in the Order's own book of the line, with the two names. The priest's formulas at the opening and close are the ones every priest of the Silent Judge uses, in the feminine register. At the end stands a note the Order added to its copy, in the hand of the engineer-priestess who sighted the Sehela line.

## The Opening

_The priest:_ You are at the gate. The Silent Judge is at the gate. Speak your life in your own voice, and say who you are, and what you did that was wrong, and what you did that was good, and the gate will hear it, and you will go to it with nothing in your hands.

_Rísakos:_ I'm Rísakos. I was entered under my mother, Timethia of House Hadrikes, and then under my wife, Ghamira of House Jalimes, and I'm under her now. I dug wells. That's what I am. I dug the _katelun_ for the sisters of the Waters for fifty years, near enough, and before that I carried baskets for the men who dug them. I'm going to say it the way I'd say it at the top of a shaft, because that's the only way I know how to say anything. You'll stop me if it's wrong.

_The priest:_ Nothing you say in your own voice is wrong here. Speak.

## The Men from the Delta

I was eight or nine when the men from the delta came. After the war. They came up the coast road from the east with mules and lamps and coils of line and a great deal of rope, and they walked out into the dry country past my mother's field at the edge of the water, where the wheat stops and the grass starts, and stood there looking at the ground. They were the engineers the delta queen sent. The sisters of the Waters walked with them, and the sisters asked them questions, and listened to the answers. I had never seen a sister of the Waters listen to anybody.

One of them was the master. A man. I want to say this first because it's in me and it's been in me all my life. He was a man, and he had his own tools, and they were his. He had a mark on the handles of his picks that was his mark. When he spoke, the sisters wrote it down. When he walked into a village the Hiora of the village came out and bowed to him, a little, the way you'd bow to a Dómissa. I stood in my mother's field and watched a priestess bow to a man. I didn't know a thing could be like that. I'm not saying it's wrong that it isn't like that here. I'm saying I saw it, and I was eight, and I didn't forget it.

He taught them the _katelun_. You know what it is, you drink from one. You go up into the high ground where the water is, under the ground, where it runs in the gravel above the rock, and you dig a mother-well down to it, down and down, until the bottom of the shaft is wet. Then you come away from it downhill, toward the fields, and every so many paces you sink another shaft, not so deep, and at the bottom of each one you dig sideways to meet the last, and the tunnel runs from the mother-well down to the fields at a slope so gentle you can't see it with your eye. You have to see it with the lamps. And the water comes down the tunnel by itself, all year, and out at the end into the open, onto the field, and the field is green where it never was. That's all a _katelun_ is. Holes and a tunnel and patience.

He taught the sisters to sight it, and he taught the men to dig it. I carried baskets for his gang. When he went back to the delta, the sisters had the sighting and the men had the digging, and I went on carrying, and then I dug.

## The Lamps

You keep the line with lamps. The sister sights it on the surface with her poles and her lines, and marks the shafts, and down in the tunnel you set lamps in a row behind you, and you look back along them, and if they're in a line you're true, and if one jumps out of the line you've gone crooked, and you go back and cut it again. And you watch the flames. If the flame goes small and blue, the air's bad, and you get out, and you don't argue about it. I learned that from the master's gang. A man who argues with a blue flame is a man the gang carries out.

I kept the lamp line for forty years. That's a good thing. I'll say it with the good things. I'm saying it now because it's where the wrongs start.

## What I Did That Was Wrong

_The priest:_ Say what was wrong.

_Rísakos:_ Four things. I've counted them. I've had a long time to count them.

**The water.** The first. I was a young man, a digger two years, and it was a dry summer, the summer the barley failed on the Hadrikes land. Our line ran past my mother's field, past the gate where the sisters let the water out to each field in its turn, so much for each House, written down. My mother's field had had its water and it was not enough. I went out in the night and I opened the gate with the iron bar we used in the tunnels, and I let the water into her field for an hour, out of its turn, out of the next House's turn, and I shut it again before light and nobody saw. I never told my mother. She didn't ask. The barley lived. Someone else's had an hour less. I don't know whose. That's the first.

_The priest:_ It is heard. Go on.

_Rísakos:_ **The blow.** The second. I hit a man. Not a woman. I've never raised a hand to a woman, and my wife is quicker than me with her hands and always was. A man. In the tunnel of the line at the Ghanes well, in the dark, a digger called [Gáberos]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who said a thing about my mother, and I hit him in the mouth and broke his tooth. A man hitting a man is nothing to the law. It was something to me. I meant to say I was sorry and I didn't, because he'd said the thing about my mother. And the next year Gáberos was killed in a fall on another line, not mine, and I never said it. So I'm saying it here. Gáberos, I'm sorry about the tooth. That's the second.

_The priest:_ It is heard.

_Rísakos:_ **The crack.** The third. This is the one.

The line to Sehela. You'll have heard of it. It's the longest line I dug, the longest the sisters had sighted then, and it went from the mother-well in the hills a long way down to the village, and the village was dying. The old wells were going dry, and the House that holds it, Khalithon, had told the Order that if the water didn't come by the next harvest the village would go. So we were behind, and we knew we were behind, and every man on the gang knew that the harvest was coming down the road at us like a cart.

I was the head of the gang by then. The sister who sighted the line was [Thériria]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a Hiora of the Waters, and she was the best I ever worked under. She saw the ground like she could see through it.

In the third shaft from the mother-well, one morning, I went down first, as the head of a gang goes down first, and at the bottom I put my lamp up to the wall where we'd cut the day before, and there was a crack in it. Not a big one. A thread. Running up from the floor into the gravel above. And a little dust came out of it while I watched, and stopped.

You know what you do. You come up, and you tell the sister, and she comes down and looks, and you shore it, or you stop and cut a new turn around it, and you lose a week. A week. With the harvest coming down the road.

I didn't come up. I looked at it for as long as it takes to say this, and I thought: it's a thread. I've seen a hundred threads. Most of them are nothing. And I set the gang to cut on down the tunnel away from it, and I didn't say anything to Thériria when she came round at noon, and I didn't say anything to the gang. I put the men in the tunnel past it all day.

It came down the next morning. Not on me. I was at the top of the shaft. It came down on [Tharkós]{.name data-type="being/npc"} and [Nabiros]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who were cutting in the tunnel below the crack, with the gravel above it. We dug for them with our hands. It took us till dark. Tharkós was entered under his mother, Rakhimea of House Damines, and Nabiros under his wife, Timelra of House Lamides. Nabiros had a daughter born that winter. They're in the Order's book of the line, both of them, I made sure of that, I said their names to the sister who writes the book. I said their Houses.

And Thériria came to me that night, at the top of the shaft, with the bodies laid out on the ground under the cloth, and she asked me. She said: Rísakos, did you see a crack?

And I said no.

That's the third. I said no to a Hiora of the Waters, in the night, with the two of them under the cloth. I've said no to her every day since in my head. She's old now. She's still alive, they tell me. I'm saying yes. I saw it. I saw it the morning before, and I said nothing, and I put them in the tunnel, and it came down. That's the third.

_The priest is silent a long while._

_The priest:_ It is heard.

_Rísakos:_ **The wish.** The fourth. I don't know if this one's a wrong. You'll tell me. I'm saying it so it's said, because it's the only thing I've never said to anybody, not my wife, not anyone.

All my life I wanted to be the man from the delta. Not to go to the delta. I didn't want to live there. I wanted to have my own picks with my own mark on the handles, and have them be mine, and walk into a village and have the Hiora come out and bow to me, a little. That's all. I never did anything about it. I never would have. I never thought I should have it. I just wanted it, every day, at the bottom of the shaft, when I looked at the handle of the pick and it had the Order's mark on it and not mine. Is that a wrong?

_The priest:_ The gate hears what is said in a man's own voice. It does not say to him what is wrong. That is not my office. It is said, and it is heard.

_Rísakos:_ Then it's said. That's the four.

## What I Did That Was Good

_The priest:_ Say what was good.

_Rísakos:_ I'll say them the same way. You have to say both, they told me, or the gate gets a crooked line.

**The lamps.** I kept the lamp line forty years and it was true. You can go down into any line I dug and look back along it and the lamps will sit in a row like beads on a string. And after the master's gang went home I never lost a man to bad air, not once, in forty years. Every man on every gang of mine knew the blue flame. I beat it into them. If a man argued with me about the flame I put him on the baskets for a month. Some of them are alive because I did that. I can't tell you which. That's the way of it with air.

**The boy.** There was a fall on the line at the cistern below the Hadrikes land, not a big one, and a boy of the gang was under it, [Lékhisos]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, a carrier, twelve years old. I was nearest. I dug him out with my hands, the gravel coming down on me while I dug, and I got his face clear first and then the rest, and he was alive. He's a head of a gang now himself, at the lines in the east. He sends my wife oil at the Golden Harvest. That's a good thing, and I'll say it. I'd say it louder if I had the breath.

**The gangs.** I taught a lot of men to dig. I taught them to listen to the sister and to watch the flame and to look back along the lamps. Most of them are still digging. You can't dig in Bethua without somebody I taught being on the gang.

**Sehela.** The line to Sehela. Yes. The same line.

We finished it before the harvest. We lost the week anyway, and more, digging Tharkós and Nabiros out and cutting the turn around the fall that I should have cut before it, and we finished it all the same, the last of it working by lamplight through the nights, and we broke through at the bottom into the open, below the village, at the edge of the dry ground, one morning in the end of summer.

And I was at the mouth when the water came. Thériria opened the gate at the mother-well, up in the hills, a long way off, and we waited at the mouth, the whole gang and the whole village, the women of Khalithon and their daughters and the men behind them, all standing in the dust in front of a hole in the ground with a stone lip on it. And we waited, and nothing. And we waited. And then you could hear it. You hear it before you see it. Like someone breathing in the tunnel. And then it came out over the lip, brown, and then clear, and ran down the channel we'd cut to the first field, and into it, and the ground drank it, you could see the ground drinking, it went dark like a cloth when you wet it.

The Dómissa of Khalithon was there. She went and stood in the first field in the water in her good sandals, and stood there, and didn't say anything. And then the children went in, and then everybody.

That night I walked the line. You know the line. Every child in Bethua knows it. Where the water stops. On the one side the green and on the other the straw. I'd walked it at the edge of my mother's field when I was eight, watching the men from the delta. And I walked it that night at Sehela with a lamp, and it was in a different place. It was out past the village, out past the last field, out where it had never been. I'd moved it. We had. Me and the gang and Thériria and the two of them under the cloth. I walked the whole of it with my lamp, from one end to the other, and it took me till midnight, and I've never been so tired or so glad.

I don't own a field at Sehela. I don't own a field anywhere. I never will. The fields are Khalithon's, and the water is the Order's, and the _katelun_ is the realm's, and I'm my wife's. That's how it is. I'm not saying it should be different. I'm saying I moved the line, and the line is still where I moved it, and the children at Sehela play in water that came down a tunnel I kept true. That's the good. That's the one I'd put on the scale against the crack. The Judge keeps his scale to himself. I know. But that's the one.

**My daughter.** And one more. My daughter. [Pólithana]{.name data-type="being/npc"}. She was put forward for the temple when she was small, because she could see the ground the way Thériria could, she could tell you where the water was from the color of the grass, and my wife put her forward, and she was a Parthena and then a sister of the Waters. She sights lines now. In the east, past the Hadrikes land. She's at the head of a mother-well this month and she couldn't come down, and I told my wife not to send for her. Tell her I said it was right she didn't come. The mother-well matters more. I dug wells for fifty years and I never sighted one, and my daughter sights them, and I've been down a line she sighted, the last line I went down, and the lamps sat in a row like beads on a string. That's a good thing. I didn't do it. I'll say it anyway.

## What I Ask

_The priest:_ Is there anything you ask?

_Rísakos:_ Three things, and I know none of them are mine to give.

I ask my wife's House to send oil to the Houses of Tharkós and Nabiros at every Silent Passage for as long as it stands. Damines and Lamides. I know it's hers to give and not mine. I'm asking her. She's here. She's nodding, priest, write that down, she's nodding.

I ask that somebody tell Thériria I said yes.

And I ask that this go in under my own name. I know it does. They told me. I'm asking anyway, so I've asked. Rísakos. Not under my mother and not under my wife. Just the once.

That's all. That's the whole of it. The lamps are in a row.

## The Close

_The priest:_ It is heard. Every word of it is heard, in your own voice, the wrong and the good, the four and the five, and nothing is left in your hands. Go to the gate lightly, Rísakos. The Silent Judge is there, and he does not hurry, and he hears.

_(The scribe's line:)_ Entered under his own name: Rísakos, digger of the Waters. He died in the night.

## The Note of Thériria

_(Added to the Order's copy, in the hand of the Hiora Thériria of the Waters, who sighted the line to Sehela.)_

I was told what he said, and I have read it, and I add this to the Order's copy, so that it is read with his at every mother-well.

He said yes. I say yes also. I came round the third shaft at noon that day and I put my lamp to the wall where he had been, and I saw the thread, and I said nothing, because the harvest was coming and the village was dying and the Dómissa of Khalithon had written to the Izet-Mu twice that month. I thought he had not seen it, because he had not come up. I thought: he has seen a hundred threads. I did not go down to look. That is mine. When I asked him that night whether he had seen a crack, I was asking so that he would say no, and he said it, and I let him carry it alone for thirty years, because a digger's silence is a small thing in the Order's book and a Hiora's is not.

Let the gang hear this before the spade goes in: a crack seen is said, by whoever sees it, to whoever is there, and the work stops, and the harvest waits, and the village waits, and the Order answers for the week. The line to Sehela was finished before the harvest, and it cost two men and a lie that two of us told, and the water would have come a week later without either.

The line is where he moved it. The Order holds that it is.
