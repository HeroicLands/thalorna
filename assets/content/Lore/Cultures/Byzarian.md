Byzarian culture is the culture of the bargain. The tongue has more words for kinds of agreement than most languages have for colors, the courts are staffed by professional arbitrators, and the highest civic honor in any of the five cities of the [[affiliation-byzarianlg|Byzarian League]] is to be named Warden of the Weights—the officer whose inspections make the city's word good. The Weighing at Khrýshteíkhos, about 455 AF, is where the first Warden proved a tax-farmer's brass standard false ([[lore-heladweighing|The Weighing at Khrýshteíkhos]]). A Byzarian understands that his cities have no great river and no divine mandate. What they have is a reputation for keeping terms, and he regards that reputation as infrastructure.

## The Guild Is the Skeleton

Every trade is chartered, and every charter carries privileges and obligations in that order and in writing. A master's seal from one of the great cities opens doors from [[affiliation-cnfdrtntrzlnstts|Tarûzel]] to [[affiliation-jurthatempr|Jürthāt]], and it does so because the guild behind it will ruin a member who discredits it faster than any magistrate could.

A Byzarian is therefore placed by his trade before his family and long before his city. He introduces himself by craft, names his master, and expects the same in return; a man who cannot name a guild is a man nobody knows how to deal with.

Apprenticeship is long and is understood as a kind of adoption. A master owes his apprentice training, maintenance and a character, and the apprentice owes obedience and, afterward, a lifetime's loyalty to the charter. Breaking that is the one social offense from which there is no recovery.

## Two Byzarías

The coast and the caravan cities are a deep mixture—Helonic, Tarûzeli, Khazryn and Vylarian families intermarried for generations, with Dunhari and Khazryni merchant quarters that keep their own tongues, faiths and law and are left to do it.

The plateau is another country: herding clans and farming villages, more homogeneous and considerably more conservative, speaking with an upland accent and regarding the coastal cities as foreigners who happen to buy their wool. The plateau feeds and clothes the coast and resents it; the coast pays the plateau and patronizes it. Neither can do without the other, and both say so only when drunk.

## The Terms Come First

The Byzarian answer to what is good is short: **good is the term kept exactly as agreed, and the defect declared before the sale; evil is the defect concealed, the term bent after it has turned against the bender, and the quarrel carried out of the paper.** The first two cheat a counterpart, and the third cheats the arbitrator, the guild and every house whose contracts depend on disputes ending where they are heard. A bad bargain honestly kept is a misfortune. A good bargain dishonestly kept is the offense.

Success is a name two seals will stand behind. The marks come in a recognizable order. A man is enrolled in the city's register, which makes him a freeman. He holds his guild's seal and takes apprentices of his own. His house is mentioned when a council seat falls open, and in the end he is trusted with an office that makes the city's word good. Failure is not poverty, since a failed venture honestly settled harms nobody's name. It is to be a person whom no counterpart can place: the man with no guild, no master and no house behind him, or the one whose contracts are void because the roll does not carry him.

A Byzarian values exactness, patience and a warmth that costs nothing and commits nothing beyond what is written. He values the plain statement of a defect, a name that closes the right way, and an arbitrator who is thought fair by both sides. He values least the general promise, the handshake on "the usual rate" and the improviser who will settle the details later, because that is how swindles begin. Courage in the ordinary sense is not prized for itself. The League raises conscripts for its frontier and hires companies when the frontier turns dangerous, and it books both as lines in a budget.

## The Man Admired

[[being-alykosmpsn|Alýkos Bridutis]], a physician of Selímara, is the Byzarian ideal at the scale of a working life. He was the younger son of a silk trader, who meant him for the family counting house, and he apprenticed himself to a healer against his father's strong objections. He served as apprentice and journeyman for years before opening a practice of his own, and he has trained young physicians since. His reputation rests on accurate diagnosis and on the plain telling of what he has found: a patient hears the defect in his own body from Alýkos before anyone else hears of it. He listens to the poverty, fear and family quarrel behind an illness, speaks gently while he examines, and has publicly exposed charlatans to the authorities at a cost to his own comfort.

The admiration has a price he knows. The Physicians' Guild holds his standing high and its traditionalists resent his methods, and rumor credits his new treatments with deaths. Each patient he cannot save stays with him, and he has become solitary. The culture admires him anyway, because the Byzarian test of a physician is the same as the test of a merchant: he states the defect in his own goods and stands behind the seal.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-helnmystks|Bazîa Lokios]], a priest of the Sacred Forge in Órashion, is admired for repairing what terms cannot. She was the daughter of a leather worker and a midwife. As a small child she kept a group of separated children together through a city fire, and a priest who saw it took her into the temple as a novice. A cart injury in her teens nearly crippled her, and she shows the scar to people ashamed of their own wounds. She is known for restoring intimacy after betrayal, reconciling estranged families and bringing people to peace with grief, and she has trained younger priestesses in rites she devised for wounds the old rites missed.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the man's. Alýkos earns his standing by stating the truth of a body, and Bazîa by mending the bond a broken term leaves behind. A culture that cannot enforce goodwill needs someone who can restore it. Her cost is visible in her too: she absorbs the pain of others, and a conservative cleric opposes her changes to the ritual as departures from proper practice.

## What the League Keeps

A Byzarian can point to a handful of things that make the system work. **The register** of each city lists who is a freeman, and being struck from it voids every contract the person holds. **The charter** of a trade states its privileges and obligations in that order. **The seal** is a brass die with the master's own mark and the year it was issued; the cartographers' impressions are red wax on a civil commission and black on a military one, and no general in the League marches on a chart without a guild seal. **The standards** against which the Warden of the Weights inspects measures, coin and quality are the nearest the cities come to a sacred object. **The Cartographic Archive** holds every map a guild master has sealed since the charter was granted, and is the guild's treasure and its weak point.

The League's **common tariff** is the closest it has to a founding document, and the **Merchant Hall** in Khrýshteíkhos, where council and exchange share one roof, is its standing temple of business. Each house keeps its own copies of every paper that binds it. **The paper-script** of the Danvar carries large sums across the League's borders and across the sea.

## Faith on the Road and at the Tomb

Faiths are not forbidden in [[place-byzariargn|Byzaría]]. The [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aurèldían pantheon]] is the civic faith in a distinctly eastern rite—mystical, contemplative, elaborate, served by monks, whose monasteries are the region's chroniclers, healers and manuscript houses. The [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|Āsháian pantheon]] has a substantial following along the caravan roads, and small temples of it stand openly in every League city.

Byzarian tolerance is practical, and the Byzarians say so without embarrassment: a faith forbidden costs more in lost trade than orthodoxy could recover. A foreigner who mistakes this for principle is half right. It began as arithmetic and has been held long enough to become a habit they are proud of.

An ordinary Byzarian's religion happens at the turns of his working life and leaves the long middle to the monks. He is blessed when he departs and gives thanks when he arrives; he renews his contracts before witnesses at the turn of the year; he sends his sick to the monastery or to a temple hospital; and he pays the keepers of his family's tomb. The rite itself is personal. It puts revelation and elaborate ceremony ahead of an institution's orders, so a layman looks to a monk for guidance and not for a ruling. The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Ordo Arcanis]] keeps a chapter-hall in each League city and is treated as a licensed trade.

## The Year Kept by Terms

The festivals that matter to a Byzarian are the ones that mark a term beginning, running or ending. The Feast of Thresholds at the turning of the year is Árdavon's, and the temples host the renewal of oaths and contracts: merchants renew their agreements, spouses their vows, and the priests' witnessing writes each one into the temple's records. The Wanderer's Blessing at the spring equinox, kept by the temples of Pôzêrâs, commissions caravans, ships and soldiers for the road, and the priest anoints the traveler and takes an oath of the journey. The Celebration of Arrivals at the autumn equinox gives thanks for the roads that were walked safely, and the stories brought back are gathered into guides and maps. Mârendâs's Dedication of the Apprentice at the beginning of autumn presents the young entering their trades to her priests for blessing, and it suits a country whose apprenticeship is an adoption.

The Silent Passage of Sélmoros in late autumn closes the temples to the living while the priests perform the rites of the year's dead, and families set black candles in windows and speak the names aloud. At Nýkhtion the festivals that honor the dead are elaborate and are the city's own business, funded from its perpetual endowments. Each of these is for something concrete: the Thresholds renew what the year runs on, the two travelers' feasts begin and end the caravan season, and the Silent Passage settles the dead.

## What Terms Earn and Break

Reward is a longer contract. An apprentice who passes his examination becomes a journeyman, and a journeyman who presents an original work to the sitting masters and wins their vote becomes a master. A master's seal opens doors and a master who sits on the guild's council settles examination standards. Where a house has no name, the League's assessors may grant one, fixing the ending by what the house is to be held to. Honor is the Warden's office, and trust is to be named by both sides as the arbitrator of their quarrel.

Penalty climbs the other way. The lightest is the arbitrator's ruling, which names the clause and the damages. A guild that finds a member has discredited its seal ruins him, and the ruin is felt in every market before a magistrate hears the case. A man who takes private revenge has his charter suspended and his contracts voided. Some trades carry a license, and practicing without it is fineable; in cartography, producing a map of a League fortification or military disposition without guild oversight is treason. At the bottom of the ladder is the roll: a man struck from it has no charter, no protection, no standing in the courts, and every contract he holds is void.

## Where the Council Ends and the Monastery Begins

Authority in Byzaría is three institutions, not two. The first is the city. Each of the five keeps its own council, coin standard, courts and guard, and the council sits by the weight of the houses behind its members. The Chancellor keeps the charters, treaties and correspondence, the Treasurer the customs and tolls, the Harbormaster the port and its dues, and the Warden of the Weights the markets. The League council meets in the Merchant Hall and decides tariffs, the joint fleet and army, the three marches and quarrels between cities, and nothing else.

The second is the guild. It places a man, trains him, enforces the ruling of an arbitrator and disciplines the member who spoils its seal. The arbitrator is chosen by both sides, and a ruling is enforced by the guilds and not by the city. The council of a city that is asked to hear a quarrel between houses has never heard of the parties and sends them to an arbitrator.

The third is the faith, and it is kept apart from both. The monasteries chronicle, heal and copy; the monks of Argýrteíkhos record what crosses the pass and speak to the peoples beyond it. Nýkhtion governs itself, with a council of senior priests and hereditary tomb-keepers who collect fees, maintain the chambers and oversee the festivals of the dead. Every major house keeps tombs there, and no merchant-prince wishes to offend the people who guard his ancestors' rest, so the council holds a soft power no census would show.

When the three disagree, the paper decides. A claim is heard where the terms name a hearer, and whose seal stands on a document and which arbitrator both sides named are the questions the others defer to. An authority with no paper in the matter has no standing in it.

## The Written Thing

Byzarians write everything down—contracts, weights, inspections, judgments, and the terms on which a quarrel was settled. A household of any standing keeps its own copies, and a dispute opens with both sides producing paper. Most Byzarians cannot read the paper that binds them, so a hired scribe reads it aloud before it is sealed, and the arbitrator's scribe reads both sets of terms aloud before anyone argues.

This shapes the manner. A Byzarian is courteous, patient and relentlessly specific. He will not agree to a thing in general terms and work out the details later, because he regards that as how swindles begin. Negotiation is slow, exhaustive and conducted with real warmth, and at the end of it both parties know exactly what was agreed, because it is on a page with two seals.

Hospitality is generous and is also a negotiation, which the Byzarians find neither cynical nor contradictory: a man who feeds a guest well before asking has said something true about how he does business.

## Arbitration Instead of Feud

The professional arbitrators are the institution a foreigner should understand. A Byzarian dispute goes to a named arbitrator chosen by both sides, whose ruling is enforced by the guilds and not by the city, and whose own standing depends entirely on being thought fair.

The effect is that Byzaría has very little feud for a region bordering three peoples who run on it. A Byzarian who took a private revenge would find his charter suspended and his contracts void, which is a more comprehensive destruction than a blood-feud could manage. The League council wrote the rule into ordinance about 584 AF ([[lore-heladordinance|The Ordinance of Arbiters]]).

## Where the Kept Go

The dead of a Byzarian house are kept, and the word is meant in its commercial sense. The faith teaches that a soul passes first to the spirit realm and that the rites of the god and the ancestors receive it. The tomb-keepers of Nýkhtion say the same in their own terms: "We do not sell graves. We keep them, and the keeping is what you pay for." A family that wants its dead received and tended does not leave it to piety. It buys the keeping, as a perpetual endowment its heirs establish, which pays the temples of [[affiliation-selmoros|Sélmoros]] and the ancestral spirits to maintain the chambers and say the rites for as long as the endowment runs.

This changes an ordinary Tuesday. A Byzarian of any means has a tomb in the necropolis and a contract for its keeping, and he checks that the endowment is current the way he checks the lease on a warehouse. He names the endowment, the tomb and its keepers in his will, because an heir who inherits an open obligation to the dead has inherited a quarrel. A dying man settles his accounts aloud before witnesses, calling in what is owed to him and acknowledging what he owes, so that his heirs inherit closed terms. A prosperous house treats its tomb as a standing charge, and the necropolis has districts that run from the columned mausolea of merchant families to the plain stone markers of common ones. A poor family pays for what it can. The priests of Sélmoros bury paupers and the unclaimed without fee.

The dread that corresponds to all this is the account that nobody keeps. A tomb without its keeping, a house that has run out of heirs, a soldier left on a field with no one to pay for his rites: these are the Byzarian nightmares, and they explain why a necropolis city holds so much of the League's wealth and why a merchant-prince does not insult its council.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the temples of [[affiliation-selmoros|Sélmoros]] and the ancestral spirits at Nýkhtion, through the endowment that pays for the rite, and through the festivals of the dead. The pilgrims and mourning families who come from across the League and beyond are the city's custom, and the keepers sell keeping, not conversation. Sélmoros opposes anyone who raises the dead or violates a grave, so the faith gives the living no way to question them. The dead do for the living what an ancestor does for a house: they are the reason to keep the account open and the name sound.

## What Is Owed

Ask a Byzarian what a person owes, and he answers: the terms first—what was agreed, exactly as agreed, whether or not it has turned out well. Then the guild that stands behind his seal, then his master or his apprentices, then the city whose weights he trades under.

He will add, if pressed, that a man owes the truth about a defect in his own goods, and that a people who did not hold that would have nothing left to sell.

In return the guild owes a member its seal, its standing in the market and the discipline that keeps the seal worth having. A master owes an apprentice training, maintenance and a character. The city owes a freeman a place in the register, a market to trade in and a court to plead in. The arbitrator owes both sides a ruling on the clause and not on the name. The sum is exact.

## The Weak

Care in Byzaría is placed in the same institutions as everything else, and each draws its line where its charter does. The apprentice is the guild's first dependent, since the master owes him maintenance. A trade's charter writes down what the guild owes its members, and where the charter is silent the household carries its own. Beyond the guild, the monasteries are the region's healers and the temples of Lîzêlâs and Mórváná keep houses of healing and hospices in the larger cities, where the sick and dying are tended without regard to wealth. The priests of Sélmoros bury the unclaimed without fee.

The limit is the roll. The resident works and lives under the city's protection without its freedom or a vote, the unfree are bound by service or debt and cannot own property or contract, and the man struck from the roll has no standing at all. The destitute freeman has his place in the register and his guild. The destitute man without a guild or a master has the monastery and the hospice, and nothing that a counterpart is obliged to honor.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A freeman who is injured keeps his place in the register, and with it his right to be heard in court and to hold property. If he cannot meet his terms the first remedy is the arbitrator, to whom both sides may go before a default and not after, and who can reset a schedule on the clause. If the terms cannot be met at all, the debt-bondage that bounds the bottom of the ladder is where a failed account ends, and it is the harshest thing the system does. Scarcity makes the terms harder, not softer: a bad year does not cancel a contract, and the Byzarian answer to that is to buy the margin earlier, in the form of a house's reserves, a guild's chest and the grain road from Velanthia kept open.

The old move out of the shop and into another service. A retired guild master sits on the examination board or settles quarrels as an arbitrator, since his standing is the thing he can still lend, and an advocate who has left the courts advises the next generation behind the scenes. The plateau clan keeps its old as it keeps its flock. The dying are the monastery's and the temple's.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household's first and the guild's second. The merchant quarters of the Dunhari and the Khazryni keep their own law and their own faith inside the cities and take in their own. A traveler without a patron finds the caravanserai, where the master feeds a guest well before he names a price.

Teaching is the guild's. The apprentice is admitted by examination in his early teens, trained in the trade's foundations by a master who answers for him, and taught the grades in order. The monasteries copy manuscripts and keep libraries, and the merchant academies teach the standard written form to a literate few. Most Byzarians learn their letters only as far as the trade requires, which is why the scribe reads the paper aloud.

Defense is bought and conscripted together. Each city keeps its guard and the militia its guilds are bound to raise. The League's joint army is raised by conscription from all five cities and paid from the common treasury and the silver of Argýrteíkhos. The marches are held by lords the council can dismiss, and when the frontier turns dangerous the council contracts mercenary companies. The plateau herder is protected by the same chain of garrisons and by his clan, which carries its own.

## House, Guild and Name

The unit is the house. A Byzarian carries a given name and a house name that the tongue builds by different operations, so that a clerk reading a contract aloud never has to ask which is which. A house name descends in the male line and states what the house is answerable for: a trade, a place, a forebear or an eastern founder. The League's assessors grant a new name to a merchant whose concern no existing house can account for, and refuse to change the suffix to flatter the holder. Membership is the name and the register, and beyond the household stands the guild.

The ladder of a life follows the ladder of a trade. A child learns the household's name and the household's terms. In his early teens he is apprenticed, and the apprenticeship is understood as an adoption, so the master's house becomes his second. He is examined as a journeyman and sworn to the charter, and a master qualified by a vote of the masters takes his own apprentices. A freeman who prospers becomes a guild master, a house factor and a house head, and a house head of weight sits on the council. The old are arbitrators and examiners, and the dying are the keepers'.

Women hold seals and flocks of their own: a woman chairs the council of masters of the Cartographers' Guild, and [[being-smrnorenkt|Samarína Oreiníkot]] raises fine wool on the plateau under her own name, careful to commit herself to nothing she cannot fulfill.

## What the Ledger Does Not Show

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Byzarians' is the person who cannot contract. A system that settles everything by terms has no category for a harm that no one agreed to. The unfree, the resident and the man struck from the roll have no recourse that a counterpart must honor, and the League's monasteries and hospices are charity, not entitlement. Thoughtful Byzarians dispute whether a culture that treats an exact bargain as its moral center can call itself just to the people the bargain leaves out.

The sin is the village of [[place-trianthion|Trianthion]]. Within living memory the village refused a local lord's tax collectors and garrison and sheltered a fugitive bandit chief, and the lord hired [[affiliation-irnwlvscmpny|the Iron Wolves Company]] to take it. By the end of the day every adult was dead, and the bandit chief could not be told from the others. The company collected its standard rate and the lord paid without comment. The company executes a contract as written and regards the moral character of the work as the employer's problem, which is the Byzarian ethic with the edges cut off. The League that seals every other transaction has no account of this one that it says aloud. The company keeps its base outside [[place-veridon|Veridon]], a town whose people answer a question about the company with exactly what it drinks and what it pays. The League's neutrality is a reputation for keeping terms, and the Iron Wolves are the work it prefers not to admit buying. A survivor who testified in public would force the League to act.

The Cartographers' Guild shows the same pattern in a smaller place. Its seal makes a map a warranty, and someone inside the guild has been selling what the seal protects.
