**The Mother's Yes and No** is the winter tale the wise-women, the _tsingárra_, tell in the mountain villages of [[lore-tarvenanclt|Tarvénia]] of the Mother, _menútsa_, who is kept in the caves under the gods of the pantheon. It is told at the hearth in the long nights after the flocks are down, to children and to anyone else who stays up, and it is the account the high valleys give of three things: where the mountains came from, why a Tarvénan swears on a stone and not on a book, and when the world ends.

It is told in prose, in the teller's own voice, with the Mother's words said exactly, because she has only two. The tale names no one. The two shepherds are the shepherd of the east side and the shepherd of the west side, and the son is the son. A wise-woman who is asked their names gives the same answer every time, and it is the tale's own: the Mother did not ask them, so the tale does not.

: The Mother's Yes and No {#tale}

Put another log on. No, the beech, not the chestnut. The chestnut spits. Sit where you can see the fire and not where you can see me, because I'm old and the fire's better to look at. You want the Mother. You'll have her. Don't ask me questions in the middle. You'll see why.

**Before the wheel.** The priest who comes up from the town for the weddings says that the World-Weaver spun the world on her wheel, the whole of it, the sea and the grain and the vines and the people, and I don't argue with the priest. He's a good man, and he comes up the hill with his bad knee, and he's right. She spun it. She spun the lowlands and the sea and the grain and the lowland towns, and everything that goes round and round as a wheel does: the year, and the harvest, and the sun going over.

But when she sat down to spin, the mountains were already there, and she spun round them. That's why the lowlands go round the mountains like wool round a stone in a carder's hand. Look at it from the top of the pass on a clear day and you'll see it's so.

The mountains were already there because the mountains are the Mother. She lay down. Nobody knows when; before anybody. She lay down on her side with her back to the sea, and her shoulder is the high ridge, and her hip is the next one, and her knees are the peaks you can see from the summer meadow, the two with the snow on them in June. The springs that come out of the rock are hers. The water is cold because she is old and deep. The caves are where she keeps herself, the places in her that go in. That's why we say she is kept in the caves. She isn't kept there by us. She keeps herself there.

**The two words.** Now. The Mother has two words. She has _yes_ and she has _no_. That's all of them.

She doesn't need another. Everything that's so, she says _yes_ to. Everything that isn't, she says _no_ to. A thing that's half so and half not, she doesn't answer, and you go out of the cave with your question still in your hand, and that's an answer too, and you'd better think about it on the way down.

That's why the wise-women ask the sick the way they do. You've been sick. You know. _Does it hurt here? Here? When you breathe in? Did you eat the mushrooms from the beech wood?_ We ask yes and no, because yes and no is what she taught us, and a sick child can't tell you a long story, but a sick child can tell you yes. A town doctor asks you to tell him all about it, and you tell him, and he writes it down, and he pays more attention to what he wrote than to you. We don't write. We ask her way.

And she can't bear a lie. Not a big one. Not a small one. She hears every word that's spoken in the mountains, because the mountains are her and the words are spoken on her, and when someone tells a lie in the high country she wakes a little, and turns, and that's when the stones come off the crags in a still night, and the dog barks at nothing.

And when the lie's a bad one she gets up out of the cave she's in and goes to another, and she goes across the sky between the peaks as a fire, and you've seen it, the light that goes over in a summer night and is gone before you can point. And then comes the hail.

The priests say the storm is the Thunderer's, and so it is. The men go up on the high places in the spring for his first storm, on the Vigil, and they're right to. He makes the storm, and he makes the noise of it, and the light. But the hail, the hail that comes in a clear summer afternoon on one meadow and not the next, on one man's barley and not his neighbor's, that's her, going over, angry, looking for the mouth that said it. The Thunderer is loud. She isn't loud. She just finds you.

**What you do in her cave.** If you have to go to her, and some have to, you go to the cave on the side of the mountain where the offerings are, where you've seen the cheese left at the mouth and the bit of bread and the little pot of milk, and you take something and leave it at the mouth. Then you go in.

There are four things. Listen, because you'll need them, and I won't tell you twice.

You speak plainly. You say the thing. You don't say _I was wondering whether perhaps_. You say what you want to say, and if it's a question you ask it so that it can be answered _yes_ or _no_.

You don't sit down. You stand in her cave the whole time, however long it is. You're in her house, and you haven't been asked to sit, and she won't ask.

You take nothing out. Not a stone, not a drop of the water, not the bit of cheese somebody else left. What's in there is hers.

And when you come out you come out the way you went in, facing her, walking backward, one foot behind the other, all the way to the light. You don't turn your back on her. You've seen the old men come out of the cave that way at midsummer and you laughed. Don't laugh. They're doing it right.

That's all. Now I'll tell you about the stone.

**The east side and the west side.** There was a high meadow once above the third valley. Not this valley. That one. It's the best meadow in the high country, the grass comes early and stays late, and there's a spring in the rock at the top of it. On the east of it there was a shepherd, and on the west there was a shepherd, and they both drove their flocks up to it on the summer drive, and both of them said it was theirs.

It wasn't a quarrel at first. It was the summer. The east-side man's ewes grazed up to the spring and over it, and the west-side man's ewes grazed up to the spring and over it, and they met in the middle, and the dogs fought, and the two men shouted at each other across the meadow. That's all it was for years. Every summer.

But you know how it goes. The shouting got worse. And one summer the east-side man's dog killed the west-side man's dog, and it was a good dog, a black one with a white foot, and the west-side man came over the meadow with his crook and broke the east-side man's arm with it. And the next summer the east-side man came up early, before the drive, with three cousins, and they built a wall of loose stones across the meadow, just below the spring, on the west side of it, so the spring was on his side. And the west-side man came up on the drive and saw the wall and pulled it down, stone by stone, with his own hands, and threw the stones down the scree, and it took him two days. And then they both came up to the meadow with crossbows.

That's when the women took them to her. Yes, the women. Who else? The east-side man's wife and the west-side man's wife met at the spring one night, because they'd both had enough, and they said: this ends now, or we bury one of them this summer and the other next. And they each went home and said to their men: you're going to the Mother. And they went. Of course they went.

**In the cave.** They went to the cave together, the two men, and their wives behind them as far as the mouth, and no further. The wives left the cheese. The men went in.

They stood. Nobody told them to stand, they knew. It was dark, and cold, and the water was dripping, and they couldn't see anything, and they didn't know where she was, but they knew she was there, the way you know someone's in the room in the dark.

And the east-side man said, because he was the one with the broken arm and he thought that gave him the right to speak first: _The meadow is mine, up to the spring and over it._

And out of the dark she said: _No._

That's all. Just: _No._ It wasn't loud. It came out of the rock all round them, and the water stopped dripping for a moment, and then it went on.

The west-side man laughed. He shouldn't have. He laughed and said: _The meadow is mine, up to the spring and over it._

And she said: _No._

Well. They stood there. They hadn't thought of that. Each of them had come in to hear the other one told _no_, and they'd both been told _no_, and now they didn't know what to say. A man who's been told _no_ wants to argue. But you can't argue with her. She hasn't got the words for it.

So the east-side man said, after a long while: _Whose is it, then?_

And she didn't answer. Because that's not a question you can answer _yes_ or _no_. You see? I told you. You go out of the cave with that one still in your hand.

They stood there a long time more. The west-side man's legs were aching. He'd walked up that morning. And at last he said, very low, because he was beginning to understand: _Is it yours?_

And she said: _Yes._

**The bargain.** Now this is the important part. Listen.

It was hers. The meadow and the spring and the grass that came early and the rock it came out of. It was all hers. It had always been hers. They'd been fighting about who owned a piece of her knee.

So the two of them, the east-side man and the west-side man, stood in the dark and, for the first time in all those summers, they talked to each other instead of shouting across a meadow. And they had to do it plainly, because she was listening, and every time either of them said a thing that wasn't so, she said _No_, out of the rock, and the water stopped dripping.

The east-side man said: _My ewes need the spring in the dry month._ And she said _Yes_, because they did.

The west-side man said: _Your ewes don't need the whole meadow._ And she said _Yes._

The east-side man said: _Your dog started it._ And she said _No._ And he went red, in the dark, and he didn't say that again.

The west-side man said: _I'm sorry about your arm._ And she didn't say anything at all. That one was half so and half not. He was sorry he'd had to. He wasn't sorry he'd done it. She knew. They both knew she knew.

And so it went on, all the afternoon, and into the evening, and the wives at the cave mouth sat down on the rocks and waited, because they could hear the voices but not the words, and they could hear that the voices weren't shouting. And at the end of it the two men had said this, and she had said _Yes_ to every part of it:

_The east side has the spring in the dry month. The west side has the spring the rest of the summer. The meadow is cut across from the crag with the chough's nest on it to the bottom of the scree. Above the line is the east side's. Below the line is the west side's. The dogs are kept on their own sides. Neither of us builds a wall._

And when they'd said the last part, she said _Yes_, and then she did a thing she hadn't done before.

**The stone.** There was a sound in the dark like the sound you hear when the snow lets go of the crag in spring, far off, and the floor of the cave moved a little under their feet, and something came rolling out of the dark toward them across the cave, slowly, and stopped at their feet. And they couldn't see it, but they put their hands down and felt it, both of them, and it was a stone. A round grey stone, about as high as a man's knee, cold, and wet from the water.

And they understood. Don't ask me how. They understood that she was giving them a piece of herself to carry, and that they were to carry it up to the meadow and set it in the line they'd agreed, between the crag with the chough's nest and the bottom of the scree. And that whatever they said with their hands on that stone, they were saying in her cave.

So they carried it out. Now, how did they carry it out, and come out facing her? Think. That's right. The east-side man walked backward at the front, holding one side, and the west-side man walked backward at the back, holding the other, and they came out of the cave between them, and neither of them turned his back on her, and the wives at the mouth saw them come out of the dark carrying the stone between them, backward, step by step, the two men who'd come up to the meadow with crossbows, and the wives began to laugh, and then they began to cry. Then they all four carried it up to the meadow.

They set it on the line. They put their hands on it, both of them together, and they said the bargain again, the whole of it, from _the east side has the spring_ to _neither of us builds a wall_. And the stone was cold under their hands. And nothing else happened. It didn't need to.

That's the first stone. That's the oath stone, the _goitsára_. Every one you've seen, the one in the fair's yard, the one at the head of this valley by the shrine, the one the companies swear their contracts on before they go down to the lowlands, every one of them is a piece of her. That's why you put your hand on it. You're putting your hand on her. And whatever you say there, you're saying in her cave, whether you can see the dark or not.

**The son.** Now you think that's the end. It isn't. It's never the end with a stone.

The east-side man had a son. He grew up with the stone in the meadow. He'd known it all his life, the way you know the step at your door. And when his father was old and the son had the flock, he looked at the line across the meadow, from the crag with the chough's nest to the bottom of the scree, and he thought: it's a long way below the spring, that line. It could be a stride lower. Who'd know?

Who'd know. You see where this goes.

So one night in the summer he went up to the meadow and he dug round the stone and he rolled it a stride down the slope, one stride, and he set it again, and he stamped the earth round it so it looked as it always had. And he went home and slept.

In the morning he went up to the meadow and the stone was back where it had been. A stride up. The earth round it wasn't even broken.

He told himself he'd been drunk. He hadn't. He went up again that night and dug round it again and rolled it a stride down, and set it, and stamped the earth, and this time he sat by it till dawn, and he must have slept, because when he opened his eyes it was light and the stone was a stride up the slope where it had always been, and his hands were black with earth.

And the third night, you know there's a third night, he went up and dug round it and rolled it down. Two strides this time. And as he was stamping the earth round it there was a light in the sky, from the peak on the east side to the peak on the west, going over, quick, like a torch thrown, and gone. He saw it. He stood there with his foot on the earth and his mouth open.

The hail came the next afternoon. It was a clear afternoon. There wasn't a cloud over the west side. The hail came on the east side of the line, and only there, out of a little black cloud that sat over the east side of the meadow and nowhere else, and it came down like stones off a crag, and it killed his flock. The ewes, and the lambs, and the old ram with the bell. The bell rang the whole time, and then it stopped. The west-side man's ewes, a stone's throw below the line, grazed through it with the sun on their backs.

**What the son asked.** He went to her. What else could he do? He took the last cheese in the house and he left it at the mouth and he went in, and he stood. He didn't sit. He knew that much. He stood in the dark a long time before he could say it.

He said: _I moved the stone._

She said: _Yes._

He said: _Can I put it back?_

She said: _No._

He stood there. He thought he understood. He thought she meant she'd put it back herself, she'd done it twice. But it wasn't that. He'd moved it three times, and twice she'd put it back for him, and the third time the stone was where he'd put it, two strides down, and the hail had found him there. He'd moved it, and it was moved, and that was a thing that was so now. You can't make a thing not have been so. Not even her.

So he asked, and his voice was very small: _Can anyone?_

She said: _Yes._

And he thought about that for a long time. And then he asked the right question.

He said: _Can my son?_

And she said: _Yes._

That's what it costs, you see. That's the price of a moved stone. He couldn't put it back. He was the man who'd moved it, and he was that man for the rest of his life, the way a man who's broken a contract is that man, and the whole company with him. But his son could. His son, who hadn't moved anything, could go up to the meadow when he was grown and put his hands on the stone and roll it two strides up the slope to where his grandfather and the west-side man had set it, and say the bargain over it, the whole of it, and keep it.

And that's what happened. The son's son did it, when he was grown. The west-side man's grandson stood on the other side of the stone with his hand on it, and they said it together, and it held. It's held since. You can go and look. It's there, below the spring, between the crag and the scree. There's a chough's nest on the crag still. Not the same chough.

He walked out of her cave facing her, the son, all the way out, backward. He never sat down in it. He took nothing out. And he never went in again.

**When the gods came.** Now, later, people came over the mountains from the east with their words, the words we speak now, and long after them came the twelve and their priests. I'm not going to tell you about that. Ask the priest; he likes telling it. He'll tell you how they came, and that's all true.

What I'll tell you is that when the priests came up into the high valleys, they said that oaths are the god of the gate's. Árdavon's. The two faces. That a man who swears, swears before him, and a man who breaks his word offends him. And that's true too. I don't argue with priests.

So they built a shrine to him, at the head of the valley, and they told us to swear there. And we did. We go to the shrine and we say it there, in front of the two faces. And then we walk the ten steps from the shrine to the stone and we put our hands on the stone and say it again.

The priest doesn't like that much. He says the god hears it the first time. I'm sure he does. But the god's at the gate. He sees you come in and go out. She's under your feet the whole time. She hears every word you say on her. And she says _yes_, or she says _no_.

**When she gets up.** Now. Last thing. Then bed.

She's lying down, the Mother. On her side with her back to the sea. She's been lying there since before the wheel. And one day she's going to get up.

When she gets up the mountains get up with her, because they're her. Her shoulder comes up, and her hip, and her knees, the two peaks with the snow on them. And everything on her slides off. The snow comes off the peaks, all of it, all at once, and comes down the valleys, every valley, the way the snow came down the gorge above the third valley the winter your grandfather was born and took the byre and the mill, only all the valleys at once. And under the snow go the villages and the meadows and the stones and the shrines, and the snow doesn't stop at the bottom of the valleys. It goes on down to the lowlands and over them, over the walled towns and the vineyards and the roads, over everything the wheel spun round her, and on into the sea. And then she stands up. And there's nobody left to tell her a lie.

When? I'll tell you when. She gets up on the day when there's no hand on any stone. When nobody, in any valley, anywhere in the mountains, puts his hand on her and says a thing that's so. When the last man in the last valley who still goes to the stone stops going, because a paper's easier, or a priest is easier, or a lie is easier. That day she'll think there's nobody left on her worth lying still for. And she'll get up.

So that's why. That's why your father goes to the stone at the fair, every year, and puts his hand on it, and says: _The east side has the spring in the dry month_, or whatever it is with us, the water, the boundary with the miller, the lease. That's why the companies swear on the stone before they go down, and why a man who breaks a contract is the worst thing a man can be. It isn't only his company he's let down. Every time a man puts his hand on her and says a true thing and keeps it, she lies still another year.

Somebody has to keep doing it. It doesn't have to be a lot of people. It has to be somebody, in some valley, every year.

Will she get up in your time? I don't know. Go and ask her. You know how to ask. Plainly. Standing up. Don't take anything. Come out facing her.

And if she says _no_, come back down and keep your word for another year.

And if she doesn't answer, you'll know that one's still in your hand.

Bed.
