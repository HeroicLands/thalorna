**The Adept and the Hail** is told at the hearths of the hill farms under [[place-durgalis|Dúrgalis]] when the winter is long enough to want a laugh in it. It is the valley's tale of an adept of the [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Ordo Arcanis]] who came up from a city chapter to sell the farms a contract against the hail, and of the wise-woman **Berúzta**, the Chestnut, so called because she was old, broad and prickly, who told the valley's assembly how to buy it.

It is a tale of a contract kept, and the valley tells it as a comedy. It turns on what the high valleys hold about the hail, that the hail is the Mother's and comes for a lie ([[lore-motheryesno|The Mother's Yes and No]]), and a teller who has told that one first tells this one shorter. The adept is not named in it. A teller who is asked why answers that he signed his name to everything, so the valley had enough of it.

: The Adept and the Hail {#tale}

You want a funny one. All right. This one's funny. Your grandmother didn't think so at the time, because she lost a field of barley in it, but she laughed later.

**The adept.** One spring an adept of the Ordo came up the road from the city to the valley. You know what that is. A mage. Not a wise-woman, not a hedge-man who can charm a wart. A mage of the order, with a ring and a seal and a little case of papers, and a mule with better harness than the lord's.

He came to the assembly. He'd asked who governed the valley, and somebody told him the assembly did, which is true, and he waited three days at the inn for it to meet, and he was very polite about it. And when it met, in the square under the castle, he stood up on the steps of the well and made a speech.

It was a good speech. I'll give him that. He said the valley lost a field or two of barley to the hail every summer, which was true. He said the hail was weather, and weather was a thing the order had studied in its colleges for as long as it had had colleges, and it could be turned. He said that for a fee, a modest fee, paid at the autumn fair, he would stay in the valley the whole summer and turn every hail-cloud that came over the pass, and the barley would come in whole. He had a contract, already written out, with the order's seal on it. He held it up. It was a beautiful thing, all red wax and ribbons.

And the assembly sat there. You know how they sit.

**What the assembly thought.** Nobody in the valley wanted a mage. Nobody in Tarvénia wants a mage. A mage is one more somebody who wants to tell you what to do, and this one had a seal on it. But the barley. Everyone had lost a field to the hail at one time or another. And he was offering.

So the old men argued, and the young men argued, and the lord's steward said the lord thought it was a good idea, and that made everyone like it less, and it went round and round. And then old Berúzta stood up.

You've heard of her. The wise-woman. The Chestnut. She'd been the valley's wise-woman forty years. She'd set half the bones in that square and delivered the other half. When she stood up the assembly stopped talking.

She said: Let's buy it.

They stared at her. She said: Let's buy it. But not with his paper. With ours. And with my terms.

The adept was delighted. He said of course, of course, the valley's own terms, the order was always glad to accommodate local custom. He was very polite.

**Berúzta's terms.** She stood there in the square with her stick, and she said her terms, and the notary from the castle wrote them down, and they were these.

One. The adept stays in the valley from the first barley to the harvest and does what he says he can do.

Two. For every field in the valley that the hail does not touch this summer, the valley pays him one silver at the autumn fair.

Three. For every field in the valley that the hail does touch this summer, he pays the valley one silver at the autumn fair.

Four. Every week, at the assembly, he stands on the steps of the well and tells the assembly what he did that week, plainly, so that the valley knows what it's paying for.

That's all. Four terms. She sat down.

The adept thought about it. You could see him think. There were over two hundred fields in the valley. If the hail took ten in a bad year, that was ten silver from him and nearly two hundred to him. If he turned the hail at all, if he turned half of it, he'd go home rich. He couldn't lose. And the fourth term, the weekly account, that was nothing. A mage loves to tell you what he's done.

He said: Done. And they took it to the stone in the square, and the adept put his hand on the stone, and looked a bit puzzled at that, because they don't do it that way in the city, and he swore it. And the valley swore it. And the notary put the castle's seal on it beside the order's seal, because the adept wanted that. And the summer began.

**The summer.** Now, the adept was a real adept. Don't think he wasn't. He went up to the pass in the first week with his case of papers and a little brass instrument on legs, and he set it up on the rocks, and he looked at the sky through it, and he wrote things down. And when the first black cloud came over the pass, in the second week of the barley, he stood up on the rocks and did something with his hands and said something in the order's speech, and the cloud split in two and went round the valley on both sides. The shepherds saw it. It was a real thing.

And on the assembly day he stood on the steps of the well and said to the assembly: This week I turned a storm at the pass. It went round the valley on both sides. There was no hail.

Which was true. And there was no hail.

The next week another cloud came, and he turned that one too, and he stood on the steps and said so, and it was true, and there was no hail.

And the third week he turned one, and he stood on the steps, and this time he said: This week I turned two storms at the pass.

He hadn't. He'd turned one. The second one went over in the night while he was asleep, and it was only rain, and it didn't matter. But two sounded better than one, for a man being paid by the field. He was a city man. In the city you round up.

And the next afternoon, in a clear sky, out of a little black cloud that sat over the valley and nowhere else, the hail came down on one field. One. It was the field behind the inn, where the adept had his lodging, and his mule was in it.

The mule was all right. The barley wasn't.

**The weeks.** He couldn't understand it. He went up to the pass with the brass instrument and looked through it for a day and a night and found nothing. He said at the assembly that week: There was a small irregular cloud, of a kind the colleges have described, and it could not have been foreseen. And it was a lie, because he didn't know what it was. He'd never seen a cloud like it.

The next afternoon the hail came down on the field below the inn. One field.

The valley watched this. Everybody in the valley knew by then what Berúzta had done, except him. The old men sat in the square and watched him come down off the steps of the well every assembly day, and they watched the sky every day after. They started betting on it.

And it wasn't only him. That's the funny part. That's the part your grandmother didn't laugh at till later.

Because the hail didn't only find the adept. You know what the hail comes for. It comes for a lie. Every lie in the high country, she hears. And that summer, in that valley, everybody was watching the sky for hail, and everybody was counting fields, and everybody was thinking about silver.

The miller told the assembly his weir had been damaged by the lord's men, so he could have the lord pay for it, and it hadn't. Hail on the miller's barley, the next day. The lord's steward told the assembly the lord's tithe barn was half empty, and it was full. Hail on the lord's home field, in front of the castle, with the lord watching from the wall. A man swore in the square that his neighbor's goat had eaten his beans, and the goat had been tied up all week. Hail on his beans. And on the goat, which wasn't fair, but there it is.

And your grandmother. Your grandmother told her sister she hadn't been to the fair in the next valley to look at the blacksmith's son, and she had. Hail on her barley. One field. She told me so herself, sixty years later, and she still went red.

**The reckoning.** Well. The harvest came in, and the autumn fair came, and the assembly met in the square, and the adept stood on the steps of the well with his case, and the notary from the castle stood beside him with the contract, and they counted the fields.

Two hundred and twelve fields in the valley. The hail had touched twenty-three of them.

The adept had turned every storm that came over the pass. Every one. He'd done what he said he could do. It was true. The storms from the pass never touched the valley that whole summer. All twenty-three fields had been hit by hail out of a clear sky, from a little black cloud, the day after somebody lied.

So the notary read the terms. One silver from the valley for every field the hail didn't touch: a hundred and eighty-nine silver. One silver from the adept for every field it did: twenty-three silver. The valley owed him a hundred and sixty-six silver, and it paid. Every bit. In the square, in front of the stone, counted out on the steps of the well. The valley keeps its contracts.

And the adept stood there with his hundred and sixty-six silver, which was a great deal of money, and he should have been happy. He wasn't. He'd had the hail on his own field six times that summer, and he'd paid the innkeeper for his barley each time out of his own purse, because the innkeeper had made him, and he didn't know why, and he hated not knowing why. He was a mage. Not knowing why is the worst thing that can happen to a mage.

He said to the assembly: I turned every storm at the pass. You saw it. Where did the other hail come from?

And Berúzta stood up with her stick.

**What Berúzta said.** She said: You turned the weather. The weather's yours. You did what you said, and you're paid for it, and you're paid right.

The other hail's not weather. It's hers. It comes for a lie. You told us four of them on those steps, and she heard every one, and she found your field every time, because your field's where your mouth sleeps.

And the rest of us told her nineteen. She found those too. Twenty-three fields. Twenty-three lies. You paid a silver for each of them, under term three, because that's what we agreed.

So what the valley has had from you this summer, for a hundred and sixty-six silver, is this. Our barley's whole from the pass storms, which is worth it. And we know who our liars are. Every one. By name, by field, by the day after.

And the hail can't be turned, because it isn't weather. And it doesn't come for us at all, if we don't lie. So we won't be buying it again next year. We'll be doing it ourselves. Thank you.

And she sat down.

The assembly didn't laugh. Not then. You don't laugh at a man in front of the stone. They thanked him, very politely, and they stood up when he left, and the lord's steward walked him to the edge of the valley. He went down the road to the city with his hundred and sixty-six silver and his brass instrument and his mule.

They laughed after. They've been laughing since.

**After.** He wrote it up for his order. They say he did. They say it's in the order's library in the city to this day, in a big book, with drawings of the little black cloud, and a note that the hail of the hill country under Dúrgalis is irregular and cannot be studied, and that the inhabitants are uncooperative.

That last part's true.

The miller mended his own weir. The steward was kept on, because the lord liked a steward who'd lie for him, but the lord stopped believing a word he said. The man with the goat paid for the goat. And your grandmother married the blacksmith's son, in the end, from the next valley, which you know, because he's your grandfather.

And the valley never bought another contract against the hail. Every year since, at the autumn fair, the assembly stands in the square and says out loud how many fields the hail took that summer. It's never as many as twenty-three. Some years it's none, and in those years, while she was alive, Berúzta sat through the count with her eyes on the young men and said nothing at all.

That's the adept and the hail. Bed. And if you told me you'd washed your feet, go and wash them, because it's a clear night.
