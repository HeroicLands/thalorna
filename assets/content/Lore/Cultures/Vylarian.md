A Vylarian lives inside a memory. The [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|empire]] still spans half of Ankaris on the maps, the [[affiliation-seldan|Senate]] still debates, the aqueducts still run in the older quarters—and every educated Vylarian knows, without ever putting it plainly, that the best of it is behind him. The result is a people who are sophisticated, proud, formidable, and difficult to sit with for long. A Vylarian does not boast about the empire; he assumes it, which is worse.

## The Old Forms

The aristocracy clings to the forms, and the forms are elaborate: layered social hierarchy, patronage networks, precedence worked out to the step, and a Senate whose procedure is immaculate and whose authority erodes a little further every decade.

The stratification is real. The old senatorial families hold vast estates and run politics through a web of alliance, marriage and obligation; below them sit the _gulmenoi_ (equestrians), wealthy merchants and military officers; below those the citizens, most of them veterans of the legions and their wives; below the citizens the residents, who are the great majority of the people, born to the empire, taxed by it and holding no share in it; and below all of them a substantial population of slaves and indentured servants. Mobility exists and is limited, and the gap between the top and the bottom widens with each generation.

What a foreigner should understand is that the forms are the machine by which a shrinking authority is still made to work. A magistrate whose writ nobody fears can convene a court that everybody attends, because attending is what is done, and a surprising amount of the empire runs on exactly that. The cities set magnificent temples, forums and aqueducts beside crumbling tenements and public works nobody has funded in two generations, and Vylarians see both and mention only the first.

## The Order of What Is Owed

The language has one word for duty, _yerpalon_, and it is a list: the gods, the ancestors, the house, the patron, the city, the empire, in that order and no other. **Good is giving each its due in its place; evil is setting a lower claim above a higher one, or striking at the record that keeps the list.** The offenses the culture finds hardest to forgive are the ones that falsify the record: a forged seal, a counterfeit edict, an oath broken after it was sworn before the gods. An oath (_zaron_) sworn before the _holzathos_, the officer who administers the Imperial Cult for the Orzas, binds in the courts as well as before heaven, and a document sealed in the absence of the keeper of seals is a forgery. The Scribal Guild treats a forged edict acted on as a crisis of trust the whole apparatus might not survive.

Success is _velda_, honor, which the language defines as a public thing made of offices held and remembered. A Vylarian is a success when the city repeats his name and a failure when it stops: _anvelda_ is "a name the city has stopped repeating." The marks of success come in a recognizable order. A house name stands on the roll with an heir named behind it, a patron's morning call is crowded, a seal is honored where it is shown, and a person is placed on the right step of precedence the moment he enters a room. A person who cannot be placed, whether a foreigner or a guest who will not name his patron, is _anzirothos_, unplaced, and the word is uneasy.

The Vylarians value weight and endurance. _Solkima_, gravity, is the manner proper to a man of standing, and an enduring thing is worth more than a bright one. They value a law obeyed when it costs something, a craft kept excellent, a line kept unbroken, and hospitality done correctly; the generous host and the exacting snob are the same man. They value least the things that threaten the list. Wealth without standing earns contempt: the tax-farmers of the Tolvan are rich, politically useful and despised. Force outside the law belongs to the barbarians, who have preferences instead of laws, and novelty is not prized: a guild treats a technique as property, so craft is excellent and advances slowly. And no Vylarian should draw a sword on another. The estates of [[place-hylen|Hilen]] hold that rule most fiercely, because the armies of the Broken Succession marched across their wheat ([[lore-olcassabtl|The Battle of Olkassa]]).

## The Man Admired

[[being-xrphnklmkns|Zerofon Kalmakanes]], a jailer in Moktur, is the ideal at ordinary scale. He began as a Watch soldier who preferred evidence, records and patient questioning to glory, and he has run the largest prison in the province for many years. No one from the highest official to the lowest prisoner questions his commitment to his stated principles, and his word is held reliable. He keeps order by being predictable, so the prisoners know what each act will cost, and he extends the occasional small mercy to a prisoner honestly trying to reform. He refuses to join corruption and despises the warden the court has set over him. His working belief is that order is the precondition of every other good, and he sees himself as a servant of the Aureldian principles of law and consequence. Justice, in a prison, is a form of mercy.

The admiration has a price he is aware of. He is rigid, emotionally distant, suspicious of the new locks and systems that younger guards trust, and he has built his whole life around a post that may be redesigned out from under him. The culture admires him anyway. What it prizes in him is the consistency of the man who makes the forms work for everyone, the lowest prisoner included.

A second male example comes from the histories. When the _felzos_ (legate) Makandas took the burned city of Orzara, he forgave the legions that had fought him, paid the Senate's regiments as well as his own from the treasury he had just seized, and founded the house that rebuilt the capital ([[lore-restorehouse|The Restoration]]). The line "restored by Makandas" runs along every monument of the rebuilding, though he touched none of the stone. Moktur remembers him as the _felzos_ who put a house on the throne.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-ailemkrmys|Ailema Keramyes]], a Guildmaster of the Imperial Scribal Guild, is admired for the custody of the record. She was apprenticed at fourteen to the finest calligrapher in the empire, illuminated a copy of the Code of Imperial Law at twenty and held her Guildmaster's seal at twenty-two. Her work is finished on time and to exacting standards, which is why she commands premium prices, and the Guild she belongs to holds that documentary authority comes from procedural integrity: an edict becomes the Orzas's word only because a scribe's seal and a witnessed procedure stand behind it. Her kind of authority is not the soldier's or the jailer's. It is the authority of the person who can tell a real edict from a forged one, and who is believed.

She pays for it too. Her perfectionism has become a cage, her life has thinned to a workshop and a list of clients, and she has begun to wonder whether the permanent perfect page has eclipsed a life lived. The culture respects the type and does not pity it. The admirable woman here is the one whose reliability the city's machinery runs on, as the admirable man is the one who makes its law work.

The coast keeps a third example in the widow Ilthera Salken of Bralvik. One winter a raiding crew held the headland above her burned village and the imperial _felzos_ chose to starve both them and the village below. She carried a basket of fish up the slope to the crew's smoke-rack and brought it down smoked, and the trade went on under the pickets' eyes all winter ([[lore-bankedfire|The Winter of the Banked Fire]]). Her family's smokehouse still keeps the Old Coal that lights every smokehouse in the town. She is the Vylarian answer to scarcity: bargain with what is in front of you, keep the fire banked, and send the bill afterwards.

## What the City Keeps

A Vylarian can point to a short list of objects. The **Code of Imperial Law** is the written law, codified, argued, appealable, and copied in illuminated hands. The **orkaima** is a legion's consecrated eagle, and a legion that loses it is disbanded. The **thrima** is the sacred line walked and consecrated at the Founding, marked with boundary-stones, inside which citizens live by right and no legion may march and beyond which, and never within, the dead must lie. The **wax masks** a house keeps in the cupboard beside its door are its charter in the only sense that matters to the house. The **Veterans' Law** has become a speech: every year a centurion tells the recruits that there is one door and the army holds it open, and he does not know the words come from a statute. The **statue of the Founder** in the Orzas's Circle bears a crowned rune where the script puts a name, enclosing a patch of stone worked smooth, and the clerks of the Seldan refer anyone who asks to a record that does not give the name. The **Vylarian Calendar** counts every year from the Founding and is kept far past the empire's borders, and each of its months belongs to one of the Twelve.

None of these is a scripture. The Vylarians keep law, memory and boundary, and each is something a person can hold, show or walk.

## The Kept Fire and the Twelve

An ordinary Vylarian household keeps a fire (_karalzon_) that is never let go out, and the household gods, _karalzis_, are "those of the kept fire." Someone of the household feeds it every morning, and the day begins from it. Beside the door stands the cupboard with the wax faces of the ancestors who held office, and anyone leaving the house passes the line. The Twelve are served in the temples, which dominate the old city, on the feast days of each month, and the faithful make their offerings to the god whose keeping the month is in. The Imperial Cult, _zalvon_, is a department of the state with revenue and seats at public ceremonies, and sharing in it is a declaration of loyalty as well as a prayer. A Vylarian does not experience a conflict between these. The gods rule the city's year, the ancestors rule the house, and the Imperial Cult is a courtesy owed to the throne.

The seven days of the week are a farming year in miniature and set the rhythm of work. Newday is the day a piece of work is begun, Slowday is the day accounts are settled, and nothing is begun on Setday. The empire's religious life beyond this core is a bazaar: fire-shrines of the Ashaian faiths draw the fashionable, Asguardian warrior-cults draw officers, Helionite mystery initiations draw the curious aristocracy, and the Senate and great houses use patronage of a temple as a political tool. A _kurthos_ (senator) may pray to Árdavon in public, consult a fire-priest in private and sit through an initiation for the connections.

## The Year Kept in Public

The festivals that matter are the ones that renew something owed. The Feast of Thresholds falls at the turning of the year, in the gate's month, Kalpezandi, the last month of the calendar, when Árdavon's priests host the renewal of oaths and contracts: lords and vassals, merchants and spouses stand before the temple and have their bonds witnessed and written down. Kalmethandi, the month of going to the ancestors, is Sélmoros's, and his three-day Silent Passage puts black candles in windows and the names of the year's dead on every tongue. The Night of Ashes is a public festival of the Black Flame in the commercial cities, and the throne's refusal to proscribe the faith leaves the orthodox clergy no way to stop it.

The state's own days are the triumph and the accession. A triumph turns the crossing of the thrima by a returning army into spectacle along the Paldor, and an accession pays the legions and the guard the donative that began with Makandas. The games are the other half of "bread and games": the capital's dole includes the price of them, and the Racecourse is where the Orzas learns the mood of the people. The Seldan's clerks sweep the empty Senate hall of the Old Hill on the Republic's feast days and lock it again. Each of these is for something concrete. The Thresholds renew the contracts the year runs on, the Silent Passage settles the dead, the triumph and the donative pay the army for its loyalty, and the games keep the capital fed and quiet.

## Honors and Penalties

The ladder of reward runs from the private to the public. A client's work earns his patron's favor, a favor leaves a debt (_orfenon_) that is real though no court enforces it, and a long record of favors earns a place in a patron's household, a recommendation, a post. A soldier's service earns the citizenship after twenty years, and an act of loyalty or a great benefaction earns it as a rare Senate grant that makes the grantee a citizen just as service does. Manumission (_liskaima_) gives a slave a gender and a name. Offices held and remembered build honor, and honor is cut into stone, as the line "restored by Makandas" is.

The ladder of penalty runs the other way. The lightest is a lost favor, and for a client the loss of his patron's door is the loss of his place. A fine or other penalty (_kuthreza_) comes next, then the loss of a seal, a charter or a commission, which ends a trade. A resident can be given the lash or sent to the arena; a citizen is owed a trial and, at the worst, a clean execution. A citizen's gravest court sentence is exile (_venthodis_). The gravest of all is unnaming (_neharlon_), which strikes a person from every roll, forbids anyone to say the name aloud, and is given only by the Senate. Proscription is the other extreme: the property is forfeit to the treasury and the life to whoever collects it.

## Where the Senate Ends and the Temple Begins

Four parties share the empire: the Orzas and his court, the Senate, the military commanders and the Thordimalon (the Arcane Order). The Orzas holds the title of Sarnaks Maldor, so the state cult's chief priest is the sovereign himself. Civil authority and religious authority are therefore one institution wearing two hats at the top, and two institutions below. The _peltroi_ (ward magistrates) keep the ward, the Civil Courts hear civil suits, the Criminal Tribunal judges theft and violence, the Verdan Orzara hears the gravest civic cases, and the throne's own imperial tribunals sit in the Imperial Offices for anything touching the nobility, the Senate or the crown. The Imperial Scribal Guild keeps the archive and makes a document an act. The Tolvan collects. The Sélmoran priests keep the death records, and Árdavon's priests, who also keep the Temple of the Twelve, witness and record oaths and contracts for a fee.

When these conflict the strongest party at that moment decides, and the strongest party is whoever holds the gate or the granary. The Sorvan learned that the gate decides a reign, the Senate learned how little a made Orzas is worth without a legion, and the legions learned that an army holding the right granaries can walk over both. A case can be lifted from the civic courts into the imperial ones at the throne's word, and a well-placed enemy on the Verdan Orzara is as dangerous as a charge. The courts are real and the law is a currency, and both statements are true at once.

The Thordimalon is an imperial institution and in Vylaria works at full strength: licensed, chartered and built into the administration, and regarded by ordinary Vylarians as one more arm of government. Every child who shows magical aptitude is expected to be reported and enrolled, and a practitioner outside its register is hunted. Provincials find this unnerving, and Vylarians find the provincials' hedge-witches squalid. The Arcane Order and the throne need each other and do not trust each other, and the Arcane Order's leaders do not always agree with the Orzas or the Senate.

## Where the Dead Go

A Vylarian who dies is said to go to the ancestors (_kalmethathen_). The soul, _ziravon_, is the breath that goes to them, and they are the dead a house still counts, the row of wax masks in the cupboard by the door. The Silent Judge Sélmoros keeps the gate and his priests attend the dying: a dying citizen makes a final testimony, confesses wrongs and affirms goods, and the priests keep the record. The ancestors are not remembered out of piety alone. A line that ends has no one to keep the masks, speak the names or maintain the tomb, and the dead are counted only by the house that remembers them.

That belief changes an ordinary Tillday. A Vylarian rises, feeds the kept fire, passes the cupboard of masks on the way out and goes to the patron's morning call or to work, reminded whose line he carries. Only a house with office behind it keeps the faces; a veteran's family keeps the fire, the household gods and the names. Either way he guards the name more closely than the purse. He writes a will (_lemvisa_) early and names an heir, and when a line runs short he adopts one (_aipsena_), which the language calls as honorable a way to get an heir as any other. He pays for a tomb outside the boundary, along the road, because the dead may not lie inside it, and the Necropolis of the capital is ranked by wealth from the senatorial mausolea down to the pauper-trenches. He joins a funeral where living men of the right build wear the masks of the line and walk behind the bier. He pays a priest of Sélmoros for the funeral and for perpetual care of the tomb, which is how the temples fund their independence. He weighs what he leaves against the inheritance tax, which falls on whatever passes at a death. And he fears the unnaming more than exile, because it takes the name and so the place among the ancestors.

## Reaching the Dead

The living honor the dead with wine that noble houses pour on their ancestors' graves, with the names spoken at the Silent Passage, with masks worn at funerals, and with the care of the tomb. A licensed diviner (_ozethos_) reads signs in a bird or a flame, and the army's chaplains, the Augurars, are licensed jointly by the legions and the Arcane Order. Nothing in the faith lets the living question the dead. Sélmoros opposes anyone who raises the dead or violates a grave, and the priests of the Necropolis, Sélmoran and Asguardian, hold its vaults in a custody that Orzara's people speak of in lowered voices. What the dead do for the living is the ordinary thing the ancestors do for a house: they are the standing reason to keep the line.

The institutions charge accordingly. Sélmoros's priests are paid for funerals and tomb care, never for answers from the dead, and they bury paupers and the unclaimed without fee.

## What Is Owed, and What Is Paid

A citizen owes the taxes his station carries, the obedience of the law even where it is inconvenient, and the twenty years of service that made him a citizen if he is not noble. He is owed the dole, the right to dwell in Orzara, standing at law with an appeal to the imperial tribunals, a clean death if condemned, and first claim on the posts the empire reserves for those who served it. A resident owes taxes and the patron or sponsor on whom the residence depends, and is owed the protection of the empire "after a fashion." A slave owes labor, is declined as a thing, and has no standing at law except through an owner who answers for him. A freedman is owed the standing that manumission gives him, and the language marks it by giving him a gender.

A client owes his patron the morning call and carries the patron's interests; the patron carries the client in return, and the debt each favor leaves is real though no court enforces it. The master of a household (_zorkos_) answers at law for everyone beneath his roof, and the household owes him obedience. A host owes a guest his roof and his protection, and a guest owes the host an answer to who he is and whose he is. Each of these can be recited, and Vylarians do recite them, in the order of the list.

## The Weak

Care is a duty to those who can hold the giver to it, and a kindness to everyone else. The dole is a citizen's floor and the citizen's alone. Beneath it, the Aureldian faiths supply what the state does not. Morvana's hospices of the Waning Moon in every major city shelter and comfort the dying, funded by gifts and endowments, and Sélmoros's priests bury paupers. The sailors' confraternities in the ports keep wages, berths and the widows of drowned men, and the guilds look after their own as far as their funds go. A patron feeds his clients in a lean year or loses them. A citizen's widow keeps her citizenship and the dole for life. A widow or an orphan outside it, with no kin, falls to whoever will take them, a patron's household or a temple, and a house short of an heir may adopt (_aipsena_).

The line between citizen and resident decides the rest. A resident's right to sleep within the thrima lasts exactly as long as his employment, and the waiver the courts register for a fee ends with it, so the destitute drift to the outskirts beyond the Clearance. How far the lean-tos have crept onto the Clearance is a quiet measure of how the empire is faring: in confident years the ground is swept gravel, and in lean years the Watch comes through to pull the shanties down or burn them out.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A citizen who is injured or broken keeps the dole, the one thing that cannot be taken away, and the empire has built the army on that promise. A soldier discharged at thirty-five is hale and wants work, and the City Watch recruits straight from the veteran-citizens; one who cannot work still draws the ration and lives in the capital by right. A guildsman is kept by his guild's roll as far as the guild's funds go. A resident who falls ill or loses his post loses his sponsor and so his waiver, and the Watch sees him out. A slave who falls ill is the owner's property, and the owner decides. A person who belongs to none of these depends on a patron's kindness, a temple's bed or the hospices of Morvana.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

The patron's house is the standard place for all three. A client's children eat in it, learn from its tutors and are defended by its guards and by its influence in court. Where a family has no patron the Guild or the legion supplies the substitute. The Imperial Scribal Guild trains its members in a seven-year course, the Tevran Maldor certifies an apprentice in one city as good in another, and the Arcane Order's chapter houses are the finest schools in a region, teaching law, rhetoric, mathematics and history to the children of patrons. The army takes a boy of fifteen and gives him a mess, a trade and, at the end of twenty years, a citizenship. Defense is the legion on the frontier, the Watch in the capital, the guard of a rich house or a tax-farmer's convoy, and the neighbors of the Hilen estates and the forts of Moktur on the margins.

## The House and the Hand

The unit is the household, _zorka_, which is everyone under one roof and one master: wife, children, freedmen, slaves and the clients who come to the door each morning. The master holds a power over his children that ends only at his death, and the word for his hand, _dekazu_, is also the word for the power a master holds over a person: to be "in the hand" of someone is to be his ward, his slave or his wife. Membership therefore follows authority rather than blood. Kin matter and the language has many exact words for them, but a freedman or a client or an adopted heir belongs as fully as a son. A bride is handed from one household to another with a dowry, and a house with no son adopts one.

The ladder of a life follows this. A child is _nurizon_, a neuter noun in the old tongue, a person not yet of age and in law not yet a person, and shares its gender with slaves and tools. A commoner boy takes the legion's oath at fifteen, trains for a year as a Yunar and serves out the twenty years; a noble boy buys a commission as a Kadel at fifteen and an officer's post at seventeen. A woman marries by being handed into another household, and to be in a man's hand is to be his ward, his slave or his wife; a woman of the Guild or a trade may keep her own workshop and seal, as Ailema does. A commoner who survives the twenty years is a citizen at thirty-five, which the empire counts as middle age, and a man's later years are patrons, posts and the dole. A _kurthos_ holds his seat for life, and the old of every order are placed by the same precedence that placed them when young. The last rung is the dying chamber, the last testimony and the mask in the cupboard.

Citizenship does not pass to a commoner's children, who are honorary citizens only while they are his dependents, so a veteran's family renews its place in the body by sending each son through the same door. His wife keeps the citizenship for life, widowhood included. Because the nobility hold it by station, the noble house is the only family the empire treats as a body with a past.

## A Name Struck Out

Every real culture has a blind spot, and the Vylarians' is the unnaming. A people who govern by the roll and the mask answer an embarrassment by striking the name. The Senate alone may give the sentence of _neharlon_ on a person, but the habit runs through the whole record. The chancery struck the commander of the strongest army in the empire from every roll that carried his name when he was crowned in the Khelathi delta, and has not written it again ([[lore-deltaexped|The Delta Expedition]]). The record does not give the name of the _kurthos_ who became the first Orzas, and the Founder's statue carries a smoothed patch where a name should stand. The imperial archive, which held the rolls of the Republic, burned, the cause was never established and the question was never put in public ([[lore-archivefire|The Archive Fire]]). A visitor who remarks on the decay commits a real discourtesy. The remark is true, and that is no defense: a Vylarian has decided what his city is and expects the courtesy of being met there. Among themselves Vylarians are far more caustic about it than any foreigner would dare to be.

The sin sits underneath it. After the Republic conquered Helionis it carved the college of arcane philosophy out of the Epistémion (the Academy of Knowledge) and put it on a register, and when the Arcane Order was chartered the Sekran spent a generation hunting every practitioner who refused it: enrolling those who would be enrolled, imprisoning those who would not and killing the few judged too dangerous. The free Helionite schools that had taught the Arcane Order its craft were closed one by one ([[lore-huntingyears|The Hunting Years]]). The standing instruction for the work has been reissued without a change of wording. Vylarian children are taught the Hunting Years as a cautionary tale about the age before the Arcane Order, not as something done to people.

Thoughtful Vylarians dispute whether the forms are the machine that keeps a shrinking authority working or the veil over its decline, and they argue it from opposite ends. The Guild's senior scholars say the documentary apparatus is more fragile than the throne understands, and that every original in the central archive was written after the fire. The reform factions say the dole and the citizenship are a charge the treasury can neither afford nor safely cut, and the veterans answer that a missed dole is how dynasties fall.

The reflex to law has a blind side of its own. It serves those with standing to use it. Most Orzarans see one magistrate in their lives, the _peltros_ of their ward, and a resident without a patron meets the courts as the place where friends and purses decide.

## Law, Steel and Manners

The one unambiguous gift the empire gave the world is its law, and Vylarians know it: codified, argued, written down, appealable, and applied in principle to a citizen anywhere the empire reaches. A Vylarian's instinct in any dispute is to reach for a legal remedy, and he does it in circumstances where every other people on the continent would reach for a knife or a kinsman. He is often disappointed. He tries anyway. The town of Bralvik has sent the Kastamar admiralty a bill for its burned boats every autumn since the fire, and the admiralty has paid none of them. The town keeps sending it, and conviction that this is the civilized reflex is the single most Vylarian thing about the people.

Vylarian craftsmanship is the best in Ankaris, and **Vylarian Steel** is its emblem: an alloy whose forging is a guarded secret, whose weapons and armor command extraordinary prices, and whose reputation has outlived several of the provinces that used to pay for it. A guild treats a technique as property of the same order as land: inherited, litigated over and defended in court. That is why Vylarian craft is excellent and why it advances slowly.

The manners are formal, precise, and conscious of rank at every moment. A Vylarian establishes precedence early in an encounter and is comfortable once it is settled. A foreigner who will not be placed makes him uneasy, though seldom angry, so the quickest way to an easy evening is to say who you are, whom you serve, and whose guest you are before anyone has to ask. Patronage governs private life as thoroughly as law governs public life. Vylarians are generous hosts, tremendous snobs, and better company than their reputation, provided the question of whose empire it is has been settled to their satisfaction before dinner.
