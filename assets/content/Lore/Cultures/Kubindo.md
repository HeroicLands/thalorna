The **Kubindo** are the court-keepers, one of the four traditions of the [[lore-bikoclt|forest peoples]]. Their name is _kubi_, a spirit-court, with the ending of one who keeps it. Each Kubindo tribe keeps one.

## Where They Live

Kubindo tribes are scattered through the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]], wherever a spirit holds a place: a great tree, a pool, a rock in a river, the range of one beast. Each tribe lives within a few days' walk of its court, and the court is the centre of its world. Two Kubindo tribes can be weeks apart, with Yesoma or Luwando country between them.

## How They Live

The Kubindo forage, hunt and fish as the Yesoma do, and keep no gardens. Their bands move through the country around the court and come back to it at every new moon, when the whole tribe gathers there for a night. They hunt only what their court's spirit allows, and every court forbids something: one forbids the leopard, another the river-fish, another any fire within sight of its tree.

The courts are also where the sick are brought, by the Kubindo and by others. The keeper of a court knows its plants and its manners, and the sick are laid at the court for a night and asked what they did to offend.

## What the Forest Is

To the Kubindo the forest is a household of spirits. Every place in it belongs to an _mbosa_, a spirit, and every spirit has its _mbuyo_, its manners: where to step, what to say, what may be taken and what must be left. The tribe's own court is the household it knows. Every other part of the forest belongs to spirits whose manners the tribe does not know, and a Kubindo moves through it as a guest moves through a stranger's house, quietly and touching little.

A court's mbuyo is long, exact and its own. The keeper of it is the _kelendo_, the eldest _mbeli_ who knows the whole of it, and the kelendo teaches it to any member who asks. The kelendo is not a priest. The keeper has no helpers, no house apart, no share beyond anyone else's, and no voice in the tribe beyond an elder's. When the kelendo dies, the next eldest who knows the whole mbuyo keeps it.

## The Four Standings, and the Leader

A Kubindo child is a _tolu_ until it can keep the whole of the court's mbuyo through a gathering night without a fault. Then it is _wibo_. A spouse from another tribe is a tolu until they can do the same, which takes years.

The spirit makes the _kpaso_, through the _ngobe_, the night-sitting. When the tribe needs a leader, the elders name the members they think fit, and each sits one night alone at the court. In the morning each tells the kelendo what came in the night: a dream, a sound, a beast at the water, or nothing. The kelendo decides whom the spirit answered, and that person is the leader. When the spirit answers no one, the tribe waits for the next moon and tries again.

The kelendo can never be kpaso, and can never be named for the ngobe. The Kubindo hold this rule hardest of all their rules, and tell why: there was a court on a black river whose keeper made himself its leader, and then took helpers to keep its manners for him, and then built a house of stone for its serpent. The serpent died of it, and the dead of that court still turn its wheels. The court is the [[place-drwndzgrt|Drowned Ziggurat]], and every Kubindo child knows the story before it knows its own court's manners.

## Kin

A child belongs to the court where it was born, and its birth-cord is buried there. That is the whole of Kubindo kinship: those whose cords lie at one court are one people, whoever their parents. A Kubindo marries out, to another Kubindo tribe or to another tradition, because a marriage within the court is a marriage within the household. The incoming spouse learns the new court's manners, and their children belong to it.

## The Dead

The dead are buried at the court, beside the tree or the pool, and join the spirit's household. They are _gbulu_, ancestors, and the kelendo speaks to them at every gathering, telling them the tribe's news and asking what the spirit wants. A Kubindo who dies far from the court is carried back to it, however far that is.

## The Other Three

- **The [[lore-luwandoclt|Luwando]]** are kind hosts at a house with no manners. Their river takes anyone, and the Kubindo think a host who asks nothing of a guest is no host. They welcome the Luwando sick and send them home.
- **The [[lore-yesomaclt|Yesoma]]** are careless children who walk through every spirit's house without a greeting. When the hunting fails them, the Kubindo think they earned it.
- **The [[lore-pewayeclt|Pewaye]]** have forgotten the manners. They guard a line between the forest and the outside, and a line has no spirit and asks nothing.

The Kubindo say the sorcerer who leads the [[place-lpdkngcrt|Leopard King's Court]] has done what the drowned court did, and expect the same end for him.

The [[place-slntclrng|Silent Clearing]] belongs to no spirit. Kubindo elders who have been near it say it has no manners at all, and a place with no manners is a place nobody can bargain with.

## What an Outsider Sees

An outsider sees a sacred tree hung with offerings, an old man or woman who speaks to it, and a tribe that will not hunt a certain beast, and writes down a temple, a priest and a cult. Northern scholars have written of spirit-courts with priestly hierarchies for generations. What the outsider does not see is that the old keeper has no more say in the tribe than any elder, that anyone may learn what the keeper knows, and that the tribe keeps the keeper from power on purpose.
