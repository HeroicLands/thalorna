In autumn the scouts come in at a run, the shout _tanshe stuhan_ goes down the camp, and nobody raises a voice again until the herd has gone over the cliff. The Grass People live on the bison, and everything they are follows from the way they take it: on foot, all together, by steering a herd of thousands toward a drop it cannot see. They call themselves the [[affiliation-tenlaya|Tenlaya]], the people of the grass. About 300,000 of them live on the plains at the center of the north and in the dry country beyond the western mountains, some in hunting bands that follow the herds with their dogs, and some in villages of earth lodges on the river bottoms, where the women grow corn. The south calls them _P'enqal Ch'akal_, after their grassland, and its traders the Coyote People, after the coyotes painted on the shields of one of their societies.

## Where They Came From

The Grass People tell two beginnings, and they keep both. The bands say that the people lived under the earth in the dark until the bison found the way out, through the [Breathing Hole]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, a cave in the western hills that still breathes warm air in winter and cold air in summer. The herds went up first, and the people followed them into the light, and have been following them since. The villages say that the people walked up the rivers from the east carrying seed corn, which the women of the first villages had been given in a dream, and that they planted it on the first river bottom where the soil was soft enough to dig with a bone.

Both stories end the same way, with one people who lived two ways. A band and a village speak the same tongue, smoke the same pipe and dance the same summer dances, and most families have kin in both. A band son marries into a village and lives in his wife's lodge; a village family whose fields fail goes out to the bands for a winter, and sometimes stays.

The rest of their history is the history of the herds and the corn, and of the bargain between them. Every year-robe on the plains carries the same picture somewhere in its spiral, a ditch with flames above it, and every band and every village can tell what it means: the [Winter of the Ditch]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, told below, when the bands burned a village of their own people.

## The Open Hand

**Good is the open hand: the meat shared, the robe given, the enemy touched with a bare hand in front of witnesses. Evil is the closed hand: the corn kept back, the meat hidden, the drive spoiled for one man's supper.** The Grass People hold that the herds come to a people who share them. A hunter who hides meat from a hungry lodge has stolen from the drive that killed it, and the drive belonged to everyone who crouched behind the cairns.

This belief governs the smallest manners. Anyone who walks into a lodge while food is cooking is fed, and a guest who refuses food has refused the lodge. A family that kills a bison alone in a lean month carries the meat to the lodges with the least before it carries any home. A person who boasts of what they own is laughed at; a person who boasts of what they gave away is listened to, and expected to give again.

Courage is the other half of the good. Killing an enemy is ordinary, and earns little. Touching a living enemy with a hand or a stick and coming away alive is a coup (_sketa_), and a coup counted in front of witnesses is the greatest honor a fighter can earn. The worst wrongs are the opposite of both: to kill one of the people, which no gift can wholly mend, and to start the herd early, which takes the winter's meat from everyone at once.

## A Name Worth Saying

Success is a name worth saying aloud. A person's standing is what they have done, given away and dared, told and retold, and it can be lost. A man rises by coups, by meat given, by drives led and by the young men who choose to follow him on a raid. A woman rises by the robes she has tanned and quilled, the lodge she has raised, the feasts she has given and the children she has kept alive through bad winters, and her quillwork is counted the way a man's coups are. The Grass People keep the count: at a giveaway a speaker recites, deed by deed, why the family is honoring someone.

Failure is the closed hand and the turned back. A man who hoards meat, flees a fight or bullies his band's lodges loses standing slowly and in public, and the young men stop following him. At the bottom is the _tewiya_, the outcast, who has killed one of the people or spoiled a drive and will not mend it, and who eats with the dogs at the edge of the camp because no lodge will feed him.

The Grass People value most the herd, the open hand, courage, kin and the right to leave. Any family may strike its lodge and join another band, and a chief who loses too many of them has stopped being one. They value least hoarding, bossiness and command that lasts longer than the work it was given for. A trader from the settled nations who calls a hunt chief a king is laughed at.

## The Man Admired

[Keshunwinte]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Cairn band is a hunter of the eastern grass, and the bands name him when they say what a man should be. He was born Kuntonwiwan, Stands in Thunder, and earned the name he carries now, Gives Robes, in his twentieth winter, when he came home from his first raid with four robes taken from a One Fire town and gave all four away before he reached his mother's lodge. He has counted seven coups since. He has been hunt chief of his band twice, and both times he laid the office down on the day the season ended, which the bands admire more than either drive.

The bands admire three things in him, and all three are the open hand. He gives first, and keeps nothing a widow lacks. He fights in front, and has never been seen to strike a man who was already down. He commands only while the work lasts. His cost is plain to everyone: he is the poorest man in his band. His wife has quilled three robes for him and seen two of them given away, and she says so, in public, at every giveaway he holds.

## The Woman Admired

[Moshentiyu]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Heart of the Lodge, is a lodge owner (_moshenka_) of [[place-tansheyawa|Tanshe Yawa]], the largest of the river villages, and the villages name her when they say what a woman should be. She raised her own earth lodge, cutting and setting its four great posts with her sisters, and she has quilled more robes than any woman of the village. Four winters ago, when the herds went west and three bands came to Tanshe Yawa starving in the snow, she opened her cache pits to them in midwinter, long after the trading, when the Corn Pipe bound nobody, and fed a hundred people through the worst of the winter. Her lodge planted that spring from borrowed seed.

Her admiration is of another kind from Keshunwinte's. He gives what he takes from the herd and the enemy, and it is gone by evening. She gives what the village has grown and kept, and what she gives is next year's planting. The villages say that she remembered what the cache pits are for, and they say it as a reproach to the lodges that kept theirs shut that winter. Her cost is the seed. Her daughters planted late, the corn came in thin, and Moshentiyu spent the next autumn trading robes she had quilled for seed corn.

## What the Robes Keep

The Grass People write nothing down, and what holds them together is painted, wrapped, stacked or remembered.

- **The year-robes** (_skewa keshun_) are the people's history. Each band's year-robe keeper paints one small picture for each winter in a spiral out from the center of a robe, and names the winter by it: the winter the river froze to the bottom, the winter a great man died, the Winter of the Ditch. A keeper can recite the whole spiral in order, and an argument about when something happened is settled by counting pictures.
- **The bundles** (_chekan_) are the holy things of a village or a band, wrapped in hide: a pipe, a bison skull, an ear of corn, the skins of birds, and the songs that go with each. A bundle keeper opens it for the drives and the plantings, and a village whose bundle is lost or stolen counts itself in danger until it is recovered.
- **The pipe** (_opoya_) binds a promise and makes kin. The [Corn Pipe]{.name data-type="miscgear"} of Tanshe Yawa, which binds the bands and the villages to the peace of the autumn trading, is told below.
- **The drive lanes** are lines of stone cairns (_wehi_) running for miles across the plains toward the cliffs and the pounds. They are older than any band. Every band that uses a lane restacks its fallen cairns, and nobody builds a new one.
- **The four posts** of every earth lodge stand for the four quarters, and the women who set them sing a song for each.
- **The songs** of the societies and the bundles are the people's law, and are taught by the keepers and repeated without change.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is the first bite and the first smoke. A woman cooking sets the first piece of meat aside for the spirits before anyone eats, and a man lighting a pipe offers it to the sky, the earth and the four quarters before he draws on it. Nobody steps over a robe, a weapon or a sleeping person, and nobody wastes a bison: the meat is eaten or dried, the hide becomes a robe or a lodge cover, the bones become hoes, scrapers and needles, and the sinew becomes thread and bowstring.

Before anything that matters, the people sweat. A sweat lodge (_pishan_) is a low frame of bent willows covered with robes, and water poured on stones heated in a fire outside fills it with steam. A hunter sweats before a drive, a war leader before a raid, and a family before it opens a bundle. A young man, and now and then a young woman, goes up a hill alone to fast for four days and wait for a vision (_shiyota_). What comes, an animal, a voice or a song, is that person's helper for life, and is painted on their shield and lodge cover.

## The Round of the Year

The year turns on the herds and the corn, and the band and the village keep different halves of it.

- **The spring planting** is the villages' first work. The women clear the fields on the river bottoms, dig with hoes of bison bone (_skapo_), and plant corn, beans, squash and sunflowers, while the old women sing to the seed. It is for waking the soil, and every village keeps it.
- **The summer gathering** (_hekawa_) brings the bands of a whole region together in one great camp circle, at a place their year-robes name. The societies dance, young men choose societies and the societies choose them, marriages are made, and the pledge dance runs for four days around a cottonwood pole: men who made a vow in danger keep it by fasting and dancing until they fall. It is for renewing the people and the herds together, and for settling the year's quarrels between bands.
- **The summer hunt** empties the villages. Once the corn is hoed, most of a village goes out to a hunting camp on the grass with its own dogs and hide lodges, and comes home for the green corn. It is for village meat, and for reminding the villages how the bands live.
- **The autumn drive** is the greatest work of the year, when the hides are thick and the meat will keep. It is told below.
- **The autumn trading** brings the bands in to the villages with meat, fat, hides and robes, to trade them for corn, beans, squash, sunflower cakes and the tobacco that the village old men grow for every pipe on the plains. The Corn Pipe is smoked at its opening. It is for keeping band and village one people.
- **The winter dances** fill the earth lodges. When the snow is deep, the men of a village dance in bison heads and robes to call the herds close to the river, and the bands, wintering in the sheltered valleys, tell stories and paint the year-robe at the first thaw. It is for bringing the herds back.

## The Drive

Everything else the Grass People do is arranged around the autumn drive (_yuchan_). The band's council names a hunt chief (_yuchanka_) for the season, the bundle keeper sings over the bundle, and for days the band eases a herd toward a drive lane by moving behind it and letting it drift into the grass upwind. Young men disguised in bison calf skins wander out in front, bawling like lost calves, and the herd follows them. When it enters the lane, the people hidden behind the cairns stand up in turn and wave robes, and the herd runs between the lines of cairns, faster and faster, toward a cliff (_toshe_) or a corral of logs and brush. A good drive kills hundreds of animals in an hour, and the women butcher and dry for weeks.

The warrior societies (_yewan_) police the drive. While a herd is being steered, nobody hunts alone, and a man who goes out after his own supper and starts the herd early has spoiled the winter for everyone. The society on duty breaks his bow, cuts his lodge cover to pieces and kills his dogs if he fights it. If he takes the punishment quietly, the same society gives him a new bow and a new cover after the drive, and the matter is closed.

## Praise, Mockery and the Dogs

Reward is a name and the people's regard. A boy who brings his first meat home is praised at a feast, and the meat is given away. A young man who counts his first coup takes a new name and the right to wear an eagle feather, and a society may ask him to join. A woman whose quilling is fine enough joins the women's society of quillers and is honored at its feasts. A family honors its own at a giveaway (_wokashe_), where it gives away robes, food and lodge goods while a speaker recites why.

Punishment runs from laughter to the dogs. The first and most common is teasing by a joking relative: a sister-in-law or a cousin who may, by custom, mock a man to his face for his faults, and does, at the top of her voice. The second is a society's punishment for spoiling a drive, given in front of the camp and closed when it is accepted. The third, for a killing among the people, is a payment: the killer's family sends robes and goods to the dead person's family with a pipe, and if the pipe is smoked the matter ends. If it is refused, the dead man's kin may take their revenge, and a feud runs. The last is to be made _tewiya_, an outcast, for four winters or for good. An outcast may follow the band at a distance, but no lodge feeds him and no society will have him.

## Councils and Bundles

The Grass People have no government that lasts longer than its work. A band is governed by a council of its respected men and women, who name a hunt chief for the drives and accept a war leader (_yashenka_) for a raid, and both lose their authority when the season or the raid ends. A village is governed by its council and by its lodge owners (_moshenka_), the women who own the earth lodges and the fields and decide who eats from the cache pits. Each village chooses a village speaker (_hoyanka_) to bargain with the bands at the autumn trading.

Religion has keepers and no rulers. A bundle keeper (_chekanka_) holds a village's or a band's bundle and its songs, a year-robe keeper (_keshunka_) holds its history, and a person who has had a strong vision may doctor the sick or find the herds. Their word carries weight in council, and none of them commands. When the bundle keeper says the signs are wrong for a drive and the hunt chief wants to go, the drive waits, because a hunt chief who drives against the bundle and fails is finished.

Nobody keeps records except on the robes. The year-robe keeper keeps the years, the society songs keep the law, and the council remembers what it decided. Disputes inside a band go to the council and are settled with gifts; disputes between bands go to the summer gathering; disputes between a band and a village go to the autumn trading, under the Corn Pipe. Nobody collects anything. After a drive the hunt chief divides the meat by lodges, and every lodge's share is the same, the widows' first.

## The Spirit Road

The dead walk the Spirit Road, the band of pale light across the night sky, toward the camp of the dead, where the herds never fail and every lodge is full. Where the road forks, the dead are met by everything that was given away in their name, and they are carried on it to the camp. A person in whose name nothing was given walks the short fork alone, and the Grass People do not say where it goes.

That belief settles an ordinary day more than any law does. Whatever a person gives is laid up for their walk, and so a generous life is a safe death. When someone dies, the family gives away nearly everything the dead person owned, and much of its own, before the body is laid out. The closest kin cut a lock of the dead person's hair and wrap it in a soul bundle (_nashe chekan_), which they keep for a year in a place of its own in the lodge, and feed at every meal. The keepers may not quarrel, fight or turn anyone away while they keep it. At the end of the year the family releases the soul with the greatest giveaway it can manage, and gives away everything it has, lodge goods, robes and food, down to the last bowl.

So a family that has lost someone is poor, and the band refills it. The meat, robes and lodge goods given at a release go to the band's poorest first, and within a season the people who received them have helped the mourning family to a new lodge cover. Inheritance is simple, because there is almost nothing left to inherit. The earth lodges and the fields of the villages belong to the women and pass to their daughters; a man's weapons and shield go onto the scaffold with him or are given away.

The bands lay their dead on scaffolds (_wechan_) on high ground, wrapped in their best robe, and leave them to the sky. The villages lay theirs on scaffolds too, and when the scaffolds fall, the women gather the skulls and set them in a ring on the prairie outside the village.

## Feeding the Dead

The living reach the dead through the soul bundle and the ring. A soul bundle is wrapped by a holy man or woman who knows the songs, and the family pays for the wrapping in robes; the year of keeping costs the family its quarrels, its raids and finally everything it owns. A village woman takes food to the ring of skulls, sits beside the skull of her mother or her child, and talks to it about her day, and the women of the villages say that the skulls listen.

The dead reach the living in dreams and visions. A grandfather seen in a dream may give a name or a song, and a name given that way is held in high regard. A soul that is never released wanders around the camp, and the Grass People hold that a wandering soul makes children sick. A family that cannot afford a release borrows from the band, and the band always lends.

## What a Person Owes

What a person owes comes in a fixed order, and children learn it by heart: an open hand first, because the herds depend on it; then obedience to the society while a drive is on; then a share of every kill to the lodges that need it; then a place in the fight when the camp is attacked; then revenge for kin, or a pipe smoked in place of it; then a giveaway at every death and every honor in the family.

In return a person is owed what the band and the village give. Anyone of the people is owed a lodge's food when they walk in hungry, an equal share of every drive's meat, a place in the camp circle, help striking and carrying a lodge, revenge if they are killed, and a scaffold, a soul bundle and a release when they die.

## The Weak

The open hand is the first resort, and the drive's share rule the second. A widow keeps her lodge and her children, and her lodge takes the first share of every drive; in a village she keeps her lodge and fields as well, because they were always hers. A widower goes back to his mother's or sister's lodge. An orphan is never without a mother, because the Grass People call a mother's sisters mothers and a father's brothers fathers, and one of them takes the child in as a matter of course. Feeding the poor is a duty, and it is also the road to a name: a man who wants to be followed feeds the people nobody else will.

A stranger who walks into a camp is fed once, and then asked who he is. A stranger with no kin among the Grass People and no pipe of the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] is anyone's to kill or to take captive. A captive child is usually adopted, and grows up as one of the people.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot hunt or carry is kept by the lodge, and the band's dogs and travois carry them when it moves. A hunter who has lost the use of a leg makes arrows, a crippled warrior teaches boys the bow, and a blind elder keeps songs. A woman who cannot walk far tans and quills seated at the lodge door, and her work is counted the same as anyone's. Year-robe keepers are often men whose hunting days are over.

The hard case is the old person who cannot keep up when a band moves. Some ask to be left at a sheltered camp with food and a fire, and the band honors the request. More often a band family takes an old parent in to a village where it has kin, and the old live out their lives in an earth lodge beside the corn. This is the most common reason a band keeps its ties to a village.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the lodge, and the lodge belongs to the woman who made it: a band wife makes and owns the hide lodge (_keyan_), and a village woman raises and owns the earth lodge (_moshen_). Teaching is the grandparents' and the uncles': a child learns the songs and the stories from its grandparents, a boy learns the bow from his mother's brother, a girl learns tanning and quilling from her mother's sisters, and a young man learns to fight from the society that takes him.

Defense is the societies. They guard the camp at night, lead the counter-raid when a band is struck, and scout ahead of a band on the march. A village defends itself behind its ditch and palisade, with bastions at the corners, and every village on the river bottoms has deepened its ditch and doubled its palisade since the Winter of the Ditch. The Grass People fight on foot with bows, stone-headed arrows, clubs and shields of thick bull hide, and they move faster across the grass than any town can muster. In the compact they are the scouts and the mobile fighters, and they have the least patience of any nation with the settled nations' talk.

## The Lodge and the Band

The household is a lodge: a woman, her husband, their children and often her mother and an unmarried sister. In a village, the lodges of a clan's women stand together, the clan comes from the mother, and a man marries into his wife's lodge and works her fields while his own clan stays his mother's. In a band, kin are counted on both sides, a new husband lives with his wife's family for the first year or two, and a band is a cluster of related lodges that hunt and camp together. A family belongs to a band by moving with it.

The age ladder is plain. A child is named on its fourth day and carried, then set to gathering wood and minding the dogs. A boy shoots at birds and then at rabbits, and his first meat is given away at a feast. At fifteen or so a young man fasts for his vision, follows a raid as a water carrier, and waits for his first coup; a girl learns tanning and quilling, and at her first blood spends four days apart and comes out a woman. Young adults marry, join a society or a women's society, and raise lodges. Adults hunt, farm, fight and give. The old sit in council, keep the bundles, the songs and the year-robe, and tease the young. The dying give away what is left and sing their death songs. The dead walk the Spirit Road, carried by what was given.

## The Winter of the Ditch

The sin the Grass People committed is the Winter of the Ditch. It came at the end of a run of dry years, generations ago, when the grass failed, the herds went far to the west and the bands went hungry for three winters running. The villages were hungry too, and in the third autumn the village now called the [Burned Village]{.name data-type="place/site"} kept its corn in its cache pits and refused the bands at the trading. The bands camped outside it for a month. In the first deep snow, the societies of four bands went over its ditch at night, burned its lodges and killed nearly everyone inside, close to five hundred people, men, women and children, and took the corn. They left the dead in the ditch, unburied and unreleased.

The Grass People tell it as two wrongs in one winter, and as the worst they know: the village had closed its hand, and the bands had killed their own people. [Loshitayu]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Corn Dream, was a girl of the burned village, taken captive that night and adopted into one of the bands that burned it. She grew up in the band, married a society head, and when she was a grown woman she walked to the river villages carrying her band's pipe and offered it to every village speaker in turn. The peace she made is the autumn trading as it is kept now: the bands come in, a society keeps the peace of the trading camp, and every village opens its cache pits to the hungry before it sets a price. Its pipe is the Corn Pipe, kept at Tanshe Yawa, and smoked at the opening of every trading.

The blind spot the Grass People argue over is the raid. A man's standing rests on coups and gifts, and the surest way to both is a raid. A young man with no coup has no name, no society and few prospects of a wife, and so the young men raid: the western towns of the [[affiliation-tokchiaska|One Fire]], the western villages of the [[affiliation-osetakona|Maple People]], and other bands of their own people. The raiders come home with names. The towns they raid strike back, and they strike back at what does not move: the river villages, whose lodges and fields stand where they stood. Some elders say that the open hand needs something to give, and that a people without raids would be a people without names. The lodge owners of the villages answer that the bands make the names and the villages pay for them, and ask how many coups the Winter of the Ditch was worth. [Kanwesawi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Coyote Runs, the war leader killed at the [Red Quarry]{.name data-type="place/site"} by a One Fire noble, is remembered as a hero by the bands. The villages remember the One Fire's raids that came after his death.
