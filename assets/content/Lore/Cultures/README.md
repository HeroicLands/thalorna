# Writing a culture note

A culture note answers _who are these people_ — not what they wear or where they
live, which belong to the region notes, but how they orient a life.

`Khelathi.md` is the worked example. It was written by answering the questions
below in order, and every answer was checked against what the corpus already
said.

## The questions

Answer all of them. A note that skips a section is a culture nobody can play.

### Values

1. How does this culture define good and evil?
2. How does it define social success and failure?
3. What does it most value? What does it least value?

### People

4. Describe a male paragon — not the highest rank, but the most admired. What
   are the characteristics of the role model?
5. Describe a female paragon, on the same terms. She should be admirable in her
   own way rather than a lesser version of the male one.

### Institutions

6. What are its cultural artifacts, religious or social? The equivalents of a
   decalogue, a charter, a founding document, a standard kept in a temple.
7. What religious practices are central? What does an ordinary person actually
   do, and how often?
8. What festivals and holidays matter, and what is each one _for_?
9. What are the rewards and the punishments, and in what order of severity?

### Authority and the afterlife

10. Where does civil authority end and religious authority begin? Are they two
    institutions, one wearing two hats, or rivals? Who tries a case, who keeps
    the records, who collects, and who decides when those answers conflict?
11. What happens after death, and — the question that actually matters — **what
    does that belief make people do on an ordinary Tuesday?** An afterlife that
    changes no behaviour is decoration. Say how it reaches ethics, law,
    inheritance, burial, and what a person spends money on.
12. Can the living affect the dead, or the dead the living? If so, through whom,
    and what does that institution charge?

### The social contract

13. What does a person owe, and what are they owed in return? State both sides
    plainly enough to recite.
14. How are the weak treated — the homeless, the destitute, orphans, widows?
    Does anyone take care of them, and is it a duty or a kindness?
15. What happens to someone injured or incapacitated who cannot meet their
    obligations but goes on living? Who feeds them?
16. Who provides shelter, teaching and defence when a family cannot?
17. How is the family organised? Is the nuclear or the extended family the unit,
    and what defines membership?

## How to answer them

**Derive from the corpus, do not invent over it.** The strongest answers explain
something already written. If the region notes describe an institution and the
culture note explains why it exists, both get stronger. If an answer contradicts
a note somebody wrote, the contradiction is the finding.

**Write for the living.** A culture is a way of orienting a life, and most of a
life is not the deathbed or the crisis. If every answer is about extremes, the
note describes a death cult rather than a people. Say what an ordinary day and
an ordinary year look like.

**Cover the whole age ladder.** Children, the unestablished young, adults, the
old, the dying. A person should be able to tell which rung they are on and what
to expect from it.

**Make it workable.** Real societies survive scarcity, illness and bad years. An
answer that only works when everyone is prosperous and healthy is not an answer.

**Give it heroes and a sin it committed.** A culture with institutions but no
admired figures reads as an administration. A culture with no crime in its own
past reads as propaganda.

**State what the culture gets wrong.** Every real system has a blind spot its own
thoughtful members argue about. Naming it is what makes the rest credible.

**Keep the afterlife load-bearing.** The test for question 11 is whether a
reader can predict a Tuesday from it. In `Khelathi.md` the belief that the dead
must work and eat is why tombs hold tools, why offerings are payments rather
than remembrance, why grave robbery is hunted across generations, and why a
dying man decides aloud what debts he calls in and what he takes with him. That
is what load-bearing looks like.
