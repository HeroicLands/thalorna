**The Honey Owed Under the Water** is the tale the captains of the [visizuni]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage" lang=taru}, a shipping house of [[place-sazumun2|Sazumun]], tell every new hand on the night before the hand's first sailing with the house. It is told on the quay or in the house's own tavern by the oldest captain in port, with the house's book open on the table at the line it explains, and the telling below is the one the house's captains give at Sazumun. The tale belongs to the house, and other houses tell it with their own founder in it, or tell it against the Visizuni as the story of a house that pays a fish. The Visizuni do not argue the point. Every year one of their ships throws a sealed jar of honey over the side at a place the house does not name, and the book carries the payment under the oldest open line in it: _To the one under the water, one jar of honey._

The tale's hero is [azara visizuni]{.name data-type="being/npc" lang=taru}, a captain who owned her hull "in the days of the _therkoi_ (governors)", and her ship is the _[visizan]{.name data-type="vehicle" lang=taru}_, Running with the Tide. A storm she refuses to bargain with drives her past the last chart of the [Academy of the Tides]{.name data-type="affiliation"}. She meets [the one under the water]{.name data-type="being" lang=en} (_hitusum_, the one who lies hidden), a thing the size of a reef that swallows ships, and buys passage from it forever for a jar of honey a year. She then comes to [the Black Island]{.name data-type="place" lang=en}, whose king has never been refused, and sells him salt. Captains tell it to teach a new hand what a bargain is, what a house owes and how far a name has to reach. The other houses laugh at the jar, and the Visizuni count among the houses that have never missed a payment.

: The Honey Owed Under the Water {#tale}

Sit. Drink. No, the first cup is the house's, and so is the second, and after the third you can pay for your own. You sail in the morning, so you are owed the story tonight, and the house pays its debts. That is the first thing you will learn tonight and the last.

Look at the book. Not that line, that is the rope-walk, everybody owes the rope-walk. Go up. Further up, past the war, past the year the old counting-room burned and the clerks carried the first book out of the fire in their coats. Up to the top of the first page, in the oldest hand, where the ink has gone the color of tea. Read it out. _To the one under the water, one jar of honey._ And under it, every year, one word in a different hand. _Paid._ Count them if you like. My grandmother counted them once and stopped at three hundred because the lamp went out.

Now I will tell you who the one under the water is, and why we pay it, and you can decide for yourself whether I am telling you the truth. Everybody on this quay decides for himself. That is what a quay is for.

In the days of the _therkoi_, when there was a governor in Besikar and a customs house on every quay that answered to him, there was a captain of this house named Azara Visizuni. She owned her hull. The hull was the _Visizan_, which means running with the tide, and she was not large: sixty feet, one mast, a crew of fourteen and a cook. Azara bought her with a loan from her mother's brother and paid him back in four years, which was a year early, and he never forgave her for it, because he had wanted to be asked for more time.

That summer she loaded salt. Salt from the pans below Sazumun, white salt, packed in jars and the jars packed in straw, the whole hold of it, for a buyer in the west who had sent his seal ahead and his money behind. Salt is a good cargo and a bad one. It does not rot, and it does not burn, and nobody steals it. It is heavy, and it eats the hold, and if the sea gets into it you are carrying brine to a man who ordered salt. Azara knew all that. She loaded it anyway, because the price was good and the buyer's seal was better.

She was eleven days out when the storm found her.

You will meet a storm, and you will pray, and that is right. Pray to the Thunderer. Give him the first portion, every meal, the way the shrine in the deckhouse wants it. But I will tell you what Azara did, and you can do as you like. When the sky went green and the swell stood up like a wall of the harbor and the first of it came over the bow, the cook went down on his knees by the shrine and began to promise. He promised the Thunderer a tenth of the voyage. He promised him a bull. He promised him his own first son, who was not born yet, and his first son's first son.

Azara took him by the collar and lifted him off his knees.

"Not on my hull," she said. "You do not promise him what my house will have to pay. Bail."

You know why. A vow to the Thunderer binds, and it binds the house that made it, and it binds the hull you stood on when you made it, and your children, and theirs. A frightened man in a storm will promise anything, and the god keeps it all, and the man's grandchildren are still paying a bull a year to the hill at Besikar for a storm nobody remembers. Azara would not have it. She said a storm is weather. She said you bail weather. You do not sign with it.

So they bailed. They bailed for three days with the sea coming over the rail and the mast groaning in its step. They threw the deck cargo over, the water jars and the spare spars, and they did not throw the salt, because the salt was the buyer's and it was sealed. On the fourth morning the wind dropped, and the sea lay down, and Azara came up out of the hold where she had been bailing with the rest and looked at the sky. She did not know any of it.

There is a chart in the Academy at Bazunel that goes further west and south than any chart in the world, and at its edge, where the coast stops, the scholars of the tides have written: _Here we have no soundings._ Azara had a copy of that chart. She laid it on the deck and looked at the sun and looked at the chart and looked at the sun again, and she knew she was past the place where it said _Here we have no soundings_, and well past it, and still going.

That is the edge. Every captain of this house has a copy of that chart, and you will be shown it, and you will be shown the edge. Nobody will ask you to go past it. Azara did not ask to go past it either. The storm did not ask her.

They sailed east, toward home, the way you would. On the second day they came to a passage between two long reefs, white water on either hand and a channel between, dark blue and wide and smooth. A good channel. Azara did not like it. She said a channel that smooth between two reefs that rough is a channel something is keeping smooth. She sent the boy up the mast and she put the lead over the side, and the lead went down, and down, and found nothing, and then found something soft.

The boy at the masthead said, "Captain, the reef is moving."

It was not a reef. It rose up out of the channel under the bow, and the sea ran off its back in sheets, and it was gray and green and crusted the way the bottom of a hull is crusted after a long voyage, and it was as long as a breakwater. It had an eye. One of its eyes came up out of the water beside the rail, and it was as big as a cartwheel and as yellow as a lamp.

The cook went down on his knees again. Azara did not stop him this time. There was nobody to promise to that she had not already refused.

The thing opened its mouth, and its mouth was the passage. You understand me. The channel between the reefs was its mouth, open, and every ship that took the smooth water sailed in.

"Wait," said Azara.

It waited. That is the first thing she learned about it, and she said it was the most important. It waited. A thing that waits when you say wait is a thing that can be talked to, and a thing that can be talked to can be sold something.

"You are going to eat my ship," she said.

The voice came up out of the water, all around the hull at once, the way the swell comes. "Yes," it said. "Ships come, and I eat them. That is the passage."

"How many ships come?"

It thought about it. She said you could see it thinking. The eye half closed. "One," it said. "Sometimes one in a year. Sometimes one in ten. The sea brings them."

"And they are good?"

"They are wood," it said, "and rope, and men, and whatever is in them. They are something. Down here there is nothing. There is salt and dark and the cold that comes up from the bottom. A ship is something."

Azara said that was when she knew she had a market. A buyer who tells you he has nothing has told you his price is high.

"What is in this ship," the thing said, "is salt. I can smell it through the planks. I have salt. The whole sea is salt. Your ship is a ship of the one thing I have."

"Then I will not sell you the salt," Azara said. "I will sell you something you do not have."

She went below. The crew thought she had gone to hide, and the cook began to cry. She came back up with the cook's own honey jar, the one he kept for the honey cakes for the feast at the end of the voyage, sealed with wax and wrapped in cloth, and she broke the seal on the rail in front of the eye.

"Smell that," she said.

The eye opened all the way. It was the first time, she said, she saw it surprised.

"What is it?"

"It is the opposite of the sea," Azara said. "It is what the bees make out of the hills. Open your mouth. Not all the way."

She poured it in. The whole jar. The cook watched the cakes he was never going to bake go down a passage the width of a harbor mouth, and he stopped crying out of pure grief.

The thing shut its mouth. The reefs on either side of the channel came together a little, and the white water quieted. For a long time it said nothing at all.

Then it said, "More."

"No," said Azara. "That was the sample. Nobody gets the cargo for the sample."

You see what she did. She had pushed back, and a thing that has been pushed back has to name a price or go hungry. It looked at her with its yellow eye for the length of a tide turning, and she sat down on the hatch cover and looked back at it, and the crew stood there with their hands full of nothing and waited to be eaten.

"Your ship," it said at last. "Your ship, and the sweet."

"You can have the ship," said Azara. "Once. Then you will have eaten it, and you will have one meal of wood and men, and a taste of the sweet that you will never taste again, because the next ship that comes in ten years will be carrying nails. Or you can let this ship go, and I will bring you a jar of the sweet every year. Every year. As long as there are bees in the hills and my house to carry it."

"You will not come back," the thing said. "Ships do not come back. They come once and I eat them, or they come once and they get away, and they never come back."

"I will come back," said Azara. "And when I am dead my house will come back."

"What is a house?"

She had to explain it. She said it was the longest she ever haggled in her life, and she haggled with the _therkos_'s own collector. She explained a house to a thing that had lived alone at the bottom of a channel since the islands came up out of the sea. She explained a mother and a daughter and a counting-room. She explained a name. She explained that when she was dead her name would still be good, if her house kept it good, and that a debt written in her house's book was owed by every captain who would ever sail under the house's seal. The thing listened. It had nothing else to do.

"Who stands for you," it said at last, "when you are dead?"

That is what they all ask. Remember that. Even a thing at the bottom of the sea asks who will stand for you.

"My house," said Azara. "Visizuni. The company of the tide. That is the name you hold. Every ship that carries that name past this passage carries your jar, and every ship that carries that name goes through. Not just this one. All of them. Forever."

"Forever," said the thing, and its eye half closed. You could see it doing the reckoning. A jar a year, forever, against one ship. A thing that lives forever likes the word forever. It thinks it is getting the better of you.

"Forever," Azara said, and held out her hand over the rail.

It did not have a hand. It put the tip of something against her palm, cold and rough as a hull bottom, and that was the clasp. Then it sank. The reefs drew back and the channel lay smooth again, and the _Visizan_ sailed through it in the afternoon with every man aboard looking down into the water and nobody saying a word, until the cook said, "That was my honey."

"Put it on my account," said Azara.

It is on the account. Go back and look. The first line is the jar. The second line, in the same old hand, is _To the cook, one jar of honey_, and it is crossed through and marked _paid_, and that one was paid the same week they came home.

Now listen, because there is a second part, and the second part is why we are rich.

The thing under the water was pleased with its bargain. A pleased counterparty tells you things. As it sank it said to her, "There is an island east of here. Its king takes ships too. Do not go there."

Of course she went there. She had a hold full of salt, fourteen men, no water, and no cook's honey. She went where there was land.

The island was black. Black rock, black sand on the beaches, black cliffs, and above the cliffs a green hill covered in thyme, so that the wind off it smelled like a kitchen. There was a harbor, a good one, deep, with a chain across the mouth and a wall around the water, and inside the harbor there were ships. A great many ships. Ships from everywhere, she said, ships with no masts, ships with their sails rotting on the yards, ships so old she had only seen their like carved on tombs. They were all tied to the wall and none of them was going anywhere.

She did not go in. She did what the woman of the hulls did. She anchored outside the harbor mouth in twenty fathoms, in the lee of the chain, and put out her stone, and waited.

In the morning a boat came out with a man in it in a gold coat. He stood up in the bow and called across the water.

"Whatever comes into the harbor of the king is the king's," he called. "That is the due. Come in, and give the king what is his."

"I have not come in," Azara called back. "I am outside your harbor. Outside your harbor is the sea, and the sea is nobody's."

That is true, by the way. You can check it with any notary on this quay. The sea is the one thing that does not belong to anyone.

The man in the gold coat did not know what to say. He rowed back in. In the afternoon he came out again with a bigger boat and more men and a chair in the bow, and in the chair was the king.

He was old, she said. Old and fat and very sad. He wore so many rings that he could not close his hands, and his crown was a ship's figurehead with the face sawn off and a gold band nailed round it. He looked up at Azara on her rail and said: "Every ship gives the king what is his."

"Name the price," said Azara.

"The price is everything."

"Then no," said Azara.

The king looked at her the way the thing under the water had looked at her. Nobody, she said, had ever said no to him. Every captain the storms had brought there had been frightened and thirsty and lost, and had come into the harbor and given everything, and had never been let go, because a captain who has given everything has nothing to sail home with. The king had a harbor full of ships and storehouses full of cargoes and he had never in his life heard a refusal.

You know what a refusal is. You will learn it tomorrow on the quay. A refusal is not the end of a bargain. A refusal is the beginning of one. The king did not know that, because nobody had ever begun a bargain with him. So Azara taught him. She came down off the rail into his boat, which was a risk, and sat on the thwart across from his chair, and said, "Now we sit. And you give me something to drink. And then we argue."

He gave her something to drink. It was wine made from thyme honey, she said, and it was terrible. They argued until dark.

This is what she learned, arguing. The king's island had everything a king could want except anybody to sell to. The fish came into his bay so thick you could walk on them, and he could not keep them, because he had no salt, and the sun on the black rock rotted a catch in a day. His storehouses were full of the cargoes of every ship the storms had brought, and he could not eat silk, and he could not eat timber, and he could not sell any of it, because no ship ever left his harbor to carry it, because he took every ship. And his hill was covered in bees, and the bees made honey from the thyme, and the honey filled every jar on the island, and there was nothing to do with it but make terrible wine.

So she sold him the salt.

She did not give it to him. She sold it. She opened at a price so high that his man in the gold coat laughed aloud, and the king, who had never in his life heard a price, did not laugh, he pushed it back, the way she had shown him, and she pushed his back, and they went at it until the lamps were lit in the boat. In the end she sold him the whole hold of salt for water, food, a pilot who knew the reefs, and honey. Forty jars of thyme honey for the hold of salt, which on this quay would be a robbery and on that island was the best bargain the king ever made, because he salted his fish that winter for the first time in his life and his people ate all through the dark months. He cried, she said. Not in front of his men. In front of her.

But the buyer in the west, you say. The salt was the buyer's, and it was sealed.

Yes. She wrote to the buyer when she came home, and she paid him back his money and the price of the salt at the Sazumun rate and something for his trouble, out of the forty jars, and he was content. She would not have done it any other way. A bargain broken is theft, and a bargain broken by a storm is still broken; you pay for it out of what the storm gave you instead.

There was one more thing the king wanted, and he asked for it at the end, when the lamps were nearly out.

"Tell them," he said. "Tell every harbor that the king of this island keeps a bargain. So that ships come to sell and not to be taken. So that I am something besides the end of the world."

He was asking for a name, you see. He had a harbor and a crown and forty storehouses, and he had no name a lender would read, and somewhere in that long argument he had understood what that meant.

"I will tell every harbor," said Azara.

They clasped. He could not close his hand for the rings, so she closed it for him.

And she did tell every harbor. She told Sazumun and Besikar and Kudumel. She told Helionis and the Khelathi quays. She stood on the steps of the customs house at Sazumun in front of the _therkos_'s own collector and said in a loud voice that there was a king past the edge of the world who kept a bargain as well as any house on this coast, and she would stand surety for him herself. Every harbor heard it.

She did not tell any harbor where.

Well. He did not ask her to.

The next spring the _Visizan_ went out again, with a hold full of salt, and a copy of a chart that was not the Academy's, and a pilot from the black island who had learned enough Taruzeli in a winter to be rude to the cook. Two months out she came to the passage between the two reefs. The water lay smooth. She did not put the lead over. She stood at the rail with a jar of thyme honey from the black island, sealed with wax, and she dropped it into the channel, and watched it go down.

Nothing rose. Nothing ate her. The reefs held still and the channel lay open, and the _Visizan_ went through.

She sailed to the black island and sold the king his salt, and bought his honey and his silks from the storehouses, the ones that had not rotted, and his timber, and the strange old ships he did not know what to do with she bought for firewood and broke up on his beach and sold the bronze. And she came home rich, and her daughter came home rich after her, and here we are, at this table, drinking the house's coffee, which is why the house can afford to give it to you.

That is the tale. Some of you are thinking it is a captain's tale, which is to say a lie with a profit in it, and that is a fair thing to think. The other houses on this quay say a great many things about us. They say the Visizuni pay a fish. They say we throw good honey into the sea because a grandmother of ours was drunk on thyme wine. They say the black island is the coast of somewhere ordinary, and the king was a harbor-master with bad manners, and we have kept the route quiet for three hundred years because it is cheap, not because it is magic. Maybe so. None of them has found it.

I will tell you what I know, and then you can go to bed.

I have never seen the one under the water. I have taken a ship of this house through that passage eleven times and dropped the jar eleven times and watched it go down into the dark blue, and I have never seen anything come up. I have never seen the reefs move. The water lies smooth, and we go through.

I have never missed a year.

Do you understand what I am telling you? I am not telling you the thing is down there. I am telling you that the line is in the book. When the war came, and the empire's squadrons sat on the island channels and nothing of ours could get out past them, there was a year when the house had no ship at sea at all. Not one. The guilds had our hulls for the flotilla, and the rest were burned at their moorings. That year my mother took a fishing boat with two old men and a girl, and one jar, and slipped the blockade at night, and sailed west for six weeks in a boat built to go out and come back in a day. She dropped the jar in the passage. She came home in the autumn with the boat half full of water and one of the old men dead, and she walked into the counting-room and wrote _Paid_ under the line, and then she sat down and did not get up for a week.

The clerks asked her afterward whether she had seen it. She said no. She said the water was smooth.

She said: I do not know what is down there. I know what we owe it.

That is what you are signing tomorrow, when you put your mark in the book. Not my grandmother's thing at the bottom of the sea. The book. Every line in it, the rope-walk and the jar and the cook. You will sail under this name, and this name has a debt on its first page that nobody can collect, and every year somebody of this house pays it anyway.

Now go to bed. And when you are in a storm, bail.
