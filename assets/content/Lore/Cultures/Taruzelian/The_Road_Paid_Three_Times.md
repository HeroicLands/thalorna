**The Road Paid Three Times** is a telling of the hill tribes of the larger islands of [[place-taruzelrgn|Taruzel]], given at the fire on winter nights when a goat-hair cloth is hung over the door against the wind. It is a hill story about the coast. Its hero is [bilgun]{.name data-type="being/npc" lang=taru} of the third fort, a hill man in the century when the tribes held the forts of [[lore-easternline|the Eastern Line]] for the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylarian Empire]]'s pay. He takes three wages for one pass over the old military road: the fort's, to guard it, a [[place-besikar2|Besikar]] house's, to close it, and the [[place-pashinar|Pashinar]] caravan masters', to open it. He earns all three to the letter, and when the three payers come up the road together to catch him, he makes them read their own papers aloud.

The tellers keep the hills' speech in the telling, short and dry, with a fixed opening and a fixed close, and the rendering keeps both. The coast tells a version of its own, in which the hill man is a thief and the Besikar house wins in court. The hills do not tell that one. The telling ends past the joke, on the war twelve years ago, when the tribes sent their warriors down the same road to fight for the port cities and came back with nothing. The tellers call that the fourth time the road was paid.

: The Road Paid Three Times {#telling}

Put the cloth over the door. The wind wants to hear this, and the wind does not get it for nothing.

This is a hill telling. A coast man may sit at this fire and drink our milk, and when the telling is over he may say what he likes about it at his own fire, and we will not be there to hear him. That is fair.

In the time of the forts, when the legions held the coast and the hills held the forts for the legions' pay, there was a man of the third fort called Bilgun. He had one goat, a good knife and a wife who was cleverer than he was, which in the hills is not rare. The third fort is the one above the pass, where the old road comes up out of the Pashinar valley and goes over the ridge and down toward the western harbors. You have seen it. The walls are still there, and the gate is not.

In those days the road was new, or newish. The legions had cut it after the great storm of riders came down out of the east and broke on the far shore, when the empire was afraid the riders would come over the strait. They did not come. The legions were afraid of them anyway, and they were afraid of us, because we were already here. So they did what the coast always does with a fear. They put a price on it. They built the forts, and they paid us to sit in them.

Hear what the coast does not know. A hill man knows what silver is worth. His people have been paid in it since the forts went up. He can weigh a coin on his tongue. He knows the empire's coin from the guild's coin with his eyes shut, and he knows which one his grandmother would take for a goat.

Now, the commander of the third fort was a man from the capital, who had bought his place and did not like the hills. He sent for Bilgun, because Bilgun had the best eyes in the fort, and he said:

"Bilgun, I will pay you a silver a month to guard the pass."

"Guard it from what?"

"From the riders, if they come. From thieves, if they come. From anything that comes up the road that should not."

"And if nothing comes?"

"Then you have been paid to sit on a rock," said the commander, "and I hope you enjoy it."

So they wrote it down, because the empire writes everything down. A clerk wrote it on a tablet and the commander put his seal on it, and Bilgun put his mark, which was a goat's foot. _Bilgun of the third fort is paid one silver a month to guard the pass against what should not come up the road._ Bilgun carried his copy of the tablet home to his wife, and she read it, because she could read and he could not, and she said, "Good. Keep it under the bed."

That is the first wage.

That summer a man came up the road in a blue coat. He was a factor of one of the great houses of Besikar, a house with a hundred hulls. You know the kind. He had a clerk with him carrying a coffee pot, and he sat down on Bilgun's rock without asking and poured himself a cup and did not pour one for Bilgun.

"Bilgun of the third fort," he said, "you are a man of some importance."

"I have one goat," said Bilgun.

"You sit on the pass," said the factor. "Every caravan that lands at Pashinar from across the strait has two ways to the western harbors. It can come over this road, on its own mules, in four days. Or it can go round the island by sea, in one of my house's hulls, at my house's price, in eight days. Now the road is cheaper and the sea is slower, and so the caravans come over the road, and my hulls lie in the harbor with nothing in them."

"That is sad for your hulls," said Bilgun.

"I will pay you a silver a month," said the factor, "to close the pass."

Bilgun thought about it. He did not say no. In the hills, a man who says no at once was never going to say yes, and the coast man knows that, so he waits. Bilgun said, "Close it how?"

"Put a gate across it," said the factor. "A good gate. Shut it. Let nothing through that has not paid at the gate. Make the price high enough that the caravans go round by sea."

"Write it down," said Bilgun.

So the factor's clerk wrote it on a paper, because the coast writes on paper, and the factor put his house's seal on it, and Bilgun put his goat's foot. _Bilgun of the third fort is paid one silver a month to close the pass with a gate, so that nothing goes through that has not paid at the gate._ The factor drank the rest of his coffee and went back down the road to Besikar, well pleased, because he had bought a road for a silver a month, and his house had a hundred hulls and he was a clever man.

Bilgun took the paper home to his wife. She read it twice. Then she laughed until she had to sit down, and she said, "Good. Keep it under the bed with the other one."

That is the second wage.

Bilgun built the gate. He built a good one, of oak from the woods below the ridge, with iron bands on it that his brother made, and a bar across it that took two men to lift. He hung it across the road at the narrowest place, below the fort, where the rock comes down on both sides. Then he shut it. He sat on his rock beside it with his knife and his goat.

The first caravan came up the road from Pashinar in the autumn. The caravan master was a man of the far side of the strait, a desert man, with forty mules and a temper. He looked at the gate, and he looked at Bilgun.

"What is this?"

"It is a gate," said Bilgun. "It is shut."

"I can see it is shut. Open it."

"Nothing goes through this gate that has not paid at the gate," said Bilgun. "It is written down. I have it under my bed."

"How much?"

Now here is the thing. The factor had said: make the price high enough that the caravans go round by sea. And the price round by sea was eight days, and the factor's house's price, and the factor's house's price was very high, because a house with a hundred hulls and no other ships on the route can name its own price. So Bilgun named a price. He named a price that was high. It was a little lower than the sea.

The caravan master swore by every god his people have, and they have a great many, and he pushed the price back, and Bilgun pushed back his push, and they sat on the rock and argued until dark, and Bilgun's wife brought them milk, and in the end they clasped on a price. It was a little lower than the sea and a good deal higher than nothing. The caravan master paid it, and Bilgun and his brother lifted the bar, and forty mules went through the gate, and the gate shut behind them.

"Well," said the caravan master at the top of the pass, looking back. "You are a thief. But you are a cheaper thief than the sea."

He went down to Pashinar again in the spring and told every caravan master who crossed the strait. That is how it is with the caravans. They talk at the wells.

The next year the caravan masters of Pashinar came up the road together, seven of them, and they sat on Bilgun's rock, and they did not argue the price. They said:

"Bilgun of the third fort, we will pay you a silver a month to keep the pass open."

"It is shut," said Bilgun. "You can see it."

"Open for us," they said. "Every caravan that pays at the gate goes through, and no caravan waits at the gate longer than it takes to pay, and the price at the gate stays lower than the sea. Lower than the sea every year, whatever the sea's price is. That is what we will pay you for."

"Write it down," said Bilgun.

The caravan masters wrote on leather, because that is what they carry. Seven seals went on it, and the goat's foot. _Bilgun of the third fort is paid one silver a month by the caravans of Pashinar to keep the pass open to every caravan that pays at the gate, and to keep the price at the gate lower than the sea._

Bilgun took the leather home to his wife, and she did not laugh this time. She put it under the bed with the tablet and the paper, and she said, "Now you will be found out. When you are found out, do not run. Bring all three up to the fire."

That is the third wage.

So for a while Bilgun had three silvers a month and a gate and a goat, and he bought a second goat, and his wife bought a pot that was not cracked. The caravans went over the pass and paid at the gate. The price at the gate was a little lower than the sea. The hulls of the house of Besikar lay in the harbor with not much in them. The fort's commander did not come out of the fort much, because he did not like the hills, and he did not ask.

Of course the factor found out. A house with a hundred hulls has a man in every market, and his man in Pashinar wrote to say that the caravans were still going over the road, the same as always, only now there was a gate. The factor came up the road in his blue coat in a fury, with two clerks and a coffee pot and a magistrate's officer. On the road he met the caravan masters coming down, and they told him about the gate, and he told them about the gate, and they all looked at each other. Then they all went up together to the fort, and banged on the door, and got the commander out of his bed, and they all came up to the pass, the factor and the caravan masters and the commander and the magistrate's officer, and they found Bilgun on his rock with his two goats.

"Thief," said the factor.

"Thief," said the caravan masters.

The commander, who had been got out of bed, said nothing yet.

Bilgun did not run. He said, "Come up to the fire, and bring your papers."

They came up to his house in the fort wall, all of them, and stood in it, because it was not big enough to sit in. His wife brought the tablet and the paper and the leather out from under the bed and put them on the hearthstone. Bilgun said:

"I cannot read. You can all read. Read them out."

The commander's clerk read the tablet. _Bilgun of the third fort is paid one silver a month to guard the pass against what should not come up the road._

"Did anything come up the road that should not?" said Bilgun.

The commander thought about it. No riders. No thieves, except possibly the man standing in front of him. Forty mules at a time, with their caravan master, and the caravan masters paid the empire's road toll at Pashinar the same as always.

"No," said the commander.

"Then I guarded it," said Bilgun. "Pay me."

The factor's clerk read the paper. _Bilgun of the third fort is paid one silver a month to close the pass with a gate, so that nothing goes through that has not paid at the gate._

"Is there a gate?" said Bilgun.

"There is a gate," said the factor, through his teeth.

"Is it shut?"

"It is shut."

"Has anything gone through it that did not pay at it?"

The factor looked at the caravan masters. The caravan masters looked at the factor. Every one of them had paid at the gate, every time.

"No," said the factor.

"Then I closed it," said Bilgun. "Pay me."

The caravan master who could read best read the leather. _Bilgun of the third fort is paid one silver a month by the caravans of Pashinar to keep the pass open to every caravan that pays at the gate, and to keep the price at the gate lower than the sea._

"Has any caravan that paid waited at the gate?" said Bilgun.

"No."

"Is the price at the gate lower than the sea?"

"Yes," said the caravan master. "A little."

"Then I kept it open," said Bilgun. "Pay me."

There was a silence in that little house, the kind that comes when people are doing sums. The magistrate's officer, who was a coast man and an honest one, turned the paper over and read the back of it, in case there was anything there. There was not. He said to the factor:

"Sir, you paid him to close it so that nothing went through that had not paid. You did not say paid to whom. You did not say the price had to be your price. You said high enough that the caravans went round by sea. You did not write that part down."

"I said it," said the factor.

"You said it on a rock," said the magistrate's officer. "You wrote it on a paper. The paper is the bargain. You know that better than I do. You told me so last winter, in a case against a captain."

The factor said nothing at all.

The commander began to laugh. He laughed so hard he had to go outside, and he laughed against the wall of his fort until the guard came to see if he was sick. When he came back in he paid Bilgun his silver for the month, and a second one for the laugh, and he went back to his bed.

The caravan masters paid Bilgun. They did not mind. They had been paying a little lower than the sea, and now they knew why.

The factor paid. He did not want to. But a coast man who will not pay on his own paper, in front of a magistrate's officer, in front of seven caravan masters who talk at the wells, is a coast man nobody on any quay will lend to by the spring. He knew it. He paid. Then he said to Bilgun, at the door:

"You hill people are thieves."

And Bilgun said what the hills say. He said: "You coast people say a hard bargain is a bargain. You say a bargain broken is theft. I have broken nothing. Read the paper. You wrote it."

That is the telling of the three wages. Drink your milk.

No. Sit down. There is the fourth.

The coast forgot the forts. The legions forgot the riders. The empire stopped paying the hills to sit in the forts, the way the empire stopped paying everybody, and the gate rotted and the iron bands went for plowshares. The empire kept up the road, because its wagons used it, and its tax men came up it every year for our goats and went down again. Bilgun is under a stone above the pass, and his wife beside him, and the second goat's great-grandchildren are on that hill still, and so are ours.

Then, in the time of my father, the coast came up the road again. Not a factor in a blue coat. Boys from the harbors, with papers. The papers had a broken chain on them, and a sun, and a ship. They pinned them to the gate-posts of the fort where Bilgun's gate used to be, and they read them out, because they knew we could not all read, and they did not ask us whether we could.

They said: the empire takes your sons and your goats and gives you nothing. That was true. They said: come down and fight it, and the coast will be free, and so will you. They said land, and they said a voice, and they said brothers.

Nobody wrote anything down. Nobody put a seal on anything. Nobody came up the road with a coffee pot and sat on the rock and argued until dark. They said brothers, and we went.

The men of the third fort went down the road, my father among them, the road Bilgun kept, the road the empire cut. They went down past the gate-posts and the papers, with their spears and their cloaks, and they fought in the hills against the legions' supply wagons, because they knew the hills, and some of them went on down to the sea, which they had never seen, and fought there. Some of them came back up the road. Some of them did not.

The ones who came back up the road came back with nothing, and the road is going to grass under them, because the confederation does not keep it. The empire at least kept the road. No land. No voice. No silver, not one coin, not even a coin of the guilds' that a grandmother would not take for a goat. The coast men had a confederation now, and a charter, and the charter did not have the hills in it anywhere.

My father went down to Besikar once afterward, to ask. He stood in the square below the old governor's palace where the money people sit now, and he asked a clerk where it was written. The clerk asked him what. My father said: land, a voice, brothers. The clerk said: show me the paper.

My father did not have a paper. He had a sheet with a chain on it, from the gate-post. The clerk read it, and gave it back to him, and said: there is nothing here but freedom, and you have that.

That is the fourth time the road was paid. That time the hills paid it. We did not write it down.

So when a coast man sits at this fire and drinks our milk, and asks us why the hills will not deal with a guild, tell him about Bilgun. Tell him a hill man will clasp on anything, with anybody, if it is written down and read out.

And tell him the hills clasped once without the paper, and we know what it cost.

Take the cloth down from the door. The wind has heard enough.
