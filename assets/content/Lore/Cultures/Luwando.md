The **Luwando** are the river-keepers, one of the four traditions of the [[lore-bikoclt|forest peoples]]. Their name is _luwa_, a river, with the ending of one who keeps it. They are the forest people outsiders meet most often, and the ones outsiders take for the whole.

## Where They Live

Luwando tribes live along the great rivers of the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]], both the ones that run north to the Vylarian Sea and the ones that run south into the flooded wetlands. Each tribe holds a stretch of one river, a few days by dugout from end to end, and its bands camp on the high banks where the water does not reach in the rains. The northernmost Luwando are the tribes nearest the river-posts of the [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Au'Khelathu]].

## How They Live

The river feeds them first. They fish with weirs, basket-traps and spears, and they move up and down their stretch in dugouts cut from a single trunk. They gather along the banks and hunt the forest a day's walk back from the water. Each band keeps a small garden of plantain and root on cleared bank, fenced with thorn against the [[being-xrthnfrs|forest elephant]] and watched from a platform against the [[being-chmpnz|chimpanzee]], and the garden is the margin, not the meal.

A Luwando camp stays in one place for a few years and moves when the river moves, when the garden tires, or when someone dies there. The houses are pole and thatch, raised a little off the ground.

The Luwando are also the forest's door to the north. Most of the _gbenindo_, the keepers of the hereditary trade-friendships, are Luwando, and the fish, salt, metal and cloth that come up the rivers pass through their hands into the deep forest, while honey, ivory, hides and the healing plants come out of it the same way.

## What the Forest Is

To the Luwando the forest is a household and the river is its host. The river brings everything: fish, rain, trade, strangers and the dead. A person on the river is the river's guest, and the river expects its guests to be fed. That is _dombe_, guest-right: anyone the river brings to a Luwando camp, forest person or outsider, is fed and housed for three nights and asked nothing. On the fourth day the guest says what they want, and the camp decides whether to keep them, trade with them or send them on.

The river itself is _yobe_, the host, and the Luwando speak of it as a person with a temper. A drowning is the host's displeasure, a good catch its welcome, and a flood a host who has been insulted. The first fish of every catch goes back to the water.

## The Four Standings, and the Leader

A Luwando child is a _tolu_ until the first fish it catches alone is returned to the river, and then it is _wibo_. A stranger the camp keeps after the third night is a tolu, and becomes wibo by giving guest-right to someone else in turn.

The _mbeli_, the elders, make the _kpaso_, the leader. When a leader dies or the elders judge that the river has turned against the tribe, the elders sit at the landing through a night and speak names. Any elder may speak against a name, and a name is set aside the moment one does. The first name that no one speaks against by dawn is the leader. The elders unmake a leader the same way, and do it more readily than any other tradition: a leader under whom the river gives bad years seldom lasts long.

The leader decides where the tribe's bands camp, settles quarrels over fishing grounds, and speaks to other tribes. The gbenindo are not the leader and do not choose one. A Luwando leader who is also a gbenindo is the exception, and the elders watch that leader closely.

## Kin

A Luwando counts kin through the mother. A person belongs to the river the mother was born on, and stays of that river whatever stretch of water they live by. Young people marry across rivers, never within their own, and a husband comes to his wife's band.

The trade-friendships pass the same way, from a gbenindo to the children of that keeper's sister. Leadership never passes at all.

## The Dead

The dead go downstream. A body is wrapped in bark cloth and laid in an _ewudi_, a bark boat made for one passenger, and the ewudi is pushed out into the current at night. The river carries the dead down to where the dead live, and the stretch of water below the camp is not fished until the next rains. The dead are spoken to at the water's edge, and they answer through the river.

The Luwando hold some reaches of their rivers forbidden, and do not fish them or pass them by day. The drowned plain below the [[place-drwndzgrt|Drowned Ziggurat]] is one, and their elders give no reason beyond the warning.

## The Other Three

- **The [[lore-yesomaclt|Yesoma]]** are children. They take from the forest as a child takes from its mother and give no one a seat by the fire. The Luwando trade with them gladly, fish and metal for honey and ivory, and think them improvident.
- **The [[lore-kubindoclt|Kubindo]]** keep fussy houses, each with its own manners. The Luwando respect them and take their sick to the Kubindo spirit-places, and are glad to come home.
- **The [[lore-pewayeclt|Pewaye]]** are rivals at the door. They sell the forest to outsiders on the margin as the Luwando do on the river, and the Luwando think they sell its manners with its goods.

## What an Outsider Sees

An outsider sees a fishing village on a riverbank, a courteous welcome, three days of food and no questions, and a trader who speaks for the village. Most outsiders take the trader for a chief and the camp for the tribe, and both are wrong. The outsider does not see the leader, who does not trade, or the elders who made that leader, and does not see the river as the Luwando see it: as the host who brought the outsider, and who expects its guests to behave.
