In late autumn the mistress of a [[place-nordheim|Nordheim]] hall has the storehouse counted again, and the steward goes back down the row with his tally-stick: the barrels of salted fish, the barley sacks, the hams on their hooks. Everything in the hall that cannot be fed through the winter is about to be killed. The count decides how many animals that is.

Vetrnaetr, the Winter Nights, marks the turn toward winter. It is the most somber of the four great festivals. Livestock that cannot be kept through the scarcity are slaughtered in a great blót, the meat is preserved, the bones are read, and the dead are honored.

## The Blót

A capable household mistress earns her standing by keeping people and stores safe, and Vetrnaetr is where the storehouse tests it: the tally says how many animals the hall can feed through the dark, and the blót takes the rest.

The gods receive their shares by the season's custom. [[lore-solrundty|Sólrún]] receives the greatest portion, because she keeps half of the honored dead. [[lore-odvardty|Ódvar]] is invoked as the wanderer of the coming long dark. [[lore-nahilddty|Náhild]]'s name is spoken with caution.

## The Door-Seat

The hall's búvördr (steward) seats its honored old by the door and serves them first. The place nearest the way out is the place of honor. A household that cannot provision its elders must seek help from other kin and halls, and the obligation can enter [[lore-kinhalcrdt|hall credit]] and weigh on a house that leaves it unpaid.

Tellers connect the door-seat to the **Three Winters**, the famine remembered in the kin-fell tale: a place near the way out became a place of honor when people swore they would feed the old. The tellers differ over the oath. The custom is plain to every guest, and no one at the door waits for the argument to finish.

## The Howe-Wake

Vetrnaetr is one of two seasons when a seeker may sit awake on a burial mound or at a crossroads to hear the dead; the other is the ninth night of [[lore-jol|Jól]]. A völva's leave is customary and seldom given, because the dead may demand a truthful answer to a question of their own. What the dead tell a seeker in the dark is no skald's witnessed record, and anyone who acts on it must first find living evidence. A wake worked against a named person can be judged a grave wrong at the ting, which then withdraws its peace from the seeker.
