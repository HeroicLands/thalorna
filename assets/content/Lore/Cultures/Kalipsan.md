# terran_analog: "A free port of the northern seas with a Norse old city and a Norman and Mediterranean merchant quarter, governed like a Hanseatic or Venetian merchant republic. Carries over: shipping houses that are families and firms at once, marine insurance and shares in hulls, an oral sea-law kept by a professional caste, guest-right. Departs: seats weighted by tonnage counted each spring, and a Harbormaster of a single term."

[[affiliation-calypsa|Kalipsa]] is a harbor that has had to welcome everyone, and the Kalipsi are what the harbor made of them. The oldest houses are of [[lore-nordheimnclt|Nordmen]] stock and the old city speaks [[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal]]; the markets speak [[skill-valtarlng|Valtari]]; Élavendri, Provénzian and Vilérian families settle on the [[place-calypsaisle|island]] every generation and their grandchildren are Kalipsi. What makes a Kalipsi of any of them is the same three things: a house, a hull, and the rule that a guest is not robbed. The Kalipsi share the faiths of the [[lore-aureldianclt|Aureldian]] peoples, and what follows is what they hold beyond them.

## The House and the Hull

A Kalipsi belongs first to a house. A house is a family and a firm at once: the kin of one name, the masters sworn to sail for it, the factors contracted to buy for it, and the hulls and shares it owns. Its name is on a roll that the head keeps, and a person whose name is on the roll shares in the house's voyages and answers for its debts. A house takes members by birth, by marriage and by contract, and the newer houses take them by contract as readily as by marriage.

The hull is what a house is worth. The [[affiliation-captnscncl|Captains' Council]] counts every house's tonnage each spring, and a house's voices in the Council, its credit with the moneylenders and its place on the quay all follow the count. A house that loses three ships in a season may be elbowed from the Council within the year; a house that opens a new trade may enter it. The Kalipsi call this merit, and they are candid that merit here means the talent to make a ship pay.

## Good and Evil

The Kalipsan answer to what is good is **the bargain kept, the guest protected and the loss shared; evil is the guest robbed, the crew abandoned and the verdict defied.** The culture is cheerfully amoral in commerce and severe in matters of personal honor, and it sees no contradiction. A sharp bargain is skill, and a bargain broken is a debt the [[lore-sealaw|Sea-Law]] settles. A Kalipsi captain who breaks a contract may be pitied. One who breaks a guest's trust is not welcome in the harbor again.

The guest's rule is the oldest thing the city says about itself. A stranger who has eaten at a Kalipsan table or been given a berth in the harbor is under the city's protection, and a wrong done to him is judged twice as heavily as the same wrong done to a citizen. A free port lives on strangers trusting it, and the Kalipsi treat the rule as the foundation of everything else.

## Success and Failure

Success is a seat. A Kalipsi has succeeded when his house sits on the Council, and he is a success when he is the one who sits in it, or the master the house trusts with its best hull. The marks are legible: a berth on the inner quay of [[place-oldmole|the Old Mole]], a bond written by the eldest syndicate without a second look, a name the arbitrators of [[place-sealawhall|the Sea-Law Hall]] recite in a verdict that went the house's way. A Past Harbormaster has succeeded for life.

Failure is to be unbondable. A house the [[affiliation-bondsyndcts|Bond Syndicates]] will not write for cannot borrow against its hulls, and a house that cannot borrow sells them. The lighter failure is to fall below the census floor and lose the seat; the heavier is to be struck from the syndicates' roll; and the heaviest is to be refused the harbor.

The Kalipsi value a sound hull, a quick reckoning, a straight answer to a straight question, the sea-sense that brings a ship home in fog, and the Sea-Law recited exactly. They value least the noble who has never made anything pay, the master who sails without a pilot, and the citizen who will not serve in the levy.

## The Man Admired

The most admired man in Kalipsa is not the Harbormaster. He is the master who brings every hull home. He has sailed the same house's ships for thirty years, has never lost a crew, has never sailed against an owner's written order or without a pilot where the city requires one, and has been offered a seat in three houses and taken none. When the fog lies on the eastern approaches the other masters wait to see whether he sails, and when he does not, nobody does. He is paid a full share of every voyage and keeps a modest house on the lower quay, and his word on what the weather will do is worth more on the quay than any Councilor's.

The admiration has a cost the culture sees. His house owes its standing to him and knows it, his sons have gone to other houses to be more than his sons, and the culture that admires him keeps him off the Council, because a master who will not take a seat cannot be outvoted.

## The Woman Admired

The most admired woman in Kalipsa is a master arbitrator of the Sea-Law Hall. She learned the Sea-Law over seven years of recitation, has heard cases for forty, and can give back word for word the rules and the verdicts that bear on any dispute a house can bring. Every great house has chosen her as its arbitrator at least once, and every great house has lost a hearing before her. No house has ever bought her, and the whole quay knows it.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the master's. He brings the hulls home; she keeps the law that says who owes what when one does not come home, and the culture holds that the two are the same work done on different ground. Its cost is visible in her too. The houses she has ruled against choose other arbitrators when they can, her fees are smaller than a bought arbitrator's, and in a year like this one, with an election hanging on a hearing, both parties want her and neither trusts her.

## What the City Keeps

The Kalipsi point to a handful of things that belong to the whole city. **The Sea-Law** is kept by recitation and nobody may write it down, and an arbitrator who recites a rule wrongly is remembered for it. **The census roll** that the Clerk of the Council keeps is the city's record of what every house is worth. **The bond paper**, sealed with a syndicate's sign and an underwriter's mark, is the one paper every Kalipsi trusts. **[[place-lantrnklps|The Lantern of Kalipsa]]**, relit every night, and **the Old Mole**, whose carved stones nobody can read, are the city's two oldest promises: that a ship coming in will see the light, and that the harbor will be still behind the mole.

## The Wayfarer and the North

The faiths of the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]] are the city's religions, and the temple of [[affiliation-pozeras|the Faith of Pozeras]], the Wayfarer's, on the quay is the one an ordinary Kalipsi visits most. A master gives at the Wayfarer's altar before a voyage and again when he returns, and a crew gives with him. A sailor's family gives on the day he sails and on the day he is due. The old Nordmen families keep the gods of the north as well, and crews at sea honor the [[affiliation-thrunvald|Faith of Thrunvald]] in ways they would not mention ashore. Weddings mix Nordmen drinking-songs with Aureldian hymns, and the kitchens combine northern smoked fish with southern spice and olive oil.

The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Thordimalon]] keeps a factor's house and no chapterhouse. The Kalipsi treat magic as a trade like any other, regulated by nobody, and an Order agent who keeps his conduct discreet is tolerated.

## The Year in the Harbor

The year is the sailing year. **Spring** opens it with the census, when every house dresses its hulls with flags at their berths for the tally-men and the whole city walks the quays to see who has gained and who has lost; in a term's last year the Harbormaster's election follows it. **Summer** is the long voyages, and the quays are quiet. **Autumn** is the grain season, the busiest and the most dangerous, when the hulls of [[place-kalipsarun|the Kalipsa Run]] come in deep-laden through the fog and the families stand on the Old Mole at dusk. **Winter** brings the hulls home, and it is the season of weddings, contracts and Sea-Law hearings, when every house is in harbor and every dispute can be heard. The festivals of the Pantheon fall where they fall in that year, and the Wayfarer's are the best kept.

## Reward and Penalty

The ladder of reward runs from the hull to the Council. A sailor's reward is his share of the voyage; a master's is a better hull and a larger share; a factor's is a house's name and a place on its roll; a house's is a seat, and a Councilor's is the Harbormaster's chain for three years and the precedence of a Past Harbormaster for life.

The ladder of penalty runs the other way. A debt is paid under the Sea-Law, by order of the arbitrators. A house that defies a verdict is struck by every syndicate at once. A crime against a citizen is tried by the city's courts and punished by fine, by the lash or by service at the oars of the navy; the same crime against a guest is judged twice as heavily. A foreigner who breaks the guest's trust is refused the harbor for life. The gravest sentence is proscription: a citizen struck from the roll and outlawed by the Council's decree, his property forfeit, whom any citizen may kill without penalty.

## The Council and the Temple

Civil authority is the Council's and the arbitrators', and the temples hold none of it. The Council makes war and peace, sets the tariffs and the levy and alone may change the Sea-Law; the arbitrators judge the Sea-Law; the Harbormaster's officers keep the watch, collect the customs and try crimes. The temples keep the gods' rites and the reading of the drowned, and claim nothing else. The Clerk of the Council keeps the census roll and the Council's record, each house keeps its own roll, and the master arbitrators keep the roll of hearings. When the answers conflict, the Council decides, and its decision is a vote weighted by tonnage.

## The Drowned

The Aureldian faiths teach that the soul passes through the spirit realm and that the rites of the gods receive it there ([[lore-aureldianclt|Aureldian]]). The Kalipsi add one belief about the sea: a sailor lost at sea is received only when his name is read aloud on the quay, at the Wayfarer's temple, by the house that sent him. The sea gives back few bodies, so the name is what the house has to give, and a drowned man whose name nobody reads is believed to wander the approaches in the fog.

That belief shapes the ordinary day. Every sailor's name is entered on the house's roll before his hull sails, so that it can be read if the hull is lost, and a master checks the roll against his crew at the gangway. A house pays the Wayfarer's priests for the reading of every name, and a house that skimps on a reading is talked about on the quay for a generation. A family stands on the Old Mole at dusk while a hull is overdue, and the autumn fog is when the old city keeps its doors shut. A sailor's will is a share: what his house owes him from his last voyage goes to whoever he named on the roll.

## The Weak and the Hurt

The Sea-Law says a loss is shared by those it saved, and the city carries the rule into the household. A sailor who drowns leaves his share of the voyage to his family, and the house that sent him owes his widow that share and keeps his children's names on its roll until they are old enough to sail or to marry out. A sailor crippled at sea is kept by his house as a watchman, a net-mender or a tally-man for the rest of his life, and a house that turns one out loses masters it cannot afford to lose. The guilds keep a box for their own.

The city keeps nobody else. A beggar with no house and no guild lives on what the temples give, and the Wayfarer's temple feeds the quay's poor once a day. A stranger in want is under the guest's rule only once he has been given a berth or a meal, and the city does not hurry to give either.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

The house shelters its own, and the guild shelters what the house does not. Children of the houses learn reckoning and letters from the houses' clerks and the sea from the houses' masters; the arbitrators teach the Sea-Law to their apprentices and to nobody else; the pilots' guild teaches the approaches to its own. Defense is the navy's, paid by the tonnage levy, and every citizen owes service in the levy in time of war. The villages of the South Shore owe the navy crews and nothing else.

## What the City Gets Wrong

The city's merit counts hulls, and the [[place-calypsashore|South Shore]] has boats. Seventeen thousand people fish, dye and herd on the island, crew the navy and sell their springs' water to the city in a dry summer, and none of them has a voice on the Council. The thoughtful Kalipsi say so and are not listened to. The culture's sin is its civil war of about 470 AF, when a Harbormaster who would not give up the office set the houses fighting in the harbor and the streets of the old city; the single term is its penance, and every Harbormaster since has stepped down on time. Its present embarrassment is the furs, which come to the outer quay of Sarmelis from the raiders of the [[place-vrystwald|Vristwald]] shore and leave it with a Kalipsan mark, and which the old houses denounce in the Council and buy on the quay.

## What a Person Owes

A Kalipsi owes, in order: his house, which means the names on its roll and the hulls it sails; his hull and its crew, whom he does not abandon; the guest, who is under his protection the moment he has eaten at his table; and the city, which he owes his levy, his service in time of war and his obedience to the Sea-Law. In return he is owed his share of every voyage he sails, a hearing under the Sea-Law in any dispute, the city's protection for every guest he brings, and, if he is lost, his name read on the quay.

The language for this is Nordmal's, and the [[skill-nordmalng|language note]] has its words.
