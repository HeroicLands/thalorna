The peoples of the [[place-cntrlrnfrsts|Central Rainforests]] have no name for themselves as a whole. A forest person is _biko_, one of the people, and anyone who speaks [[skill-ekpelulng|Ekpelu]] and lives by the forest's manners is biko too. Everyone else is _gbolo_, an outsider, and the word carries no insult: it says only that the forest does not know that person.

There are many tribes and every one is small. A band of a few families camps and eats at one fire, and a tribe is the few bands that follow one leader. Nothing joins the tribes but the tongue, the manners they share and the trails between them. Each tribe also belongs to one of four loose traditions, four ways of being a forest person, and the traditions are not peoples or polities: they have no borders, no meetings and no leaders, and a tribe holds to one because its parents did.

## One Tongue

Every forest tribe speaks Ekpelu, and a band from one end of the forest can talk with a band from the other. The traditions speak it with different accents, audible from the first sentence, and each has its own words for its sacred things, which it does not speak to outsiders and seldom to the other traditions. Ekpelu is a tonal tongue, and a call carries its words in pitch alone, so a band sends news across a valley by voice without a drum. The [[skill-ekpelulng#lexicon|Ekpelu Lexicon]] gives the common words and the handful each tradition keeps.

## The Four Standings

Every tribe in the forest orders itself the same way, with four standings.

- **A _tolu_** is a dependent: a child, a captive, or a stranger the band feeds but has not taken in. A tolu is cared for and has no voice.
- **A _wibo_** is a member, an adult with a voice. A tolu becomes wibo by a rite each tradition holds differently, and a stranger becomes wibo by learning the tongue and the band's manners well enough that the band forgets to correct them.
- **A _mbeli_** is an elder, a member whose memory and dealings with the spirits count. No one is made mbeli: a member becomes one when the band starts asking that member first.
- **The _kpaso_** is the leader, the one person the tribe follows.

A standing is a state a person is in, never a title passed from parent to child. The child of a kpaso is a tolu like any other child. How a kpaso is made is one of the deepest differences between the traditions, and each tradition thinks the others do it badly.

## Nothing Above the Tribe

The _ndaro_, the tribe, is the largest thing in the forest. Two tribes may feast together, marry their young to each other, hunt one stretch of forest by agreement or feud for generations, and each of these is a thing that happened, not a body that lasts. No forest leader has ever spoken for more than one tribe.

The tribe at [[place-lpdkngcrt|the Leopard King's Court]] is the one the forest points to when it means a tribe that has gone wrong. It is one tribe under a sorcerer-leader whom its neighbours fear, and the tribes around it carry gifts to him so that he leaves them alone. The gifts buy quiet, not rule: the tribes that bring them have their own leaders and owe him nothing else.

## The Forest Is Alive

Every tradition holds that the forest is alive in a way other woods are not, and that a person lives in it by agreement, not by right. It is full of _mbosa_, spirits, beyond counting, and many of them hold a _kubi_, a spirit-place with its own manners. A person owes the forest what a guest owes a host: a _dipo_, an offering, before taking; restraint in the hunt; and apology for harm done. A hunter takes what the band needs and leaves the rest, a gatherer leaves part of every bush, and a band that kills more than it eats expects the forest to answer. The forest's answers are a fall, a fever, a trail that does not lead home.

What the forest _is_ differs by tradition: a host, a parent, a household of spirits or a boundary. That difference is what the traditions are.

## Trade Through the Door

No forest person trades with a stranger. All trade between the forest and the outside world passes through a _gbeni_, a trade-friendship that binds one forest family to one outside family and passes from one generation to the next. The forest partner is the _gbenindo_, the keeper of the friendship, and only a gbenindo may take an outsider's goods into the forest or carry the forest's goods out.

The forest sends out hardwood, ivory, hides, dyes and the plants that heal or intoxicate. It takes in metal, glass, salt and cloth. On the northern rivers the gbenindo deal with the river-posts of the [[affiliation-empireakhlth|Empire of Au'Khelathu]] and with [[lore-okharinclt|Okharin]] river-factors and herders; on the southern margin they deal with the [Sengala]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} of the [[affiliation-nylbtrblntn|Nyáluba]], the leopard-clan (see [[affiliation-nyalbleop|Leopard Totem]]) that trades north. A merchant who goes past the gbenindo to deal with a deeper tribe finds the trade stopped, the trail lost, or the fever waiting.

## The Four Traditions

| Tradition                    | Where                                     | What the forest is                                 |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| [[lore-luwandoclt\|Luwando]] | along the great rivers                    | a host: the river feeds whoever it brings          |
| [[lore-yesomaclt\|Yesoma]]   | the deep interior between the rivers      | a parent: it feeds whoever lives as its child      |
| [[lore-kubindoclt\|Kubindo]] | scattered, each tribe at one spirit-place | a household of spirits, each with its own manners  |
| [[lore-pewayeclt\|Pewaye]]   | the forest margins, north and south       | a boundary: inside is the people's, outside is not |

The four names are the common tongue's, built from what each tradition keeps or lives beside: the _[[lore-luwandoclt|Luwando]]_ keep the river, the _[[lore-yesomaclt|Yesoma]]_ are children of the forest, the _[[lore-kubindoclt|Kubindo]]_ keep a spirit-court, and the _[[lore-pewayeclt|Pewaye]]_ dwell at the edge. Most of the forest's people are yesoma. The luwando and the pewaye keep small gardens and the others keep none, and every tradition lives first by hunting, fishing and gathering.

## What an Outsider Sees and Cannot See

An outsider sees one people. Every tribe wears bark cloth, hunts with nets, spears and blowpipes, carries honey in leaf parcels, sets carved posts at its camps and speaks the same soft, open tongue. Every tribe is courteous, careful and slow to answer a question, and every tribe sends the outsider to someone else to trade.

What the outsider cannot see is the tradition. The accents sound alike to an ear not raised on them, the sacred words are not spoken in an outsider's hearing, and the differences that matter most are matters of belief: whether a leader is chosen, recognised, dreamed or earned, whether the dead go downstream or stay, whether a stranger is a guest, a child or a danger. A traveller who has met one forest tribe knows how to greet the next one and nothing else about it. The forest people know this, and the gbenindo who handle outsiders say as little about the other traditions as the outsiders allow.
