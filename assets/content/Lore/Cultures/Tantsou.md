Tantsou holds that the cosmos was made by sorting, that the sorting is incomplete, and that the work of holding the sorted world apart from the unsorted one is the business of every living person. Everything else about the Tantsou follows from that sentence, including the parts a foreigner finds cruel. A Tantsou does not experience his caste as an injustice or as a blessing—he experiences it as his classification, in the same way a comet has one, and the ministry that records his is a branch of the ministry that records the comet's.

## The Eight

Society runs in eight castes, which dictate profession, legal standing, dress, marriage, and the name—if any—by which a person may be known.

Two features of that list unsettle foreigners, and a Tantsou explains both without heat, because to him neither needs defending.

The sorcerers rank second and they are property. The Wánduo stand above generals and scholars and are chattel, bound to the houses they serve, and the two facts are one fact: their power is exactly why they are ranked high and exactly why they are owned. An unbound sorcerer is no free man in Tantsou thinking. He is an unclassified thing, which is the worst category there is.

The merchants rank seventh, below the peasants. A farmer feeds the empire and a merchant moves what other men made, so the Shuonong are frequently far wealthier than the Tsaihuo above them, and the wealth buys comfort and not one inch of standing. Foreign traders find this maddening.

## Classification Is a Sacred Act

The state religion is a Celestial Bureaucracy, and the earthly government is modeled on it and not the other way round. The Celestial Dragon is the First Classifier—the principle of order itself—and beneath it the Jade Sovereign, the Minister of Storms, the Minister of the Moon and the Five Elemental Lords administer their domains as ministries administer provinces.

A Tantsou clerk filing a return is therefore performing a small act of the same kind the Dragon performs, and doing it badly is impiety before it is inefficiency. The ministries that track the movements of comets also track the ritual purity of provincial officials, and no Tantsou sees the two tasks as different tasks.

Against all of it stand the Infernal Realms—the zones where the Great Sorting was never finished, and where the Unclassified presses on the borders of the sorted world. Imperial ritual, the labor of the bound sorcerers, and the moral discipline of the entire population are understood as one continuous custodianship of that border. Let it go and the cosmos does not fall or burn; it dissolves, back into the undifferentiated thing it was sorted out of. That is the Tantsou nightmare, and in their own estimation a people this rigid are holding a line.

## What Is Good, and What Succeeds

**Good is the thing kept in the place the Sorting gave it: a person doing his caste's work, in the form it requires, at the time it requires. Evil is the thing moved out of its place, and at the far end of that road the thing dissolved.** A Tantsou does not weigh a deed by the feeling behind it. He asks what it entered on the ledger and whether the classification is stronger or weaker for it. Murder, theft and a forged tablet are one kind of wrong with different weights: each moves something out of its category, and the law treats a crime across caste lines as graver than one within a caste for that reason.

Success is credit. A household has succeeded when its ledger is strong, its shrine provisioned, its heir fit, its caste duties kept and its marriages filed in the right order. The outward signs are strong ancestors who argue well in heaven, a harvest that holds, a lawsuit that goes the right way, and, rarely, a granted name. Failure is default: a debit that comes due as illness, a lost crop, a lost case, and at the bottom the stripped caste, which leaves a person unclassified.

The Tantsou value most exactness, precedence, patience with procedure and the keeping of the pattern as the Great Sorting set it. The highest virtue is to keep the pattern so correctly that the Unclassified never finds a gap, and the ideal Emperor is no visionary but a flawless administrator. They value least frankness, innovation and improvisation, which are acts with no bureau to receive them, and commerce, which moves goods between classifications and earns nothing by its own labor.

## The Man Admired

**Weng Hieng Rao Chai**, "Wind Tiger Roars Across Thunder Mountain," is the Tantsou's admired soldier. He was a Wohou officer known for years only as the Second Son of the Shield Bearer of the Zhowou Clan, near Téngmou, a description a registry clerk could file and no more. He led his troops to a victory that saved a province, and his liege lord granted him the name for the fury of his charge. He has signed nothing else since.

The Tantsou admire in him the thing their histories prize: a man of the third caste, neither Warlord nor Emperor, who did the work his classification gave him so thoroughly that the court registers had to find room for it. His name is a single deed, entered beside the old description that is never struck out, and he does not try to be more than the deed. His cost is the cost of the name. Everything he does is read against the charge, and an officer who is only ever his best day is expected to produce it again.

## The Woman Admired

**Zhi Zhai Leng Cheng**, "Rising Bright Star Bird," is the Tantsou's admired scholar. She was known as the Third Daughter of the Sage of the Zhi Zhai Clan, near Weichai, until she observed and recorded a new star that her seniors had missed. The court registers carry the star under her name, and the grant of her own name followed from it.

The Tantsou admire in her the exactness of the Tanchéi at its best: she looked, she set down what she saw, and she filed it in the form the registers could hold. Her admiration is not a lesser form of the officer's. He keeps the classification in place by force, and she widens it by finding what it had not yet recorded. Her cost is the one correction carries in a society that ranks precedence before accuracy: the star she found is also the measure of what her seniors missed.

## What the Record Keeps

A Tantsou can point to the documents and objects that hold the empire together. **The Dao Nong Chou**, the Great Person Record, is the census of every person from the Son of Heaven to the lowest Noutsou, and it is the mortal twin of the Master Registry the Archive of All Records keeps in heaven. **The Teng Lou**, the Heavenly Law, and **the Zhaolou**, the Caste Laws, are the code and its foundation. **The Zhao Mou**, the hardwood classification tablet, is the proof a traveler carries that he exists inside the sorted order, and forging one is among the gravest crimes there is. **The ancestor tablets** on every household shrine are the dead's addresses in the celestial filing. **The Zha Dao**, the Life Record the Tanchéi keep, lists every kind of living thing by classification, element and habit.

Certain sacred texts and relics, the cosmological treatises and the formulae of the great rites, may be held and read by the Tanchéi alone, and possessing one is a crime for anyone of another caste.

## The Day Kept

Ordinary religion is the upkeep of a working relationship, and it is done in small acts. A Tantsou of any caste keeps **Leichéi**, the slow meditative exercise performed at dawn in courtyards and village squares, which realigns the body's classifications with the cosmos. The household shrine is swept, the brazier lit and food set before the tablets, and spirit paper is burned for whatever the household owes or wants sent up: a petition to the right bureau, spirit money paid into a celestial account, goods for the dead's use. A family keeps its stock of spirit paper as an Aureldian family keeps grain, and running out is an emergency.

The rest of the day is the caste's work, which is itself an observance: the farmer at his terrace, the potter at his kiln, the clerk at his return. A temple is an office, and a person goes to it with a form, the right seal and the right bureau's name. A farmer who writes to the Lord of Water when the Minister of Storms holds the rain finds the clerk correcting the address and sending the paper to the brazier all the same. Once a year the Hearth Monitor (_Tsaikén_) files its report on the household's conduct. Alms, kept rites, peace within the family and kept caste duties are credits, and breaking caste, neglecting the rites, dishonor and crime are debits.

## The Round of the Year

The festivals are celebrations and filing days at once, and a district that misses one has failed to send heaven its paperwork.

- **Hou Lei Jui**, the Festival of Harmonious Lei, falls on the first day of the year. The Emperor performs the annual audit of the empire's Lei, opening the celestial filing for the new year, and every household that takes part in the feasts and lantern-lighting is entered in the empire's reckoning.
- **Wuen Tsai Jui**, the Festival of the Five Elements, fills five days, one for each element, and confirms that air still behaves as air, fire as fire and earth as earth.
- **Hou Nié Jui**, the Mid-Year Festival of Balance, is the half-year reconciliation, when ordinary people settle debts, end family quarrels and check their ledgers for hidden debits.
- **You Jui**, the Moon Festival, falls in late autumn, when the boundary between the sorted and the unsorted is thinnest. Lanterns are lit as points of bright Lei to shore up the boundary, and offerings go to the Minister of the Moon.
- **Singléi Jui**, the Festival of the Dead, falls at midwinter and is the costliest event of the year. Families provision their ancestors with spirit money and goods, review the ledgers and learn the dead's instructions for the coming year through the Leigen.
- **Shui Nié Wo**, the New Year Purification, closes the year. Households, temples and offices are cleansed, expired filings burned, and the Hearth Monitors submit their final reports.

## Credit and Default

Reward is credit on the ledger and the standing it buys. A kept rite, an alm and a duty done credit the household. A deed of outstanding service credits a person with a granted name, which only the Zhazho, the Wohou and the Tanchéi may receive, conferred by a liege lord or a senior clan member before witnesses. A slave who shows extraordinary courage may petition for elevation, and the petition is almost never granted. A just judgment credits the Warlord who gives it.

Penalty runs from the household to the state, and both write on the ledger.

1. **Debit.** Neglected rites, a false petition, a dishonor and an open accusation each enter a debit that stays until it is answered. A debit falls on the whole line, and the household pays it with offerings.
2. **Fine and restitution.** Fines fall mostly on the Shuonong and the Nouwén, and restitution pays the victim's household for the damage to its ledger. An offender who cannot pay is set to work off the debt in a lower working category.
3. **The lash and the brand.** Flogging, caning and branding mark a person who stepped outside his category, for life.
4. **Cleansing.** Desecration and sorcery short of the death penalty are answered with long purification rites under a Tanchéi or a Leigen. A rite left incomplete is answered with exile or death.
5. **Public humiliation.** A higher-caste offender may be paraded, dressed in a lower caste's clothing or set to the Noutsou's work, and must atone on the ledger to recover his standing.
6. **Prison.** Imprisonment is rare and is feared more than death, because a prisoner earns no credit while the celestial administration goes on keeping its accounts.
7. **Exile.** The exile loses household, shrine, caste and ledger standing, and many die of that isolation sooner than of hunger.
8. **Execution.** Death is the usual penalty for a grave crime, carried out with exact ritual so the soul reaches the Ministry of Final Account and is not loosed into the unsorted. Unlicensed magic and a forged tablet are punished with it at once.
9. **Ruising and Tseising.** A Wohou or noble condemned to die may be ordered to take his own life under a magistrate's eye, which spares his family a public execution. _Tseising_, the same rite chosen freely, answers a dishonor no court can undo, or protests an injustice in a form nobody can reverse. A Wohou offered the choice between outcasting and Ruising takes the knife.

## The Throne, the Magistrate and the Temple

Religion and government are one system seen from two sides, so the question of where civil authority ends and religious authority begins has an answer the Tantsou find odd: nowhere. The Emperor holds the Mandate of Heaven, an appointment to administer the mortal plane, and is the last court of appeal. The Warlords hold the provinces and read the Teng Lou through their magistrates, the _Guichai_. The Tanchéi advise on every case where heaven and law meet, and their reading of a question of classification is authoritative. The clan elders (_Zhaokuo_) of the Nouwén settle disputes by clan custom and the commands of the ancestors the village Leigen carries.

Records belong to the Tanchéi, who keep the registry, and taxes and tribute belong to the Wohou administrators of each holding. When the questions conflict, a case climbs: the Wonong who walks the street, then the magistrate, then the Warlord, and only a crime across caste lines or one that touches the rites climbs higher. The Mandate is the only appeal against the throne itself, and heaven conducts its reviews slowly. A dynasty is not judged by its goodness but by whether its filings are in order.

## What the Ledger Asks of an Ordinary Day

The Tantsou do not call the dead gone. They are posted. At death the Ministry of Final Account reads the moral ledger and assigns by its balance: a good balance makes an ancestor spirit, a minor official of heaven who argues for his living descendants before the bureaus; a bad one sends the dead to labor in the lower realms for a term, or to dissolution. An ancestor's posting is work. He files petitions, spends his own credit to ease his descendants' way, and can turn rain toward their fields or lean on a magistrate's ruling, if his ledger is full.

That is why an ordinary day looks as it does. The shrine is as necessary to a household as its hearth, because a family without a kept shrine has no advocates and no channel to send them anything. Offerings are payments to a working official and not remembrance, so a household feeds the dead before it buys what the living want, and keeps its spirit paper in stock. A birth, a union and a death are each registered with heaven, and a village rite enters them. A death puts a new carved tablet with a name and a death-day on the shrine. Wealth is a proof of ancestral virtue, so a family spends on temple petitions and festivals to credit its ancestors, which strengthens their advocacy, which brings fortune, which pays for more. Poverty is a debt in heaven as well as a want on earth, and the same cycle keeps it in the family. Marriages are arranged to join the ancestors who will work well together, and the heir inherits the duty with the property: he keeps the shrine, carries the petitions and answers for the ledger, and an heir who neglects them impoverishes the dead along with the living.

A person lives accordingly. He keeps his caste's duties because breaking them is a debit that arrives as illness. He does not hide a debt, because the account stands whether or not it is admitted. He gives alms because they are credit. He fears prison and exile for what they cost the ledger more than for the cell.

## Reaching the Dead

The living and the dead are bound by obligations that run both ways. The living owe offerings of spirit money and food, a shrine kept in order, a yearly report on the family filed through the Hearth Monitor, the keeping of every death-day, and children to carry on the line. The dead owe advocacy in heaven, warnings through dreams and omens, the protection of the household and counsel on great decisions. An ancestor who ignores petitions has abandoned his post, and a family may dismiss him: take his tablet from the shrine, stop his offerings and cut him off from everything it can send.

The channel is the **Leigen**, the spirit master, a Nouwén born with the gift of speaking to the dead. He carries a household's business to its ancestors and brings back the answer, reads ancestral commands, treats sickness from the spirit side, leads the village rites and settles disputes by asking the ancestors concerned to rule. He is a peasant by caste and every caste respects him; Warlords consult him in private, since acknowledging a peasant's authority openly would break caste. The cost is the offering the ancestors demand and whatever the Leigen's craft requires. The dead give orders, and a command through a Leigen, a dream or an omen is treated as a directive from heaven. The older the ancestor, the more binding the word, and in most families the dead have the last say on a great decision, including a marriage, a feud or an alliance.

The Sinale and Khazari within the empire do not keep these rites, and the bureaucracy has no place to file their dead.

## Names, and Who May Have One

A name in Tantsou is a privilege attached to classification, never a possession. Most people carry none. A person is identified by clan, position within the clan and place—"Fourth Son of the Battle Warden of the Clan of Moon Rises Over Still Water, near Weichai"—and the Nouwén, the Tsaihuo and the Shuonong live and die that way, as do the Wánduo, who are recorded as the property of a house, and the Noutsou, whose tablets name the owner and not the bearer. Only the Zhazho, the Wohou and the Tanchéi may be granted a personal name, and only for outstanding service, conferred before witnesses and entered beside the old description, which is never struck out.

The practical effect is that a Tantsou's name is a piece of public documentation. A foreigner who asks a Tantsou his name and receives a long formal answer has been handed a record, not a courtesy.

## Manner

The Tantsou manner is formal, indirect and exact. Precedence is worked out before a conversation begins and governs everything in it, and a foreigner who cannot be placed in the eight castes is placed provisionally somewhere low and treated with a courtesy that is real and entirely without deference.

Frankness is not valued. A direct refusal embarrasses both parties by forcing a classification into the open before it has been agreed, and a Tantsou goes to considerable lengths to decline without saying no. A visitor from western Ankaris reads this as evasiveness; it is closer to the opposite—an unwillingness to assert a thing until it has been properly established.

Omens are read professionally and taken seriously in practice. A Tanchéi's reading can postpone a campaign, and nobody involved regards this as superstition: the omen is a datum about the state of the classification, and acting against it is acting on bad information.

## What a Person Owes

A Tantsou owes in the order the ledger falls due: the ancestors first, then the lineage, then the caste whose duties define what he may and must do, then the Emperor whose Mandate holds the classification together.

Beneath all four he owes the sorting itself—the small daily correctness of doing what his classification requires, in the form it requires, at the time it requires. Nobody asks him to find this pleasant. He is asked to understand that the alternative is dissolution, and on that point the Tantsou are entirely serious.

In return he is owed what the classification gives. The household is owed its place in the register, a shrine whose ancestors argue for it, a magistrate who must hear a paying household's grievance and a lord who may not evict a tenant who pays his tribute. A Nouwén is owed the right to his land, his ancestors and his petition. The craftsman is owed the right to trade and train others under his guild. The ancestors are owed offerings and the descendants advocacy. The Emperor is owed obedience and owes heaven an empire in order, and when the account is wrong the Mandate is what is reviewed.

## The Weak

The Tantsou do not call poverty unjust. Each family stands where its accumulated ledger places it, and the poor are poor because their ancestors earned too little credit. That explains the weak without caring for them, and the faith supplies the caring through a second door: alms are a credit, so giving to a beggar, a widow or a hungry neighbor is spiritual business, and the person who gives is paid in the ledger as the person who receives is paid in rice.

The household is the first resort. A clan identifies every member by lineage and position, and an orphan, a widow or an aged relation has a place in the line by that description; the heir answers for the household's ledger and shrine, and its dependants come with them. The Nouwén village keeps its own: the headman who reports to the magistrate, the elders who settle disputes, the Leigen who treat what the Yaiwo cannot. The district granaries are kept sound through the summer repairs and guarded by the Wonong, and they are what a bad year draws on. In a bad harvest the dead compete with the living for the rice. A family that cannot keep the Festival of the Dead properly faces a year with weakened advocates, so the pressure is to feed the ancestors first and the children second, and the families that do so are not being heartless but following the ledger.

Below the ladder are the Noutsou, who are fed as property and are valued below any other person, and the exile, who has lost household, shrine and caste and has no bureau and no ancestors to speak for him. The law offers no general road out. Manumission, a petition for elevation after extraordinary service and purchase are the three narrow doors, and freedom does not end the mark: a freed slave is entered with the notation of what he was.

## Those Who Cannot Work

Sickness is a fault in the classification, and a fault in the classification is a debit that can be answered. The Yaiwo treat the body and the spirit together, in four ranks from apprentice to grand healer, and a Warlord rises when a Grand Healer enters his hall. Where the sickness comes from the spirit side—an ancestor who has withdrawn, a debit falling due, the touch of the unsorted—a Tanchéi or a village Leigen treats it with the healer. The province swept by plague is a province whose classification is failing, and the healers who contain it are doing the empire's spiritual work.

A person who cannot meet his obligations stays on his household's ledger. An injured soldier, a blind weaver and a feeble elder remain members of the line they were registered in, are fed from its stores and are provisioned with its shrine's care, and the heir's duty to the household includes them. The old hold a special place, because the dead's word grows more binding with age and the household treats the oldest living member as the nearest thing to that voice. The registry strikes a person off for a crime and not for being unable to work.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the clan's and the lord's. A Nouwén has the right to the land he works and cannot be driven off it while he pays tribute, and the magistrate who lets a lord evict a paying tenant answers for it. The Wohou administrator who holds a district answers for its revenue, courts and corvée.

Teaching is carried by the craft. A Tsaihuo workshop is a household: a master, his sons and apprentices, a seal registered with the district guild. The healer rises through four ranks at a master's side; the Leigen passes his craft from teacher to apprentice in the villages; the Tanchéi school their own in the ministries; the Wohou drill their households. At ten every child in the empire, whatever its caste, is tested for the gift. A child who shows it is claimed in that season, entered as Wánduo and taken to a sorcerers' compound, and does not see its family again; the family is forbidden to seek it. A child born in the compounds who shows too little is given to a Nouwén village and raised as its own.

Defense is layered. The Wonong who walks a street takes a thief to the magistrate. The Wohou command the garrisons and field armies. The Warlords hold their provinces and their sorcerers. At the frontier the Khazari lay out the fortifications that hold the mountain border, and the Wánduo work the great rites that turn a storm from the rice terraces.

## The Household at Every Age

The unit is the household, a registered merger of two families' standing with one ledger, one set of ancestor spirits and one Hearth Monitor. The clan council, not the couple, arranges the match. The council weighs the combined ledger of the two families, whether their ancestors will agree, the alliance and the astrology of the pair, and a Tanchéi or a Leigen consults the dead. Five steps make the wedding: the contract, the petition to the Bureau of Creation, the consultation with the ancestors, the transfer of tablets from the bride's shrine to the groom's, and the sealing. Only the four higher castes marry in law. The lower three live together and raise children within their caste, and a village Leigen performs a bonding rite that presents the couple to the ancestors of both families, which carries all the weight the village needs. The marrying castes may keep consorts, whose children are acknowledged and named heirs only when no child of the marriage is fit.

A child is registered at birth by a temple rite that places it on the household's ledger and classifies it under the spirit animal of its birth-year. It learns its caste's work and the shrine's duties as a household task. At ten it is tested. A young person trains under a master or in the clan's service, a Wohou youth in arms, a Tanchéi youth in the texts, a Tsaihuo youth in the workshop, and a rare few win a name. An adult marries by the council's arrangement, keeps Leichéi at dawn, answers for the caste's duties and, if chosen, becomes heir and keeper of the shrine. An old person is the lineage's living voice, close to the post the ancestors hold. Death in the household is registered by the village rite, and the shrine receives the new tablet; a person of rank who chooses Tseising writes the last record first, and the funeral registers him as a new ancestor.

## What the Sorting Cannot Admit

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Tantsou's is the sorting itself. A civilization built on the idea that what is classified exists and what is unclassified dissolves cannot hold a fact that the classification did not foresee, and thoughtful Tantsou disagree about what to do with one. One school holds that the register is the truth and that a deed it cannot hold must be corrected until it can. The other holds that a register that cannot record a correct act has stopped describing the world.

The sin is Tuija Wengyan. A general of the Wohou, named Iron Spear Piercing the Clouds for his service, received an imperial order to hold his position, judged it a mistake, attacked and won. A battle won by defying an order is proof the order was wrong, and an imperial order cannot be wrong without the Mandate behind it being questioned. So the court stripped him of his rank and exiled him, and the registry recorded the victory under another commander's name. The empire entered a falsehood in the book it holds to be the mortal twin of heaven's, in order that its own pattern might stay unbroken, and it made an enemy of the best general it had. The same habit is visible in the south, where the rice harvests have failed several years running and the Tanchéi are divided between a reading of broken classification in the domains of Earth and Water and a reading that the Mandate is weakening, which no scholar puts in writing. The empire cannot say aloud what the evidence of its own harvests says, because saying it would be a filing that cannot be withdrawn.
