The Sowides call themselves "the riders," and the rest of the world calls them the Khazryn, a name they do not use for themselves, because it belongs to the desert and not to any of the peoples who cross it. They are the herding tribes of the steppe and the dry grass between the sands, and the single fact that decides how you deal with them is that each tribe answers to nobody above its own chief. There is no king of the steppe, no council of tribes and no body that can promise on a tribe's behalf. A tribe's word binds that tribe, and a treaty with one is a treaty with one.

## The Tribe and the Dowek

A tribe is a few hundred families who share wells, a mark and a chief. The chief is the dowek, a man or a woman chosen by the elders from the strongest line, who holds the office for as long as the herds are fat and the wells are held. A dowek who loses either is replaced, usually quietly, by a cousin the elders have been counting for years.

The elders (_todwe_) meet in the moot (jutjen) at the central well, and the dowek speaks last. A dowek cannot raise a raid, move a camp or sell passage across the tribe's land without the moot's assent, and a dowek who tries finds the camp has moved without him. The war captain is a second post, held by whoever the warband will follow, and a tribe at war may have a captain whose word outweighs the dowek's for a season.

Tribes are not equal. A middling tribe is three hundred families on three wells; a strong one holds a dozen, and the weaker tribes around it bargain, marry upward and pay. Strength shifts with the rain. A tribe whose wells held in a drought year takes the herds of the tribe whose wells failed, and the tribes nearby remember who did what in a dry year for a generation.

## Herds, Wells and the Round of the Year

Horses and camels carry the Sowides, and sheep and goats feed them. A family moves with its animals along a circuit between owned water: the winter wells in the lee of the stone, the spring grass after the short rains, the summer pastures where the high ground keeps some green, and back. The whole tribe seldom camps together except at the central well, and at the moots held there.

Water is property in the plainest sense. Every well a tribe owns has the tribe's mark cut into the stone above it, and the oldest palms at its oases carry the same mark in the trunk. A small oasis too poor to settle (a pool, a dozen palms, a seep in a ravine) belongs to a tribe exactly as a tent belongs to a family, and the owners' camp is rarely in sight of it. Their riders pass through on their own schedule, and they notice who has been there.

Camp at one of them uninvited and the owners arrive in the morning. What they do depends on you. A traveler who stands up, names his business and offers to buy the night is charged a fair price and sent on. One who has watered a beast, cut a palm or fouled the pool is stripped of his goods and his animals, and goes on foot to the next well if he reaches it. One who draws steel, or who is found at a grave or a herd, does not leave.

## The Stranger and the Price of Passage

A stranger without a sponsor is fair game, and everything the tribes do toward travelers follows from that. A caravan crossing the steppe pays each tribe whose wells and grazing it uses, and each payment buys three things: the right to water at the tribe's wells, a guide (sowid, "rider," the same word the Sowides use for themselves) who knows the next three days of country, and the tribe's token (_kipud_), which a rider of another tribe will honor at the border because the mark on it is the one he would have to answer to.

The price is set tribe by tribe and changes with the season, the size of the caravan, the dowek's mood and the state of the tribe's feuds. A caravan master's real asset is the list of agreements he carries in his head: which dowek, which wells, what price last year, and which tribe is at war with which this season. The Sowides keep no written ledger, and a bargain is witnessed aloud before the elders of both parties and held in their memory. A master who cheats a tribe is not pursued by an army; he finds, three tribes along, that nobody will sell him a guide.

Once a stranger has eaten at a dowek's fire, he is a guest. The Sowides hold the guest-right as hard as the Dunhari hold salt: the tribe protects him against its own kin and against a rival, at the price of a feud if need be, until he has passed beyond the last well of its country. A tribe that gave up a guest would sell nothing to anyone for a generation, and most of its income is selling.

## Raiding and the Feud

The Sowides raid one another, and they raid the settled marches. A raid (jadek) is a tribe's business, planned in moot and led by a war captain: herds are driven off, wells are held or fouled, and captives are taken for ransom (_lusiq_) and not for slaves. A raid that kills is a feud. Blood-feud (badwe) between kindreds runs for generations and has its own ending, a payment of horses or a marriage arranged between the families, and until the ending is made, a man of one line does not eat in the tent of the other.

Raiding is how a young rider earns a place and how a poor tribe lives through a bad year, and it follows rules. Women, children and shamans are not killed. A well is not poisoned, because the next tribe to need it may be your own. A raid on a caravan that has paid the tribe's toll is a raid on the tribe's honor, and the tribe answers it. A raid on a caravan that has paid nobody is not a raid but a collection, and a complaint to the tribe that made it gets the caravan master nowhere.

## The Orqwen

Now and then a leader of exceptional force rises among the tribes, a man or a woman, and forces a number of them together under a personal dictatorship. The title is orqwen, and the union is an orqwenoq. Three of them are remembered across the steppe as great, and the most famous of those is the [[lore-hndrdbnnrs|Storm of the Hundred Banners]]. Nobody on the steppe calls an orqwen's rise a good thing.

An orqwen is made by force. He rides to a tribe's wells and offers its dowek the choice of swearing under open sky or watching the wells fouled; the dowek who swears keeps the tribe and loses the right to refuse a muster, and the dowek who refuses is replaced by a cousin who will swear. Within a few years a hundred banners follow him, fed by the herds of the tribes he has conquered and the plunder of the settled lands. The union exists because he lives. It has no council, no heir and no law beyond his word.

When the orqwen dies, the union breaks within a season. The dowek who swore are free, and the quarrels the orqwen suppressed come due together: the feuds put aside, the herds taken, the wells seized, the captured kin still held by another tribe. A decade of war follows among the very tribes that rode as one, and the tribes that supported the orqwen are blamed by those he broke. The steppe remembers the orqwen as a calamity and keeps the memory deliberately, as a reason to keep its dowek honest. Elders teach the young the names of the tribes that were erased.

## Faith

The Sowides honor the twelve gods of the [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|Āsháian Pantheon]] loosely, laid over older cults of a sky-father and an earth-mother, and served by shamans. Two of the twelve matter to them above the rest: [[affiliation-bahramis|Báhrámiš]], the storm and the protector, and [[affiliation-korsadra|Khóršádrä]], the eternal sky-light. Both fit a people whose supreme presence is the sky and whose gods arrive as weather.

The year's rites follow the herds. A dowek is invested under open sky, with the elders standing and no roof overhead, and a treaty is sworn before lightning when the weather allows, because an oath so sworn is heard by the god who would punish its breaking. The dead are carried to the laying-out ground (_yelos_) and left to the four winds; a grave is dug only for the one who was killed unavenged, so that he stays where his kin can find him.

A shaman keeps the rites, reads the weather, heals, and speaks with the sky. The [[affiliation-strmcltsbhrms|Storm Cults of Báhrámiš]] in Dunhara call the steppe tradition a corrupt variant of the true faith and denounce steppe shamans who preach there. A shaman who crosses the border answers to his dowek first, and the cults' denunciation reaches his tribe as a message it may choose to ignore.

## What the Sowides Get Wrong

A people who cannot unite without a tyrant has no answer to a threat that is bigger than one tribe. When a settled power presses the steppe, each tribe makes its own peace, sells its neighbors' routes and hopes the cost falls elsewhere, and the loss that follows is the very thing the orqwen's rise promises to cure. The Sowides know this, and the debate about it is the oldest in the moot. Nobody has found a way to unite the tribes that does not give a man the power to destroy them.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Sowides what a person owes and he answers in the order of his tent: the household first, then the kin who share its herds, then the tribe and its dowek, then the guest at the fire. The guest is owed protection to the edge of the tribe's country and nothing beyond it. The dead are owed their laying-out and a name spoken over them.

Nothing is owed to a tribe that is not your own, to a stranger without a sponsor, or to a claimant who says he speaks for every tribe at once.
