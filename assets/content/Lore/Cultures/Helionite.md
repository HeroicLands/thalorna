[[place-helionis|Helionis]] is seven cities that agree about the gods, cooperate on defense, and compete about absolutely everything else. A Helionite's loyalty is to his city first and to the idea of Helionis a distant second, and the idea is mostly invoked when someone from outside needs reminding that all seven are civilized and he is not. What a Helionite is measured by is excellence, demonstrated in public, in front of people competent to judge it. That is the whole of the culture and everything else is detail.

## Three Great Cities and Four More

[[affiliation-thyrenae|Thirenae]] is the intellectual capital and holds the great academies. [[affiliation-pelagora|Pelagora]] is the democratic naval power and commands the sea lanes. [[affiliation-kalydria|Kalidria]] is the cultural jewel, with its artists, its theaters and the **Academy of the Silver Veil**. Beside these three stand four more city-states: [[affiliation-theradon|Theradon]], [[affiliation-athenikos|Athenikos]], [[affiliation-korinthea|Korinthea]] and [[affiliation-kostaros2|Kostaros]].

Each city has its own government, laws and customs. They combine against a common threat and for the shared festivals, and they compete ferociously over trade, athletics, intellectual prestige and, from time to time, in open war. A Helionite sees no contradiction: the festivals are real, the rivalry is real, and the rivalry is what keeps the excellence honest.

## Demonstrated, Not Claimed

Public debate on philosophy and politics is a cherished part of civic life, and it is public in the plain sense—held in the agorin, before an audience, with the audience deciding. So is athletics, so is theater, so is the law. The tongue says as much: the stage and the assembly share their vocabulary, because the Helonic peoples hold that a city argues as a chorus sings, many voices to one line.

The consequence is that a Helionite reputation cannot be inherited and cannot be bought. It must be performed, repeatedly, in front of witnesses who know the difference. A rich man's son who cannot argue is a rich man's son who cannot argue; the city says so to his face, and then invites him to dinner anyway.

This makes Helionites exhilarating and exhausting. They interrupt, they demand that a claim be defended on the spot, and they regard a foreigner's reluctance to be publicly examined as either modesty or fraud, and they press him until they know which.

## The Examined Life

A Helionite holds that the examined life is the only one worth living, and the definitions follow from it. **Good is truth defended in public and excellence shown where it can be judged; evil is the wrong done in concealment and left unanswered.** A magistrate who leaves the city before the auditor has cleared his accounts has confessed.

Success is _dokharin_, glory, the thing a citizen owns: a victory at the Games, a speech that carried the assembly, a gymnasium endowed with a good teacher, a chorus that won the prize, a year that took one's name. Failure is not wickedness but dishonor. A citizen stripped of civic honor by the courts is an _átimor_, the un-honored, barred from the assembly, the agorin and the temples while still living among those who barred him, "a man with nothing left to be judged by." A freedman, a metic or a slave cannot win glory in the full sense, because the assembly in which it is counted is closed to them.

What a Helionite values most is excellence performed before competent judges: the argument that survives challenge, the athlete's risk, the play, the law defended in open court. What he values least is virtue claimed and never shown, modesty used as a place to hide, and the person who argues without a first principle, whom the schools call a wanderer, _planthis_. A man who declines to be examined is distrusted; a man who is examined and fails is forgiven faster than one who will not be.

## Honor Paid in Blood and Glory

Personal virtue is held as an achievement and not a restraint, and its proof is what a man risked. A Helionite will take an unnecessary risk for a reputation and will not regard it as unnecessary.

Feuds are conducted in the open and settled in the open, often in court, occasionally by violence, and always with an audience. What a Helionite will not tolerate is a wrong done quietly and left unaddressed, because the concealment is as much the injury as the act.

## The Man Admired

[[being-blnhdrks|Bulelon Hadrikes]], a scholar of Theradon, is the Helionite ideal at forty-one. He comes of a house that has sat in his city's assembly for six generations, and he took the oligarchs' maxim that a citizen's duty is to know more than anyone else literally. For twenty-five years he has read in the chapterhouse library, which holds texts older than the empire, and walked the old battlefields and the ruins of the mage-warlords' seats to find what the record left out. He lectures to full halls, and his students leave knowing what the old texts say and where the record stops. He is gentle, absent, helpless with anything but a text or a student, modest about his findings and immodest about the questions. What he wants is the past kept whole for whoever comes after.

The questions he pursues are the ones the record drops, and it stops, he has found, at the same place every time. A second male exemplar belongs to the histories. The arsir Sothanos Kosamides went out of besieged Thirenae to the _felzos_ (legate) and came back with terms: the walls would be breached at one gate and no other, and the libraries and colleges would stand ([[lore-heladthyrsiege|The Siege of Thirenae]]). The council voted for them. The colleges and the library stand whole, the gate was walled up and never reopened, and the colleges keep the day of the extraction as a day of silence.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-theldnkhldr|Théldina Khalidra]] is the admired Helionite woman, and her admiration is for what the Academy of the Silver Veil makes. Born in Theradon to a minor noble house with more prestige than wealth, she entered the Academy at twelve and spent eight years on music, dance, poetry, rhetoric, etiquette, herblore, law and the art of reading people, emerging at twenty as a fully licensed companion of higher standing than most of the nobility she served. She is poised, witty and socially devastating; she maintains strict boundaries around her profession; and she does not tolerate being talked down to. The Academy's first lesson is to remember what was said at dinner, and by whom. A hetairin is a companion, a counselor and an intelligence-gatherer, and her graduates form an unofficial web that makes Kalidria far more influential than its arms would suggest.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the man's. Bulelon earns his standing by finding and saying the true thing in public, and Théldina by knowing the whole room and what it needs. The cost shows in her too. Something drove her from Helionis, and she carries a quiet tension beneath the polish.

## What the Cities Keep

A Helionite can point to a handful of things that belong to a city or to all seven. Each city shows a keel-stone in its agorin as proof of its founding ship, and each tells the claim against the others at the Games. The **oath-tablets** of the cities hang in the portico behind the heralds' altar at the Sanctuary of the Games, one to a city, each in the city's own script of the day it was sworn. The **herald's person** is written into every city's offices: inviolable, even among enemies. The **bronze copy of the Pelagoran Treaty** stands in Pelagora's agorin, and a Thirenaean student has chiselled one line from it. The **Library of Thirenae** and the colleges around it stand whole beside the walled-up Commissioners' Gate. The **law of Korinthea** is kept at the Temple of Árdavon by a line of High Judges that begins in the year of the Turned Working. And the idea of the **Epistémion**, _sinthasin_, the bringing together of all inquiry, is the nearest thing Helionis has to a charter: a federation of every kind of knowledge, in which a scholar of ethics stands equal to a scholar of magic.

## Faith in the Open

The [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian pantheon]] is kept, and the shared festivals the cities honor together are the nearest thing to a Helionite national institution. The gods are addressed with the same directness Helionites use on each other: petitioned, bargained with, and reproached when they fail to deliver. Temples are civic property and priesthoods are civic offices, frequently held by men who also hold magistracies. A Helionite does not experience religion as a separate sphere with its own authority; it is one more part of the city's public business.

An ordinary person's observance is plain. He keeps the festival days of his city's patron, Marendas in Thirenae, Árdavon in Pelagora and Korinthea, Zunelas in Kalidria, and gives what he can afford at the temple. He exercises at the gymnasium, where the body and the argument are trained together, and takes part in the Games or the chorus when his city sends one, because taking part is a sacred duty. He attends the assembly. In Theradon the academy is the center of spiritual life, because the pursuit of wisdom is treated there as a sacred duty. The mystery cults, secret societies that promise deeper knowledge by stages, are a distinctly Helionite supplement, and some have real political influence.

## The Year of the Cities

The festivals are for something. The **Games**, held at the oldest stadium in the Sanctuary of the Games, exist so that rivals settle their quarrels in running, wrestling and the chariot under a truce the heralds swear at the altar. A runner from Thirenae and a runner from Pelagora race side by side, and a season later their cities may be at war. The **theater festivals** of Kalidria are for beauty and for prestige: a Kalidrian prize is the highest honor a dramatist can win. Marendas's week-long **Festival of Illumination** is for argument, with public lectures on philosophy, theology and natural science. Árdavon's **Feast of Thresholds** is for the renewal of oaths and contracts before his priests. In the darkest nights of winter the faithful of Zunelas deliberately sleep and dream while her priestesses tend the sacred flames. Sélmoros's **Silent Passage** closes the temples to the living while the priests perform rites for the year's dead. The colleges of Thirenae keep the day of the extraction as a day of silence, and Korinthea sends a judge each year to walk the ring of stones around the Sour Plain and record where they stand.

## What Deeds Earn and Wrongs Cost

The rewards are public. Glory is the first: the victory at the Games, the prize at the festival, the speech remembered. Office follows it, by lot or election through the ladder of councilor, magistrate, strátimor and arsir, and the strátimor, the one officer the citizens elect, may be re-elected without limit. The ghimarir pays for a gymnasium from his own purse as an honor, and the man who gives the city a good teacher is remembered at the council table while the man who gives it a banquet is remembered by the caterer. The arsir gives the year his name, so every record in his city is dated by whoever held the office.

Penalties climb the same ladder downward. The mildest is the face-to-face rebuke, which is delivered in public and followed by an invitation to dinner. The auditor's hold comes next: a magistrate may not leave the city until his accounts are cleared. A trial of honor follows, judged at the Temple of Árdavon in Korinthea, where it can end in a duel. _Atimira_, the stripping of civic honor by the courts, is the last. Wars between cities are fought, and the herald returns the dead; a city that harms a herald is struck out of the Games, and none has been.

## Where the Assembly Ends and the Temple Begins

Authority is the city's. The assembly of citizens by descent is sovereign. A council chosen by lot prepares its business for a single year and no one sits twice. Magistrates are elected or allotted to the markets, walls, treasury and courts, and an auditor examines each one's accounts. The _nomasfilir_ guards the laws and may halt a decree of the assembly that contradicts them. The herald speaks for the assembly and the city abroad. Priests are civic officers, and the line between them and magistrates is deliberately thin. In Korinthea the council writes the rules and the temple's judges say what they mean when two men disagree, and the High Judge's judgment is final in most cases. In Pelagora the assembly votes on war, trade and law. In Thirenae a council of senior scholars and the heads of the oldest families governs, and a reputation for learning is the road to power. In Kalidria the patron families rule by paying for what the city comes to see.

The Thordimalon (the Arcane Order) stands outside this order and is kept at arm's length. A Helionite philosopher may study arcane theory and debate magical philosophy without joining the Arcane Order, so long as he does not practice, and the line between theory and practice is where the tension lives. Conflicts between cities have no tribunal; they are settled by the Games' truce, by diplomacy, or by war.

## What Becomes of the Dead

The dead of a Helionite city belong to it. After a battle between two cities the loser's herald walks onto the field under truce and asks for the dead, the winner gives them up, and the herald takes them home to their own rites. That custom is why wars between Helionite cities leave no blood-field, and it states the belief underneath it: the dead are received by the rites of their own city, said by its own priests at its own altars. Temples are civic property, so the rite that settles a death is a public act, and the assembly funds it. A city that does not perform its rites leaves its dead unreceived. The plain where a legion and Korinthea's levy died together in one afternoon is the case in point, because no herald could cross it to bring the levy home and no Korinthean assembly has voted to say aloud what happened there. The truce the cities wrote after the War of the Harbors, [[lore-heladtruce|the Truce of the Games]], makes the return of the dead one of its terms.

Philosophers argue about everything, the afterlife included, and the argument changes no rite. Embalmers of the Morticians' Guild prepare the dead with herbs and rituals believed to protect the spirit, and a Helionite goes to burial with the rites his city keeps. Beyond the rites, a Helionite hopes to be remembered, because remembered glory is the nearest thing his culture has to a second life: the name on the arsir's list, the stone in the sanctuary, the speech that survives.

This belief shapes an ordinary day. A Helionite spends money in public. He pays for a chorus or a gymnasium or a bench, because the city's memory of him is his afterlife, and the rich compete to be remembered as the one who gave. He will not let a wrong stand unanswered, because concealment is what leaves the dead unreceived. And he fears _atimira_ more than death, because a person barred from the temples has no one to speak for him at the rites that receive the dead.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the city's rites and the herald's crossing. A priest of Sélmoros may be asked for the settlement rite that lays the unreceived dead, but a temple is civic property and the rite needs an assembly's vote, and to vote is to say aloud what happened. The dead of the Sour Plain stand on the field on the anniversary of the battle, worn down to the posture of men waiting for an order that never came, and between anniversaries predators gather where unreceived dead are.

The dead reach the living through memory more than visitation. In a civic temple the city, not the priest, decides whether a rite is paid for, and the family pays the embalmer. Nothing in the cities' practice has the dead answer the living.

## What Is Owed

A Helionite owes the city first, plainly and without embarrassment and ahead of family, because the city is what makes a man capable of anything worth doing. Then the household. Then the friends bound to him, which in Helionis is a formal and serious category.

And he owes the truth an argument. A Helionite holds that a person who lets a false thing stand unchallenged in public has failed at the basic civic duty. Dinner with three of them takes six hours.

In return the city owes the citizen a voice in the assembly, a place in the lot for office, the protection of the walls and the militia he serves in, and, abroad, the herald and the return of his body if he falls. A freedman works and trades and is barred from the assembly for life. A metic pays tax and stands the levy, and is owed a market in which to work; he has no vote and cannot own land. A slave owes labor and has no standing before the law except through an owner. The student from abroad who stays on is a metic: the most prestigious education in the west carries no vote in the city that gave it.

## The Weak

Care is a matter of friendship and patronage before it is a matter of law. The patron families who hold Kalidria, Athenikos, Korinthea and Kostaros pay for festivals, temples and public benches as a mark of their standing, and the public feasting of the festivals feeds the city. The High Judge of Korinthea has founded the Refuge of Árdavon to shelter refugees and victims of injustice, and the refuge sorts the deserving from the rest by the judge's own measure. The hospices of Morvana shelter the dying. A household takes in its widow and its orphan, and the friends bound to a man, a formal category in Helionis, stand behind his dependents when he cannot.

The limit is where the city stops counting. The destitute citizen has the assembly and the festival; the destitute metic has nothing but his own purse.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A citizen who is injured keeps his place on the roll and his vote, because the vote belongs to the citizen by descent and not to the soldier. He loses the militia, and the family and friends who bound themselves to him now carry him. The old are placed by the same logic: a man too frail for the line still speaks in the assembly, teaches in the gymnasium or the academy, and sits in the council if the lot falls to him once. Standing, in Helionis, is exercised in the voice, and the voice outlasts the arm. Scarcity changes the order in which the city feeds its people. In a siege a city eats what its walls and fleet hold. Thirenae held out through a winter and then took terms, and the colleges and the library stood afterward because the arsir bargained them out.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Teaching is the city's pride. The gymnasium is kept by a patron at his own cost, the academies teach mathematics, natural philosophy, rhetoric, medicine and the theory of magic, and the Epistémion (the Academy of Knowledge) keeps its original seat in Thirenae and a chapterhouse in Theradon. The Academy of the Silver Veil takes girls at twelve and trains them for eight years. Defense is the citizen's own: each citizen serves in the militia or the fleet, and in Pelagora every voter has a personal stake in the voyage he votes for. The strátimor commands. The herald and the truce keep the cities from destroying one another, and the dead come home.

Shelter is the household's and the friend's. When a family cannot keep its young, the friends bound to it, the patron families and the temples are the recourse, and the academies take students from abroad as metics.

## House, Clan and Friends

The house is the father's, and the clan name says whose line a man is of. A Helionite child is given a name chosen for how it will sound in the assembly, tested by saying it across a room, and among friends the given name is cut to its first two syllables with the stress kept. A citizen is a citizen by descent, so membership is the line of fathers and the roll that records it. The household includes the wife, the children, the freedmen and the slaves under the master's roof, and beyond the household stand the friends bound to a man, who are a formal category, and the city, which comes first.

The ladder of a life follows the city's ladder. A boy grows through the gymnasium and the militia into the assembly. A girl enters a household or, if she has the gifts, the Academy of the Silver Veil at Kalidria at twelve, which licenses a companion at twenty. A man's standing rises by lot and election through the council and the magistracies, and the strátimor can be re-elected without limit. The arsir holds the year for a year and hands it back. The old are teachers and speakers, and the dying are the embalmer's and the priest's.

## What the Agorin Does Not Say

Every real system has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Helionites' is the wrong that the agorin cannot see. A culture that proves everything in public has no way to prove what no one witnessed. The Sour Plain is the case: a Korinthean mage-warlord loosed a working that broke a legion and turned back on his own city's levy, and the dead of both armies were left where they fell because no herald could cross the ground. The warlord is named in no Helionite or Vylarian record. Korinthea's assembly lost its standing, the Temple took the law into its keeping, and in all the years since no assembly has voted to say aloud what the city did there. The wrong is concealed, and concealment is the offense the Helionites say they cannot tolerate.

The sin sits beside it. Helionite mages made the Arcane Order necessary, and the cities are quiet about which Helionite masters the Republic kept when the war was over. Pelagora treated alone and its ships blockaded the cities still fighting, and a Thirenaean spits on the pavement beside the bronze copy of the Treaty while a Pelagoran spits back.

The blind spot also runs through the ladder of standing. The examined life is open to those who can enter the agorin, and the metic, the freedman and the slave cannot. Thoughtful Helionites argue whether the examined life can be called civic when so many of the city's people are barred from the places where it is lived. High Judge [[being-dumarsrfrs|Dumares Rafiros]] of Korinthea shows the failure in a single man: a priest of conviction who judges the poor harshly, finds a moral failing beneath every material one, and runs a refuge content if most of its charges emerge reformed by his standards.

## Women, and the Silver Veil

Women's standing varies sharply by city. In some they are confined to domestic life; in others they take part in intellectual and even political life; no rule holds across all seven.

The hetairin occupy a position with no equivalent elsewhere: educated companions trained in music, philosophy, rhetoric and the social arts, and the most socially mobile women in Helionite society. The Academy of the Silver Veil at Kalidria trains them, and its graduates serve as companions, advisors and very effective political agents across the whole [[place-midhalnrgn|Mídhalión]] region. A hetairin is not a courtesan as other peoples use the word, and Helionites are impatient with the confusion.

## Learning, and the Order That Came Out of It

The academies and gymnasia are where the city reproduces itself, and the philosophical tradition here is the oldest and most continuous in Ankaris. Mathematics, natural philosophy, rhetoric, medicine and the theory of magic are all taught, argued over and revised in public.

The [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Thordimalon]] was born out of that tradition at the [[affiliation-panepistmm|Epistémion]], and Helionites have complicated feelings about what it became. They are proud of the parentage and uneasy about the child—an institution that claims authority over knowledge, in the one place on the continent where knowledge has always been settled by argument.
