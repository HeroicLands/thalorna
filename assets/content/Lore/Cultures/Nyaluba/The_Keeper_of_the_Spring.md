The [[affiliation-ekunda|Ékunda]] keep a perennial spring in the southern savannahs where the herders of the five clans water in the dry season, and their seat, [[place-ekundavlg|Ékunda Village]], stands at it. Three generations ago, in the drought-war, famine pushed neighboring tribes into the [Sengala]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} country and the regiments were called out against them ([[lore-nyalubaclt|Nyáluba]]). **The Keeper of the Spring** is the story the Ékunda tell about that year. A water-warden tells it at night by the spring to whoever has watered there, and it is a dilemma tale: it stops on a question and leaves the question to the fire.

It names the keeper, [Dombesa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the [Ruwozi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} herd-boy [Kisado]{.name data-type="being/npc"} and the regiment's captain [Gaduno]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and it does not name the hungry family, because the teller says the fire does not keep their names and the record does. What Dombesa decided is held by the griot who keeps the Ékunda's lines. The story never says it.

## The Telling

You've watered. Good. Your beasts have drunk and you've drunk and you've filled your skins, and you paid what's fair, and I asked you your name and your father's and where you slept, and I said them back. Now sit down, because that's the other half of watering at Ékunda. You get the water, and then you get the story about the water.

Look at it. Go on, look. That's the spring. You can hear it from here if the cattle shut up. It comes out of the rock under the fig there, cold as the morning, and runs down into the three pools we cut, and out of the third pool into the reeds, and the reeds go on to the river, two days off. It has never dried. Not once. Not in the oldest line the griots have of us, and that goes a long way back, because the people who sit by a spring that never dries are worth remembering.

It has never dried. I didn't say it never ran thin.

Three generations ago, in my grandmother's mother's time, the rains didn't come. You know the story of the dry years, everybody does. One year the rains came late and small. The next year they came later and smaller. The third year they didn't come at all. The grass stood up white and broke when the cattle walked on it, and the pans that hold water in the wet season were cracked clay you could put your arm into up to the shoulder, and the herds came here, because where else would they go? Everybody came here.

And the spring ran thin.

Not dry. Thin. You see where the water comes out under the fig? There's a lip of rock there, and in an ordinary dry season the water comes over it the width of two hands, like this, and you can't stop it with your palm. That year it came over it the width of one finger. You could stop it with your thumb, and the old ones say the children did, to see, and were beaten for it.

The elders sat down in the shade and reckoned. That's what we do, us Ékunda. Other people have cattle or gold or the river. We have the spring and the reckoning. They reckoned how much came over the lip from one dawn to the next, and how much a cow drinks, and a man, and a child, and they said: this much. This many mouths a day, and no more. And they set the turns: which herds on which day, which camp, which travelers. And that was the law of the spring that year, and the one who kept it was Dombesa.

She was the water-warden that year, Dombesa of the Ékunda. Not an elder yet, not quite. Old enough that the elders gave her the spring, and young enough that she could stand at it all day in the heat and still be standing at dark. She kept the turns. She kept the count. Every herd that came, she asked the herder the three things and said them back, and told him his day, and he went away and waited for it, because they all knew the count, and they all knew she was keeping it right.

I'll tell you how the turns went, because you're sitting at the spring and you should know. The day was cut by the fig's shadow. When it lay across the first pool, that was the herds. When it lay across the second, that was the households of the village, a skin each and no more. When it reached the reeds, that was the travelers, and the Sengala with their bundles, and anybody else. And the night was nobody's, so the spring could fill the pools again for the morning. Dombesa kept the count with stones. A row of them on the lip of the rock, one for every herd that had a day, and every morning she moved one to the other side, and everybody who came could look at the row and see where his stone was. That's not writing. Don't let a northerner tell you it's writing. It's a row of stones.

And her own mother's goats waited their day like anybody's. The story is very particular about that. There were people in the village who said the warden's household could have a skin more, just one, and nobody would know, and Dombesa said, "The spring would know, and so would I, and I'm the one standing at it." So her mother's goats waited. Two of them died waiting. She said their names at the burying, the way you do for a household's beasts, and then she went back and moved the next stone.

Some of them cried, grown men, Ruwozi with the lion-tooth on them, standing at the third pool with their cattle dying behind them, and she said, "Your day is the day after next," and they went away. She didn't cry. The story says she didn't cry once that year. My grandmother said she cried every night, at home, where the spring couldn't see.

Now. The story is about one evening. Everything I've told you is just so you know where you're sitting.

On that evening, three came to the spring.

The first was a boy of the Ruwozi. His name was Kisado. He was about the age where a Ruwozi boy is waiting for his tooth, not there yet. He came walking in out of the white grass with twelve cows, and they were walking skeletons, and he was a walking skeleton behind them, and he told Dombesa this: those twelve were what was left of his mother's herd. There had been ninety. His father was dead of the fever that comes with the dry. His mother was sick at their camp, two days west. His uncles were with the regiments. He had walked the twelve here alone, and it had taken him six days, and if they didn't drink tonight, four of them wouldn't stand up tomorrow, and if four of them didn't stand up, then his mother's line had eight cows, and you know what a Ruwozi line with eight cows is. It's a line that has to go and ask.

And it was his right. Hear that. The third finger of the Pact says the grass and the rivers and the rains are shared, and a Ruwozi boy watering his mother's herd at an Ékunda spring in a bad year is the third finger. It's the whole of the third finger. Dombesa had his name in her mouth already, his and his father's and where he'd slept, and she knew his day was not tonight. His day was two days off. But she looked at the four cows that weren't going to stand up, and she didn't send him away. She said, "Wait."

The second came an hour after, just at dark: a company of a regiment. Forty men, Ruwozi most of them, a few Sengala with them to show the way, and they'd been on the move for days, and they were dry to the bone, because the country they were moving through had nothing in it to drink. Their captain was a man named Gaduno, and the story is fair to him, so I'll be fair to him. He was a good captain. His men loved him. He'd kept them alive through a dry season in a dry country with nothing in it, and he'd done what he was sent to do.

What were they sent to do? You know. The hungry had come in out of the north and the west, into the leopard's country, the people who weren't of the five. They'd come because their own country had nothing left in it, and they'd come with their children and their old people and no cattle, because their cattle were dead. And they'd taken from the Sengala fields, because there was something in the Sengala fields. And the Sengala called, and a spear against one is a spear against five, and the regiments went.

That's what Gaduno was doing. He was the Pact's spear, and he'd been putting it where the Pact said.

Gaduno came up to the spring with his forty men and said to Dombesa: "We need water. Tonight. All of us. We're following a band of them south, they're a day ahead of us, and they're making for this water. If my men drink tonight, we catch them tomorrow. If my men don't drink tonight, half of them don't walk tomorrow, and the band gets away into the grass, and next month they're in somebody's fields again."

And Dombesa asked him the three things, and said them back. And she said, "Wait."

And the third came last, in the full dark, so that at first nobody saw them. They came out of the reeds by the third pool, where the water goes out, not where the people come in. That's how the story knows who they were before anybody said it: they came the way you come when you don't want to be seen.

A woman. An old man, so thin that the woman was holding him up. Two children, one walking and one carried. That was all. No cattle. No spears. Nothing on their heads. They were the band Gaduno was following? No. Listen. Not the band. Some of the band. The ones who couldn't keep up. The band had gone on into the grass, and these had fallen behind, and they'd smelled the water, or heard it, and come.

Gaduno's men saw them and stood up. Forty men stood up at once, and the sound of the spears coming up off the ground was like a herd getting up.

And Dombesa stood between.

She didn't say anything, the story says. She just stood there with the spring behind her and the forty in front of her and the four of them at the edge of the reeds. And Gaduno said, "Warden, move."

She said, "This is the spring. Nobody puts a spear in anybody at the spring."

And that's true, you know. That's the oldest thing about us. Nobody kills at Ékunda water. Not in the feuds before the Pact, not after. It's the one place.

Gaduno knew it. He said, "Then they drink after we're gone. Or they go back in the reeds. Or they go on south and we catch them tomorrow where there's no spring. That's up to them. But not tonight, and not before my men."

So now you have the three, and they're all at the spring, in the dark, and the spring is coming over the lip the width of one finger.

Here's the count, because the story gives you the count, and it's always the same at every telling, because it's the Ékunda telling it and we don't tell a count wrong. The elders had reckoned the spring that day. What had come over the lip since dawn was in the three pools. It was enough for one of them. If it all went to one, there'd be just enough. If it went to two, both would get some, and some of the cows wouldn't stand up, or some of the men wouldn't walk, or one of the children wouldn't wake. If it went to all three, it went to nobody.

That was the water there was. By dawn there'd be more, a day's worth, and that was all spoken for already by the turns. Dombesa had a whole dry season of turns in her head, every herd, every day, and every one of them was somebody's last twelve cows.

Now, what did she do first? That's the bit everybody remembers, even the ones who forget the rest.

She went to the woman at the edge of the reeds. She walked straight past Gaduno's spears to her, and stood in front of her, and asked her the three things.

"What is your name?"

The woman didn't understand. She didn't speak our tongue. Dombesa tried the trade-talk the Sengala use up north, and the woman knew a little of that, and so did Dombesa, a little, from the traders who water here. So she asked it again in that, slow.

"Name?"

And the woman said her name.

"Father?"

And the woman said her father's name.

"Where did you sleep?"

And the woman pointed back into the grass, the way she'd come, and said a word that meant nothing to anybody there, the name of some place in her own country, a long way off, that was nothing now but dust.

And Dombesa said them back. All three, out loud, in front of the forty. Then she did it with the old man. Then she did it with the child who could walk, who was too frightened to answer, so the woman answered for him. Then the baby, and the woman answered for the baby too.

And when she'd said the fourth one back, Gaduno said to her, quiet, not angry: "You know what you've done."

And she said, "Yes."

Because you know what she'd done. You all know. I asked you your three when you came, and I said them back, and now I've got you. You're entered. A name once said back can't be unsaid. She'd taken four people who weren't of the five, who weren't of anything, who'd been stealing out of Sengala fields, and she'd made them guests at the spring. In front of the Pact's own spear.

I'm not going to tell you their names. Don't ask me. The story doesn't keep them, because the fire doesn't keep anything properly. But I'll tell you this, and you can go and check it: the griot who keeps the Ékunda's lines has them. All four. Their names and their fathers' names and the word for where the woman slept. They were said back at the spring in front of forty men and a Ruwozi boy and a warden of the Ékunda, and so they were entered, and the griot has them, and he says them when he says the year the spring ran thin. Strangers' names in an Ékunda line. Nobody can take them out.

That's what she did first.

So there she is. Midnight, now. The spring coming over the lip the width of one finger. The water in the three pools enough for one.

On one side of her, a Ruwozi boy with his mother's last twelve cows, four of them going down, and the third finger of the Pact in his hand. His right.

In front of her, forty men of the regiment, the Pact's own spear, the second finger, a spear against one is a spear against five, dry to the bone, and tomorrow they have to catch the rest of the band or the band is in somebody else's fields next month and somebody else's children go hungry. Their need.

And behind her, at the edge of the reeds, four people who aren't of the five, whose names she has just put in her own mouth. Her guests.

Water for one.

Who did she give it to?

That's where it stops. That's where it's meant to stop. I don't tell you. Nobody at this spring tells you. You tell me.

Go on. You've got all night, and the water's not going anywhere.

No? Then I'll tell you how the fires usually go, so you've got somewhere to start.

When there are Ruwozi at the spring, they say: the boy. It was his right. It was the third finger. A Pact that doesn't hold in a bad year isn't a Pact, it's weather. And his mother's line would have had to go and ask, and a Ruwozi line that has to go and ask has lost something it doesn't get back in one lifetime.

When there are Sengala, they say: the men. It was their fields. It was their children who'd be hungry next month. A spear against one is a spear against five, and a spear that can't walk isn't one.

When there are Kolembozi, and there aren't often, because they've got their own water, they say: ask the spring. There's a guide in that rock older than any of us, and Dombesa should have gone down to it and asked, and done what it said. And then somebody says the spring's guide doesn't care which of them drinks, it only cares that it gets its share, and then the Kolembozi say: then you didn't ask it properly.

When there are Lobwezi, they say nothing for a long time, and then they say: what does the record hold? And somebody says the story doesn't say. And they say: then the story is no use to us. And then they go to sleep, and in the morning one of them asks me again, quietly, whether I'm sure I don't know.

When there are Ngonzi, they listen to everybody and they don't say anything at all, and you never find out what they think. Which is the Ngonzi.

And then there's always somebody who says the hungry, the four of them. And everybody goes quiet. Because she'd asked them the three things. Because you can't ask a guest his name and say it back and then give the water to someone else in front of him. That's not a host. That's someone who keeps a spring. And then somebody else says: then she shouldn't have asked them. And the first one says: you'd have had her not ask? And that's the night gone.

And then there's the one who says the third finger. Says the grass and the rivers and the rains are shared. Among the five. And then he stops, like everybody stops at that word, and looks at the four by the reeds, and says: or is it? And nobody answers him. The Lubemo's never answered him either.

So now you know how the fires go.

What do we say, us Ékunda? We say: whoever's turn it was. That's the law of the spring. The turns are the turns. And then we argue all night about whose turn it was, because it was nobody's turn, that was the whole trouble, the boy's day was two days off and the regiment had no day and the four had never had a day in their lives.

You want to know what she did. Everybody does. I said I won't tell you, and I won't, and it's not because I don't know. It's because it's not the story's to tell. It's the record's.

If you want it, go and sit in front of the griot who keeps our lines. He'll say the year the spring ran thin. He'll say the boy's name and his father's, and the captain's, and Dombesa's, and the four strangers' names that nobody else in the world has kept. And then he'll say what she did, in one line, short, the way the record always is.

And then you'll know, and you won't be any happier. That's the other thing about the record.

Go on. Put your head down. Your beasts are drinking. There's enough tonight. There's been enough every night since.
