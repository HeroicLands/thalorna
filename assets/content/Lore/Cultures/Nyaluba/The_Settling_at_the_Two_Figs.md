A [Kolembozi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} who loses a relative to a crocodile speaks of that crocodile by name and lineage, and settles the matter as a family affair rather than avenging it ([[affiliation-nyalbcroc|Crocodile Totem]]). **The Settling at the Two Figs** is a story told at the fish-camp fires of the eastern wetlands about one such settling, at the [Crossing of the Two Figs]{.name data-type="place/feature"}. It is a fire story and says so: the [[skill-griot|griot]] of the Kolembozi holds a short entry of the settling, which the teller gives first and then leaves behind, and the rest belongs to whoever is telling it. The version here is told by a woman of a fish-camp downriver of the crossing. Other fires give the brother a different age, or make the girl's mistake a different one, and some end it on the bank and never reach the flood.

## The Telling

Pull the pot off, it's done, and sit where the smoke isn't. You want the Two Figs. Everybody wants the Two Figs, and then they argue with me about it, so let me give you the part nobody can argue with first.

This is what the griot holds. I had it from the griot himself, and I'll say it the way he says it, because this fire knows it the way he says it.

> Lolewa, daughter of Gongwelo of the Kolembozi, was taken at the Crossing of the Two Figs by Nyandaya, daughter of Mbeshoya, who holds the reach below the left fig. The matter was settled between the two lines before the Guardian of the crossing and the griot of the Kolembozi. It is closed.

That's the record. It's short because the record is always short. Everything else is me, and I'm only a woman who smokes fish.

You know the Two Figs, some of you. There's a reach of the big water down past the papyrus where the channel narrows and runs deep and slow, and on the near bank there's a fig, and on the far bank there's a fig, and they lean at each other over the water like two old men who've been arguing since before your grandmother's grandmother and haven't finished. That's the crossing. It's the only place on that stretch you can take a canoe straight over without going half a day round by the shallows, and so everybody uses it, and so it has a Guardian, because anything everybody uses has a Guardian.

The Guardian of the Two Figs, in this story, is [Lalumo]{.name data-type="being/npc"}. He's an old man in the story and he's an old man every time I tell it. He holds that crossing and only that one, the way they all do, because what keeps you alive at one crossing is not what keeps you alive at the next, and a Guardian who mixed up two crossings would be drowning people by the second dry season.

And this is how you cross at the Two Figs. Listen, because if you ever cross there you'll want it. You go at first light and not before. You knock your paddle on the side of the canoe three times, hard, so the water hears wood. You say your mother's mother's name and her mother's, out loud, to the water. You put a fish in, a whole one, gutted, upstream of the left fig, and you let it go down past the root. Then you cross, and you don't stop in the middle, and you don't trail your hand.

That's the protocol. It's not hard. Children learn it before they learn to swim.

Now. [Gongwelo]{.name data-type="being/npc"} kept a fish-camp on the near bank above the figs, him and his wife and his old mother and his two children. The girl was [Lolewa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and she was at the age where a Kolembozi girl is about to go into the regiment with her cohort, which means she was the age where she knew everything. The boy was [Timbelo]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, and he was younger, about the age where he followed her around and she let him because it was useful to have someone to carry things.

They crossed every day, those two. Gongwelo had traps on the far side, in the backwater behind the right fig, and the children went over at first light and knocked and said the names and put the fish in and crossed and checked the traps and came back. Every day. You do a thing every day and it gets small in your head. That's not in the record either, but every one of you knows it.

One evening Lolewa remembered she'd left the good knife on the far bank, by the traps. Her father's knife. The iron one that came down from the north through the leopard people and cost him a season's salt. She'd left it on a root and come home without it.

You know what she should have done. She should have told her father and let him be angry and gone over for it at first light. Everybody at this fire knows it and so did she. But it was her father's knife, and she was the age she was, and she thought: it's just over there. I can see the root it's on.

She didn't take the boy. That's the one thing she did right. He saw her going down to the canoe and he said, "It's not first light," and she said, "Go and help Mother," and he went, because she was older.

She didn't knock. There's no knocking at dusk, because there's no crossing at dusk. She didn't say the names. She didn't put a fish in. She just pushed off.

I'm not going to tell you that part slowly. You know what happened. She was in the middle and the water came up beside the canoe all at once, the way it does, and the canoe went over, and she was in the water, and then she wasn't.

The boy was on the bank by then. He'd gone to help his mother and then he'd come back, because he was the age he was, too. He saw it. He saw her go, and he saw what took her, and he saw it again a little after, lying out on the mud below the left fig as if nothing had happened, with the dusk on its back.

He ran up to the camp screaming, and Gongwelo came down with a spear, and the boy pointed, and there she was, lying on the mud.

And Gongwelo stood there on the bank with the spear in his hand and looked at her a long time, and then he said, "That's Nyandaya."

You see? That's the part the Ruwozi never understand when they hear this. He knew her. Of course he knew her. He'd crossed that water every day of his life, and his father before him. That reach below the left fig had been held by the same line since the figs were young. [Nyandaya]{.name data-type="being/creature"} was the one there now, the big one with the pale scar over her eye, and her mother before her was [Mbeshoya]{.name data-type="being/creature"}, and before Mbeshoya was the one they called the Old One of the Left Fig, who nobody living had seen but everybody's grandmother had.

And Gongwelo knew more than that, because there was an old account between his line and that one. In his grandmother's time, the Old One of the Left Fig had taken his grandmother's brother, [Mashelo]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, at the same crossing, and the family had settled it, and the griot had entered it, and it had been closed for two generations.

So Gongwelo looked at Nyandaya on the mud, and he knew whose daughter she was, and whose granddaughter, and what was between their two houses already. And he put the spear down.

Timbelo picked it up.

Now some of you tell it that the father knocked him down. Some of you tell it the mother came and took the spear. I tell it the way I was told it: the boy picked up the spear and stood there with it, ten years old and shaking, and his father didn't touch him. His father said to him, quietly, "Who is she?"

And the boy said, "She killed Lolewa."

And his father said, "That's what she did. Who is she?"

The boy didn't answer. So his father answered for him. "She's Nyandaya. Daughter of Mbeshoya. Granddaughter of the Old One. She holds the reach below the left fig, and her line has held it since the figs. Everybody who crosses here crosses under her. Your aunt crosses under her. The [Ndalude]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} children upriver cross under her. If you put that spear in her, the Guardian has to make the pact at this crossing new, with whatever comes up the river to hold it after her, and that takes a lifetime, and maybe two, and every child who crosses here while it's being made is crossing under nothing."

The boy said, "I don't care."

And his father said, "I know. That's why you're not going to do it."

And he took the spear back, gently, the way you take a thing from someone who's asleep, and they went up to the camp, and the mother was on the ground in front of the house with her face in the dirt, and the old grandmother was sitting beside her with one hand on her back, and nobody said anything all night.

In the morning Gongwelo sent for the Guardian and the griot.

I'll tell you about the settling, because it's the middle of the story and it's the part that's the same at every fire, more or less, because it was said before a griot. You'll hear me change my voice for it. That's not me being clever. That's me trying not to get it wrong.

The Guardian came down the bank, old Lalumo, with his gourds and his staff. The griot of the Kolembozi came from the big camp downriver, in a canoe, and he came the long way round by the shallows, the way a careful man comes to a crossing that's just taken someone. The family came down: Gongwelo, and his wife with her head shaved, and his old mother, and the boy last, because they made him come.

They put the fish in. Not one fish. A whole basket, a season's best, gutted and laid in the water one by one upstream of the left fig, and the old man knocked his staff on the root of the fig three times, and said the names. Then he went down the bank to the water's edge, right to the mud, where nobody goes, and squatted there with his gourds and talked to the river in the voice the Guardians use, the one you can't follow even when you know the words. A long time. Long enough that the sun came round the fig.

And she came up. I'm telling you what the story says. Out in the deep water below the left fig, the scar over her eye, just the eyes and the nose of her, and she lay there in the water and looked at them.

Then Gongwelo stood up and spoke to her. And this is it. This is what he said, and the griot heard it, and so it's said the same everywhere, near enough:

And the Guardian said what the crocodile said, which I can't tell you, because only he heard it, and he never said it to anyone but the griot, and the griot doesn't say it either. All the record holds is that the Guardian said it was accepted.

Then the griot spoke. He said the two lines out loud, one after the other: the line of Gongwelo back through Fodemi and Kobesa, and the line of Nyandaya back through Mbeshoya to the Old One. Then he said Mashelo, and the old settling, and Lolewa, and the new one. Then he said: "These are entered together. This is one account."

And then everybody who stood there of the family had to say the word. That's how it's done. You say it, and then it's closed, and nobody can open it again.

Gongwelo said it. "Settled."

His wife said it. "Settled." She could hardly get it out, but she said it.

The old grandmother said it. "Settled." She'd said it before, you see. She was the one who'd been a girl when Mashelo was taken. She'd stood on that same bank. That's why she said it first of the women, and why she said it so plain.

And then it was the boy.

And Timbelo looked at the crocodile lying in the water with his sister in her, and he didn't say anything.

They waited. The griot waited. The Guardian waited. You can't make a person say it. If you make them say it, it isn't said.

And the boy turned round and walked up the bank and away.

Now there are fires, upriver mostly, where they make him come back and say it, right there, his father dragging him by the arm. I don't tell it that way. My grandmother didn't tell it that way. At this fire he walks up the bank and doesn't say it, and the griot looks at Gongwelo, and Gongwelo says, "He's not of age. It's my word for the house." And the griot nods and says, "It is entered that the father spoke for the house." And that's how the record has it. You can ask. It says: settled between the two lines. It doesn't say who in the house said what. That part's ours.

The crocodile went down, and the water closed over her, and that was the settling.

Afterwards Gongwelo went round by the shallows, the long way, and fetched the knife off the root where she'd left it. It was still there. Of course it was. He brought it home and put it on his belt and wore it every day after, and nobody asked him why.

So. That's the middle. Here's where it gets long, so somebody put a stick on.

Timbelo grew up. He went into the regiment with his cohort. He learned the reed canoe like the rest of them, better than most, and he married, and had a girl of his own, and he fished, and he did everything a Kolembozi man does, except one thing. He never crossed at the Two Figs.

Not once. He went the long way round, by the shallows, half a day there and half a day back, every time he had business on the far side. People laughed at him for it. His own father told him he was making a fool of himself. His wife told him he was making a fool of her, which was worse. He didn't care. He said, "I didn't say the word, and I'm not going to cross under her as though I had."

You see what he was doing? He wasn't hunting her. He wasn't lifting a spear. He'd heard his father. But he wasn't going to put himself under her either. He kept to the edge of it, like a man who stands outside a house he won't go into and won't burn.

When his own girl was small enough to ride on his hip, she asked him about it. Children do. They'd been the long way round, half a day there in the sun, with her asleep on him most of it and cross when she woke, and on the way back she looked across the water at the Two Figs leaning together, and at the other canoes going straight over in the time it takes to eat, and she said, "Why don't we go there?"

And he didn't tell her about Lolewa. Not then. He said, "Because I owe a word there that I haven't said."

She said, "What word?"

He said, "When I say it, you'll hear it."

That's all. She was too small to ask the next thing, and he was glad of it. His wife, who was sitting in the front of the canoe, heard every bit of that, and she didn't say anything either, but the story says she paddled harder the rest of the way home.

His father died. The griot said his line at the burying, and when he got to Lolewa in it he said her name and the taking and the word "settled," and Timbelo stood there and heard it and still didn't say it. And the years went round.

Then there was the flood year. The old ones here remember it, or their mothers did: the year the rains wouldn't stop, and the big water came up over the papyrus and took the fish-camps and made the whole country one brown sea from here to the trees. The shallows weren't shallows any more. The long way round was gone. There was no long way round. There was the water, and the Two Figs standing up out of it with their heads together, and that was all.

And Tímbelo's girl was sick. Burning up, the way they do, and the charms weren't touching it, and the spirit-speaker who could deal with that kind of thing was on the far side, at the big camp on the rise, and the girl was getting worse by the hour.

You know what's coming. Everybody knows. That's not why you tell a story. You tell it for how.

He waited for first light. Give him that. He sat up all night with her burning against him and he waited for first light, because he wasn't a fool, whatever people said.

You know what that night was like, some of you. The water up under the floor of the house, and the sound of it, which never stops, and every so often something big going past in the dark, a tree, a dead cow, a roof. He sat there with her and listened to it all night. The story says he talked to her the whole time, though she couldn't hear him, and that what he talked about was Lolewa. All of it. The knife, and the dusk, and the mud below the left fig, and the word he hadn't said. He told the whole of it to a girl who was too sick to know her own name, because she was the only one he could tell it to who wouldn't answer him.

And at first light he carried her down to the water, where the bank had been, and put her in the canoe, and pushed off, and paddled out to the place between the two figs, where the water was going past now fast and brown and full of branches.

And he knocked the paddle on the side three times. And he said the names, his mother's mother's and hers. And he didn't have a fish, because the flood had taken the camp and the smoking racks and everything in them. So he took the knife off his belt, his father's knife, the iron one from the north, the very one, and he dropped it in the water upstream of the left fig, and let it go down. That's what he had.

Then he crossed.

And in the middle, where the water's deepest, it came up beside the canoe.

She was old by then. The scar over her eye had gone white. She came up right beside him, close enough to touch, and lay there in the brown water with the branches going past, and looked at him. And he looked at her. The girl was lying in the bottom of the canoe, too sick to know anything.

I don't know how long. Nobody knows, because nobody was there. That's the thing you have to understand about this part. Nobody was there.

Then she went down. Just went down, the way they do, no fuss, and the water closed, and he paddled on across with the girl, and got her up the rise to the spirit-speaker, and she lived. She's a grandmother now, somewhere upriver. Somebody here probably knows which camp.

And when he'd given the girl over, he went back down to the edge of the water, between the figs, and stood there, and the story says he said it. Out loud. To the water.

"Settled."

That's where I stop.

Now, before you start on me, hear this. That last part isn't in the record. Go and ask the griot. He'll tell you what's entered: the taking, the settling, the father speaking for the house. He won't tell you about the flood. And it isn't because he doesn't know the story. Every griot knows the story. It's because nobody stood on that bank to see it. Timbelo was the only one there, and a man isn't a witness in his own matter, and the girl was too sick to know her own name, and the crocodile doesn't count. So that part was never said before a griot, and that part is only the fire's.

Which means you can tell it differently, and people do. Some of the Ruwozi tell it that he speared her that morning in the middle of the water and that's why there's a different one at the left fig now. They don't know rivers. The one at the left fig now is Nyandaya's daughter. Ask anybody who crosses there.

And some people say the word he said on the bank doesn't count, because it wasn't before a griot, and a thing you say with nobody there isn't said at all. Maybe. I'm only a woman who smokes fish. But I'll tell you what I think, and then somebody else can tell you what they think, and we'll be here till the fire's out.

I think he didn't say it to the griot. I think he said it to her.

And that's the Two Figs, the way I tell it. Somebody tells it better. Go and find them.
