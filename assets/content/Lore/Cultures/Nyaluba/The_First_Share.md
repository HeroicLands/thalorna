The [[lore-nyalubaclt|Nyáluba]] make no offering to [[lore-shorinyadty|Shorinya Ndafo]], the Great Brightness, and sing it no song, but they tell how it came, and the [Lobwezi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} and [Ngonzi]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} tell it differently. **The First Share** is a fire story told by the [Sengala]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} traders, who sit at both clans' fires and carry both tellings. It begins with the Brightness in the two ways and refuses to choose, and its matter is what came after: the guides on their ground before the people, the first person who asked a guide for anything, the first share given back, and the first share held back. It is not the record and does not claim to be. The version here is told on the northern road, at a trader's fire, to a party with a guest from the north among them, and the teller addresses the guest as he goes.

Every clan tells its own guide into the story as the first one asked. The Sengala telling makes it a water-hole, which belongs to no clan, and says why.

## The Telling

Pass that gourd round, and give our guest the first of it, he's come further than any of us. No, northerner, don't drink yet. Watch me. See? A little on the ground, for this ground. Now drink. You'll see why before I'm done, and if you don't, you can ask me in the morning when we're walking, and I'll pretend I didn't hear.

You asked me at the market, while you were trying to buy that knife off me for half what it's worth, who our god is. And I laughed at you, and you didn't like it. I'm sorry I laughed. I'll answer you now, properly, because the road's long and the fire's good, and these others have heard it a hundred times and know it better than I do.

We haven't got one. That's the short answer. Here's the long one.

Before anything, there was the Brightness. That's the name. The Great Brightness. You'll hear the Lobwezi call it that and the Ngonzi call it that and every one of us call it that, and that's where the agreeing stops.

Nobody talks to it. You understand? Not like your gods in the north that you build houses for and feed and argue with. Nobody puts anything on the ground for the Brightness. Nobody sings to it. It doesn't want anything and it isn't owed anything and there's no pact with it, because you can't make a pact with something that doesn't need anything from you. It's just there. Or it was. Depending on whose fire you're at.

Talking about it's allowed. Talking to it is a waste of breath. So I'm talking about it.

If you're at a Lobwezi fire, under one of their big trees, this is how it goes. The Brightness was the first thing there was, and the only thing. A light with no edge to it. And it didn't make anything. Mind that. It didn't sit down and make the guides the way your northern gods make things, with their hands, or their words, or whatever they use. It gave of itself. Bits of it went down, the way a root goes down, and every bit took the shape of the ground it went into. Where the water came up out of the earth, it took the shape of the baobab. Where the river bent, it took the shape of the crocodile. Where there was a crossing, it became the thing that keeps the crossing. And the Lobwezi say the Brightness is still there, under everything, like a root. Patient. Not going anywhere.

That's the root. Ask a Lobwezi and that's what you'll get, slowly, and twice.

If you're at an Ngonzi fire, up on the red escarpment where it's cold at night, you'll get a different one, and you'll get it in about a third of the words, because that's the Ngonzi. The Brightness wasn't a root. It was a flight. It came across the sky once, out of the east, going over, and as it went over the high country it shed sparks, the way a burning branch does if you swing it, and the sparks fell, and each spark was a guide. The eagle first. Of course the eagle first. And the Brightness didn't stop. It went on over, and it's gone. And at the end of everything, the Ngonzi say, it comes back the same way, along the road it left.

That's the flight.

Which one's right? There, you see, that's the northerner's question. He wants to know which one's right. He's going to make a temple out of whichever I say.

I don't know which one's right, friend. Neither does anybody. The Lobwezi and the Ngonzi have sat at the same Lubemo every fourth dry season since our grandfathers' grandfathers' grandfathers, and never once has either of them stood up and said: let us settle the matter of the Brightness. Why would they? It doesn't want anything. A question about something that doesn't want anything isn't a question the Pact needs answered. It's a question for a fire. So I tell it the root way at a Lobwezi fire, and the flight way at an Ngonzi fire, and at a leopard's fire, with nobody but leopard people and a northerner, I tell you both, and if you choose one I'll think a bit less of you.

Either way, root or flight, what you've got is this: the whole country full of guides. Every water-hole. Every crossing. Every hill and every kind of game and every tree that's big enough to be somebody. All of them bits of the Brightness, and all of them shaped like where they were. And no people. Not yet.

Then the people came.

Where from? Every clan tells you from its own country. The Ruwozi say they were always on the grass. The Kolembozi say they came up out of the river. The Ngonzi say nothing, which means the high stone. The Lobwezi say from under the first tree. We Sengala say we came from the north, through the trees, which is the one direction nobody else would claim, so you can believe it or not. What it means, all of it, is that nobody knows. The people came walking, and the ground was already full.

And here's the thing the old ones say first, before anything else. The people didn't know. They walked about all over the guides' ground, drinking out of the guides' water and eating the guides' game and sleeping under the guides' trees, and they never asked anybody for anything, because they didn't know there was anybody to ask.

And nothing happened.

That's the bit that always surprises children. Nothing happened. The guides didn't kill them. The guides didn't know what they were. A guide is a bit of the Brightness that's taken the shape of a place. It knows the place. It doesn't know a man walking through it, any more than you know an ant walking across your foot. The people were too new to notice. So they lived, and died when they got old, and had children, and walked about, and didn't ask.

Now, this is the bit where it matters whose fire you're at, so I'll tell you which way I tell it, and why.

Every clan tells you the first time anybody asked a guide for anything, and every clan tells you it was their guide. The Ruwozi say it was the lion, that a man stood up in front of a lion in the open and said: I want to cross your grass. The Kolembozi say it was a crocodile at a crossing. The Lobwezi say it was a tree. The Ngonzi say it was an eagle, I think, but they won't tell you, so who knows.

At a leopard fire we tell it as a water-hole. Not a leopard. A water-hole. Out in the middle of the grass, a little one, the kind that's there in the wet and gone by the middle of the dry. And we tell it that way because a water-hole is nobody's. It's not the lion's or the crocodile's or the leopard's. And we Sengala are traders, and a trader likes a story where nobody's in front at the start. That's the honest reason, northerner. You may as well know it.

So. A water-hole. And a woman.

She came to it in the dry season, from wherever her people were camped, with a gourd to fill, the way women have been coming to water since there was water. And the hole was low, the way they get. She knelt down and filled the gourd. And she stood up to go.

And then she knelt down again, and poured a little of it back.

Why? The story doesn't know. Some say she spilled it and pretended she meant to. Some say she saw how low the hole was and felt sorry for it, the way you feel sorry for a cow that's thin. Some say she just had too much to carry. My grandmother, who was the best teller I ever heard, said she did it because she'd taken some, and it seemed to her that if you take some you should give some back, and that was all, and that's what people were like before anybody taught them to be cleverer.

However it was, she poured a little back. Not much. A palmful.

And the water-hole noticed.

Think of it. All that time, people walking about, drinking, eating, sleeping, not one of them ever giving back so much as a drop. And then one does. And the guide of that water-hole, who'd never known a person was a person, suddenly knew: there is someone here who knows that I am here.

And the guide spoke to her.

How? I don't know how. In the voice they use. The spirit-speakers know it and I don't. But it spoke, and the story always gives you the words, because the words are the whole thing, and every fire has them nearly the same.

The guide said: "What do you want?"

And the woman, who'd only come for water, thought about it. And she said: "Water tomorrow, too."

And the guide said: "Then bring back a share."

That's it. That's the first pact. "What do you want?" "Water tomorrow, too." "Then bring back a share." Every pact any clan has ever made with any guide is that one, with the names changed.

Now you see why I poured a bit on the ground before you drank.

She went back to her people, and she told them, and they didn't believe her, because why would they? A woman comes back from the water and says the water talked. But she went back the next day, and poured back her share, and the next, and the hole didn't dry. All that dry season it didn't dry, when every other hole for a day's walk was cracked mud. And by the end of the dry season her people were drinking from it, and every one of them was pouring back their share, because they'd seen. That's how it starts with people. Not with a story. With a water-hole that doesn't dry.

And then they started to think. If the water-hole has someone in it, what about the river? What about the game? What about the big tree we sleep under, and the hill where the lightning hit? And they started asking. Everywhere. Carefully, at first. "What do you want?" And every guide wanted something different, because every guide was shaped like a different place. The river wanted fish put back. The game wanted its share left on the ground after the kill. The tree wanted something at its root. And the crossing wanted, well, the Kolembozi will tell you what the crossing wanted, at length.

And it went further than that, once they looked. The honey tree wanted one comb left in it, the best one, not the worst; a person who left the worst comb found out about it from the bees. The pool where the hippo lay wanted nobody to wash in it at noon. The hill where the lightning hit didn't want anything put on it at all; it wanted nobody to sleep on it, ever, and the people who tried didn't wake well. And some places didn't want a share. They wanted you gone. There was a hollow by a dead tree where something had been done, nobody remembered what, and when they asked it "What do you want?" the answer was a cold that went into the bones. So they learned that one too. You don't bargain with that kind. You keep it outside the fire. That's where the three ways come from, northerner, that you've heard us talk about: the ones you honor, the ones you pay exactly, and the ones you keep away from. It all started with asking, and finding out who answered.

And then there was a problem. Because there were so many of them. Every place had its share, and every share was different, and a person could get one wrong. Put the river's share at the tree's root. Leave the tree's share for the river. And the guides didn't like that. A guide that's been given the wrong share hasn't been given anything.

So someone had to remember. All of them. Which place, which share, which day. And that was the first griot.

She wasn't a storyteller. Hear me. That's the thing about the first griot, every fire says it. She didn't tell stories. Stories you can tell well or badly. She remembered which water-hole wanted what, and she was right or she was wrong, and if she was wrong somebody went thirsty. And everybody knew it, so everybody watched her, and corrected her, and she never minded, because that's how you know you've got it right: somebody who already knows the answer is listening.

The Lobwezi say the first griot was Lobwezi. Of course they do. I'm not arguing. I'd like to keep sitting under their trees.

So that's how it was. The people asking, the guides answering, the shares going back, the griot remembering. And everyone did well. The water-holes stayed wet. The game came back. Nobody went hungry who didn't have to.

And people still died. Old people. Babies in a bad year. A man gored by a buffalo. People had always died, ever since they came walking. But they died, my grandmother said, the way a fruit falls. It gets old, and it lets go. That's all. Nobody owed anybody anything for it.

Then there was the closed hand.

I tell this part shorter, because it's not a nice part, and because the one it's about doesn't get a long telling. He doesn't get his name, either. You'll see why.

He was a hunter. A good one. The best in his band. And one day he went out on the grass and he killed, and it was a big kill, a fat one, the best he'd ever had. And he cut the share for the guide of that game, the way everybody did by then, the way the griot said, and he laid it on the ground.

And then he looked at it.

And he thought: that's the best piece. That's the fattest piece of the whole beast. And who's going to know? The guide? The guide's had a share off every kill since the woman at the water-hole. One share it won't miss. And the griot? The griot wasn't there. Nobody was there. Just him and the grass and the dead beast.

So he picked the share up off the ground, and he put it in his bag with the rest, and he went home full-handed. And that night his band ate the guide's share and didn't know it, and he sat by the fire with grease on his chin and said nothing.

And nothing happened.

You see? Same as before. Nothing happened. The next day nothing. The next day nothing. And the hunter laughed, the story says. Not out loud. Inside. He thought he'd found something the rest of them were too stupid to see. He thought: the shares are a story. The woman at the water-hole had a story, and we've all been feeding the grass for it.

And then the game went.

Not all at once. A guide doesn't strike. That's not what they do. It withdraws. That's all. It goes away from you, the way a person goes away from you when you've cheated him and he's found out. The herds stopped coming to that grass. The hunters went out and came back with nothing. Then they went further, and came back with nothing. Then the band moved, and the grass they moved to had a guide too, and that guide had heard.

They had a bad season. Then a bad year. And people started dying.

And this is the part, northerner. This is the part you asked me about at the market, without knowing you'd asked it. Because they didn't die the way they used to. Not the way a fruit falls. The old ones went first, because the old ones always give their food to the children, and then the children went, because there wasn't enough to give. And the people looked at it, and they knew it was different. It wasn't letting go. It was being called. It was a debt.

That's what the old ones say death is, when it's the bad kind. When it's the drowning at a crossing, or the child born still, or the fire that takes the compound. Not a punishment. Nobody's angry. It's the account, running the other way. Somebody, somewhere, held back a share, and it's being called in. The first one that was ever called in was the closed hand's.

They found out, of course. People always find out. His wife, probably. Some fires say his wife, and some say a child who saw him put it in the bag, and the Lobwezi say the griot worked it out by counting, which is the most Lobwezi way to find anything out. And when they found out, they took the share back to the grass. Not one share. Every share he'd held, from every kill, all of them, and they cut more from their own thin food and put it with it, because by then they knew what they owed. They laid it all down on the grass and the spirit-speakers asked, and asked, and asked again, and in the end the game came back.

It came back. That's important. The account can be put right.

But the ones who'd died in the bad year didn't come back. You can repay a debt. You can't repay it backwards.

And the hunter? They didn't kill him. That's not what we do. The griot did something worse. She took his name out of her mouth. She didn't say it again, not when she said his band's line, not when she said his father's, not ever. And so his children's children didn't have him. He wasn't anybody's ancestor. He wasn't a guide to anyone. He was just a gap, a man with a closed hand, and that's all the story calls him, and that's why. He was the first name ever left out.

And here's the end, because there's always an end, and you'll want it.

What happens at the end of everything? Well, that depends on the fire again. The Ngonzi say the Brightness comes back over, out of the east, along the road it left, and every guide goes up into it like sparks going up, and then there's nothing to share and nobody to share it with, and that's the end. The Lobwezi say the end is every guide going back down into the root, the way water goes back into the ground, and the root stays where it is, patient. And the Ruwozi say the end is somebody else's business and there's cattle to see to.

And us? We're traders. We say: whichever it is, we'll find out when it comes, and until it comes there's a share owed on everything, and we'd better pay it.

There. That's our god, friend. We haven't got one. We've got the share.

No, don't look at me like that. Pass the gourd back round, and this time you pour, and you don't need me to show you how.

Good. Not too much. A palmful.
