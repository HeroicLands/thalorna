The Dunhari are a confederation of tribal peoples organized around lineage, household and the seasonal migration circuit, and everything that matters about them traces to one fact: in a desert, a person's word and a person's welcome are the only securities there are.

A tribe is led by a sheikh whose authority rests on hereditary legitimacy, personal charisma and the consent of the elders together—remove any of the three and he is a man with a title. Beneath him stand a warrior class whose standing rests on raid and caravan-escort, a class of poet-scholars who keep law and genealogy, and the broad community of herders, artisans, traders and camp-followers.

## Salt

Hospitality is sacred, and it is a law before it is a kindness. A Dunhari tribe is obliged to protect a stranger who has eaten its salt—against its own kin, against a rival tribe, at the cost of open war if it comes to that, and without regard to who the stranger turns out to be.

The rule is absolute because a qualified rule would be worthless. A desert in which shelter depended on the traveler's identity would be a desert nobody could cross. A tribe that gave up a guest would not recover in a generation.

The obligation has limits in time and not in kind: it begins with the salt and runs for a stated period after the guest departs, and both parties know the count. The host breaks the bread, presses a pinch of salt into the torn edge and holds it out until the guest has eaten, and only then asks a name. Salt binds the guest as well. A guest who brings a feud into the host's tent has shamed the host.

## The Word and the Welcome

The Dunhari keep the [[affiliation-ashanpnthn|Ashaian Pantheon]]’s reading of the world, in which every act is a choice between [Asha]{.name data-type="lore/cosmology"}, truth and order, and [Druj]{.name data-type="lore/cosmology"}, falsehood. They take the threefold path of good thoughts, good words and good deeds for a rule of the camp. **Good is the word kept and the guest kept; evil is the false word and the guest given up.** Breaking an oath is the plain case, because an oath sworn at a fire is a legal instrument and a religious act at once, and the oath-breaker has lied to the law and to the god in a single breath.

Success is a line the poets will carry. A man has succeeded when his word is taken without witnesses, his tent is a place caravans plan their route around, his feuds have known exits and his name is recited at the gatherings with the right verses. A warrior is acclaimed into [[affiliation-wrrscrcldnhrtrbs|the Circle]], a sheikh holds his tribe by descent and consent together, a woman keeps a household whose tent never turns anyone from its fire. Failure is shame and, past shame, to be Kinless: a man cast out by his own kin and claimed by none.

The Dunhari value most the word, the welcome, the keeping of water in common and the memory that outlasts a life. They value least the soft life of walled cities, which they admire for its wealth and despise for what it costs a man's edge, and the cheap word: a promise nobody witnessed, a record nobody carries in memory, a claim made without the verse behind it.

## The Man Admired

[[being-trvshshrdr|Torvash Shiradar]], the chieftain of the [Shiradar]{.name data-type="affiliation/lineage"} at the spring that never fails, is the Dunhari warrior who ended a war by refusing to win it. He was the third son of Chieftain [Rezhad]{.name data-type="being/npc"} and proved himself by mastery rather than maneuver. He took his first wounds at sixteen and had made his name as a warrior by his early twenties. When his father died, his two elder brothers began a civil war over the place, and Torvash arranged a settlement that raised neither of them: the tribe would govern itself through a council. One brother died in a later border fight and the other took a place of honor without authority. Torvash became chieftain on the consent of the elders and not by right of succession, and he has held the place for decades since, apportioning the tribe's resources, managing its dealings with the settled kingdoms and defending its ground. His camp at [[place-oasistvrzvd|Tevrazavad]] is now where other sheikhs send quarrels to be heard.

The Dunhari admire in him the art the whole law is for: he found the exit that cost his tribe least standing. His cost is vigilance. A poison from a rival left him scarred across the eye and a good deal more watchful, and he carries a grudge with the patience of a man who measures time in decades. The man who ended his brothers' war holds his own enemies to the same law, and keeps each feud until its exit is found.

## The Woman Admired

[Mehrava]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, the senior elder (_ruzhogar_) of [The Lightning-Marked]{.name data-type="affiliation"}, is the woman whose word [[affiliation-strmcltsbhrms|the storm cults]] cannot set aside. The Lightning-Marked are the smallest and most prestigious lineage of the cults, regarded by them as touched by the god in vision or by lightning, and she leads them with an authority that is personal and not inherited. The accuracy of what she reads is the source of it, and it is also the source of friction: when her reading of the sky stands against the readings of the larger lineages, the cults have two readings and one season.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of the chieftain's. Torvash keeps a tribe whole, and Mehrava keeps the word of the storm honest. Misreading is the most serious failure a storm-cult practitioner can commit, and falsifying a reading is among the gravest offenses the cults recognize, so a practitioner whose readings prove true carries what the camp has no other way to buy. Her cost is the quarrel. A woman who will not soften a reading to keep the peace among the lineages is a woman the lineages cannot ignore, and the Dunhari prefer the argument to the comfort.

## What the Desert Keeps

The Dunhari can point to a handful of things that hold their law in place. The [Laws of the Well]{.name data-type="lore/law"} are the oldest: the duties of hospitality at oases and springs, kept even by blood enemies, so that two tribes at feud water their herds in turn and draw no blade within sight of the water. **The code of salt** is the second, a guarantee a stranger carries on his own tongue. **The recitation** is the third, the poets' standing archive of genealogy, precedent and treaty, which a tribe produces whenever a claim is made.

Two standards belong to the whole confederation. The **vast-bahar**, the strip of indigo leather an acclaimed warrior wears on the upper left arm, is renewed only in the vat at the [[place-stoneofranaz|Stone of Ranaz]] and fades if the wearer stays away for three meetings, so the mark has to be earned again by showing up. The **marked stones** that choose the Voice of the Quarter at each gathering are kept between meetings by the storm cults, and a Circle meeting cannot lawfully open without them. The [Old Promise]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} and the [Quiet After]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the Circle's two principles, are the nearest thing the Dunhari have to a charter: no Dunhari blade is raised against another while any outside enemy walks Dunhari land, and when the threat ends, the Circle's war ends with it. A recognized vessel of the storm god wears an iron torc at the throat, given by the assembled elders.

## The Feud and the Ending of It

Blood-feud can span generations, and foreigners who hear that much conclude that the Dunhari are lawless. They have it backwards. Every way of ending a feud is set down precisely—restitution in stock, intermarriage, a shared pilgrimage, the formal apology of a sheikh to a sheikh—and that precision is what the law is for.

A feud is a state of affairs with known exits, and the whole art of a sheikh's life is finding the exit that costs his tribe least standing. A Keeper of the Feud records the blood owed and the blood paid, and a settlement cannot be reckoned without his tally. Poets are consulted on precedent, elders argue the rate, and the settlement once made is recited publicly and thereafter binding.

## Fire and the Storm

The Dunhari keep the Ashaian pantheon, and the faith of fire and light suits a people who live by what can be seen at a distance. Observance is portable by necessity—a hearth, a rite, a season—and no temple priesthood sits anywhere in the open desert. The foundation of an ordinary Dunhari's practice is the household fire-ritual at sunrise and at sunset, which needs no priest. A traveling fire-priest carries the flame from camp to camp and ministers to those beyond any temple's reach, and a storm-cult practitioner sits in every camp of consequence.

The storm is the god most often addressed. The practitioners of the Storm Cults of Bahramish read the weather before a chieftain moves his herds, conduct the rain rites at the season-turns and in drought, and take part in births, deaths, marriages and war-rites. Beside them go the desert mystics, who fast alone in the open sand and are housed, followed and obeyed for life if they come back with visions that prove true. Religious and tribal obligation run together, and the Dunhari want it so: an oath sworn at a fire is a crime and a sacrilege at once, which is exactly the redundancy a people with no police force requires.

The holy places are features of the land, and pilgrimage to them is central to the faith. A Dunhari who has completed the whole round carries a prestige no other deed can earn.

## The Year at the Water

The festivals that matter are those that carry the camp through water, storm and the dead. The [Calling of the Rains]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} falls in late spring when the dry season threatens to overrun its time, and the priests of the [[affiliation-bahramis|Thunder Rider]] ride and call the monsoon winds in. The [Festival of the Thunder Rider]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} falls at the height of the storm season, with contests of spear-throwing, horsemanship and wrestling, and it is where young warriors make a name. The [Summer Vigil]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} is an all-night gathering at the largest oases, kept to pray for the water to hold through the driest months, and the sick and the elderly are brought to the springs and bathed while mothers sit in places of honor. The [Feast of Flames]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} at the summer solstice sets bonfires in the open desert. The [Festival of the Departed]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} has every fire-temple kindle the [Death-Flame]{.name data-type="lore"}, and the camps speak their dead aloud around their own fires. At each season-turn the Circle and the cults' elders gather at the Stone of Ranaz.

Each is for something concrete. The Calling is for the grass, the Thunder Rider for the young men's courage, the Vigil for the well, the Feast for the fire and the Departed for the account of the dead. The gatherings at the Stone are for the law, since that is where a wrong too large for a chieftain is carried.

## Reputation, and How It Is Lost

Reputation is everything, and the Dunhari mean that more literally than most peoples who say it. A shamed Dunhari warrior is dead in every way that matters: he keeps his life and loses access to marriage, to the raid, to the council, and to the ordinary courtesies of camp life.

A tribe can be shamed as a tribe, and restoring the standing of one may take decades before its sheikh can meet another sheikh's eye. The penalties climb from restitution in stock and a sheikh's apology, to exclusion from the gatherings for the man who lets his obligations in the kin-credit lapse (the credit that carries nearly every bargain in the desert), to a place among the Kinless. The rewards climb the other way: a verse that is recited, a mark at the Stone, the pilgrim's prestige, a recognized vessel's torc, and the hospitality gifts a powerful sheikh collects from the caravans that cross his ground.

## Sheikh, Circle, Cult and Council

Authority in the open desert is layered and nobody sits at the top. A sheikh holds his tribe by descent, personal standing and the consent of his elders, and an elder settles by memory what the young dispute. The tribes meet in a common council at [[place-ghavulavad|Ghavulavad]], whose delegates argue and do not bind, and whose offices serve the tribes in common: the Lawkeeper arbitrates where two tribes' customs differ, the Truce-Warden holds the peace of the meeting and the markets, and the Water-Warden fixes the order in which the herds come to the shared wells.

Two bodies stand beside the council and answer to no sheikh. The Circle gathers the acclaimed warriors of every tribe and judges no quarrel between tribes; its authority is moral and its word carries in every camp. The storm cults recognize the vessels in whom the god is held to speak, and a vessel's word overrides a chieftain's ruling on a religious matter. A sheikh who defies either can do it once. Where the authorities conflict, the disputing tribes bring their recitations before poets of a third tribe, who judge which is sound. The Keeper of the Feud holds the tally of blood, the sheikh holds the hospitality gifts, and the oath sworn at a fire holds the rest.

## The Poets

Dunhari oral tradition is one of the wonders of [[place-worldthlrn|Thalorna]], and the poets who carry it are the keepers of law and identity, holding genealogies, histories, legal precedent, and the terms of treaties with other tribes and with distant empires, all preserved in verse and transmitted across generations.

A poet recites for days without repetition. The ability to compose and recite is a mark of nobility, and a sheikh who cannot do it adequately is at a permanent disadvantage against one who can. Written records exist here and there and are afterthoughts—a tribe's real archive walks, breathes, and can be asked questions.

This makes memory a contested field. Two tribes in dispute produce two recitations, and the argument is conducted in front of poets from a third tribe who are qualified to judge which version is sound.

## The Bridge

The Dunhari believe what the Ashaian faith teaches: after death the soul crosses the [Chinvat Bridge]{.name data-type="place/structure"}, wide and steady for the righteous and a razor's edge for the wicked. The crossing is no trial imposed from outside. It is the natural consequence of how one has lived, and the bridge is as wide as the good thoughts, words and deeds that built it.

On an ordinary day that belief makes the Dunhari watch their mouths. The false word is the Druj the bridge narrows against, so a promise made at a well is a promise made on the bridge, and a man who must settle a feud settles it in the open and in verse. He keeps the household fire at sunrise and sunset and gives the guest the salt first. He shapes a decade of conduct around a line he does not want recited after his death. The dead are carried deep into the desert on the fastest camels and left in a hidden place, with a fire kindled where the body is set down, so no tomb stands to be kept and no grave to be robbed; what a person leaves is herds, rights to a well and a name. A dying elder is surrounded by the camp, and each person speaks one deed or kindness of the dying, for the god records them before the crossing begins.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead through the fire and the verse. A fire-keeper kindles a death-fire whenever a soul departs, and the storm cults conduct the funerals and the camp's rites. On the night of the Festival of the Departed the families speak their dead aloud around the fire, recounting deeds of virtue and courage and asking the god to guide each soul across. The poets carry the lines of the dead in the recitation, which is why the Dunhari spend a lifetime shaping the line.

The dead do not reach the living. They reach the account: a debt unpaid, a feud unsettled, a verse that is told rightly or wrongly. The rite costs the carrying of the body across the sand and the kindling of the fire, and the fire-keeper who goes with it.

## What a Person Owes

A Dunhari owes in the order the desert taught: the guest first, because the guest is owed before anyone has established who he is; then the household; then the lineage; then the tribe and the sheikh who speaks for it.

Last and underneath he owes the account of himself that the poets will carry. In return the guest is owed salt, protection and the count of days that follows it. The household is owed his share of the work and its feud. The lineage owes him protection and a place in its feud, and he owes it his seat among the elders when his years have earned one. The sheikh is owed the tribe's consent and owes it the wells, the circuit, the settlement of quarrels and the hospitality of his tent. The poet is owed the tribe's hearing and owes it a true recitation. The storm cult is owed a place at the fire and owes the camp a faithful reading of the weather.

## The Weak

Care is the household's first and the lineage's second. A Bondservant is held in service or debt to a household and answers through its head. A Dependent is sheltered without being of its blood: a client, a widow's family, a taken-in stranger. A widow's family stands in the household that shelters it, and an orphan is taken in as a dependent of a kinsman. The Kinless, cast out by their own kin and claimed by none, are the one rank owed no hospitality, water or vengeance; a Kinless man who eats a host's salt is nonetheless a guest for the stated period, since the salt is the host's and not the guest's.

In a bad year the first resort is the Water-Warden's order, which keeps a well from emptying into the strongest herd, and the second is the rain-callers' ritual, which asks the sky. When the drought runs on, the slow movement of a tribe follows. The kin-credit that carries nearly every bargain without coin or written instrument carries the weak as well: a debt owed by a kinsman in a hard year is carried to the next gathering and the next marriage, and exclusion from the gatherings is the penalty for letting it lapse without cause.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A man who cannot ride a raid keeps his place in the tent, his scars and his share. The scar itself is honored, and the vast-bahar is called the scar of the mighty. The injured and the old are bathed at the springs at the Summer Vigil, and the household feeds them from the herd.

The old are not set aside. An elder settles by memory what the young dispute, and an aged shaman who spends his evenings alone at the sacred stones is still the chieftain's first adviser on disputes that resist ordinary judgment. A person who cannot travel with the camp is left with a kinsman at an oasis on the circuit and visited when the camp returns.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the tent and the salt. A household with no room gives the guest a place at the fire and the count of days, and a camp with no household for a widow gives her the nearest kinsman's. Teaching is the poet's and the cult's. A child learns the genealogy and the verses at the fire, an apprentice (_hamelogar_) to a storm-cult lineage learns to read the sky, and a young warrior learns the raid and the escort from the warriors of the tent. Defense is the warriors of the tribe, the escort passed from tribe to tribe along the caravan road, and, when an outside enemy walks Dunhari land, the Circle.

## The Tent at Every Age

The unit is the household, the tent and its herd under a Householder who speaks for them at the moot, and past it the lineage, the clan and the tribe. Membership is blood and the salt of belonging. A Kinsman is of the household's blood and entitled to its protection, its feud and its share. A Dependent is sheltered without belonging, and the Kinless belong to no one.

A child belongs to the tent and learns by the fire. A young person moves to the apprenticeship of the cult or the poets, to the raid and the escort, or to the household of a marriage that ends a feud or links two tribes. An adult takes a tent and a herd and a seat at the moot, and the old become elders. A dying person is surrounded by the camp and the camp speaks the deeds. Marriage is a contract between tribes first, and the Dunhari marry into the houses of [[affiliation-kngdmmrdd|Amradad]] as readily as into each other's.

## What the Old Promise Costs

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Dunhari's is the promise that holds their union together. The Old Promise says no Dunhari blade is raised against another while an outside enemy walks Dunhari land. It binds every tribe in the desert, and it has been tested twice.

The sin is what the Circle did with it. Twice the Circle was petitioned to act against a Dunhari tribe whose leaders had done what the petitioners found intolerable, and twice it refused. One of those tribes became a vassal of the [[affiliation-khzrncnfdrtn|Tellumi Confederation]]. The other was swallowed by its neighbors within a generation. The Circle counted both as the price of keeping the Old Promise whole. A people who will not give up a guest gave up two of their own, and the poets keep the account. Thoughtful Dunhari dispute whether a law that never lifts a blade against a Dunhari can hold a Dunhari tribe to account, and whether the confederation was kept whole or only kept.
