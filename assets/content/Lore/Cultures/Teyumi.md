The Turquoise People live where the desert of [[place-chakultzklrgn|Ch'akul Tsa'kul]] climbs onto mesas and canyon country, and everything they are follows from the rain that falls there too seldom. They call themselves the [[affiliation-teyumi|Teyumi]], the people of the turquoise, after the stone they hold to be rain made solid. About 150,000 of them live in towns of stone great houses, each house a terraced block of hundreds of rooms that stores its town's corn and hosts its rites. Straight roads run between the houses across the tableland, and signal fires on the mesa tops carry word from one canyon to the next faster than a runner. The south calls them _Ishtem Tsul_, the Bear People, after the bears painted on the walls of their chambers.

## Where They Came From

The Turquoise People say that their grandmothers' grandmothers lived farther south, in country that is desert now, and built the first great houses there. The ruins that travelers find in Ch'akul Tsa'kul, stone walls standing in sand with no water for days in any direction, are the [Old Houses]{.name data-type="place/site"} in their telling, and no Teyumi crosses the desert without leaving cornmeal at one. When the rain left that country, the people walked north carrying their seed corn and their turquoise. [Teyuwesa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, whose name means Turquoise Walking, led them, and the songs say he stopped at every spring and asked whether the rain lived there.

He stopped at last in [Hosa Oyala]{.name data-type="place/region"}, the Canyon of Houses, where the cliffs let a watcher read the sunrise against a single notch in the rim and where a summer storm still filled the washes. The first great houses of the north rose there, and the straight roads still leave it in every direction. Every town of the Turquoise People counts itself a daughter of that canyon, and its feast each autumn is the one gathering every town attends.

The rest of their history is the history of the rain. Wet generations filled the storerooms, and the towns added stories to their great houses and dug canals into the lowlands toward the desert. Dry generations emptied them, and the worst of the dry runs is the one the Turquoise People still argue about, the [Year of Shut Doors]{.name data-type="lore/history"}, told below.

## The Rain Is Everything

**Good is the calm heart, the full storeroom and the rain that answers both. Evil is the dry heart: bitterness, quarreling, greed, and anything else that keeps the rain away.** The Turquoise People hold that rain is drawn by the hearts of the people under it. A town at peace with itself, whose rites are kept and whose quarrels are settled before planting, is a town the clouds come to. A town full of grudges is a town the clouds pass over.

This belief governs manners down to the smallest. A Teyumi does not raise his voice, does not boast, and does not refuse a neighbor who asks for water, because each of these dries the heart. A quarrel is not won; it is settled, usually by the elder of a chamber, and both sides are expected to stop speaking of it once it is. A person who cannot let a grievance go is not called wicked at first. He is called dry, and the word is a warning.

Wrongs against property and person are judged by the same measure. A theft is bad because the thief took, and worse because the man robbed now carries a grudge. A killing is the worst of all, because it fills a whole clan with grief and anger at once, and the Turquoise People can name the dry summers that followed killings in living memory.

## Standing in a Town

Success is a full storeroom and a place in the rites. A household that brings a good harvest to its great house, whose men dance in turn and whose women's corn is ground fine, is a household the town relies on. Standing rises through the chambers: a child is a member of a household, a _walayo_; an initiate of a chamber is a _peluyo_; the lineage that keeps a great house and holds its rites is _hosayo_, great-house kin. The great-house kin give each town its rain chief and its house head, and the other lineages accept this because the great house feeds them in a bad year.

Failure is a dry heart and an empty field. A man who will not take his turn on the canals, a woman whose household brings nothing to the storeroom, and anyone who cannot keep a quarrel quiet lose standing slowly and in public. At the bottom are the _pesayo_, the Dry, put out of their town and refused water at every town on the roads. No household takes in one of the Dry, and no great house sends one corn.

The Turquoise People value most the rain, the calm heart, the storeroom, the turquoise and the order of the year. They value least haste, display and anything done alone. A trader from the south who praises a man to his face is answered with silence, because praise is a kind of boast.

## The Man Admired

[Lumawesa]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Cloud clan is a dry farmer of a canyon town north of [[place-kwatuhosa|Kwatuhosa]], and the canyon towns name him when they say what a man should be. He plants his corn deep on the mesa top, a forearm down where the snowmelt has soaked, and waits for it with a patience his neighbors call stubborn until the corn comes up. He is a runner as well. In a dry spring three years ago he carried word of a southern war party, sighted at the desert's edge, from Kwatuhosa to the Canyon of Houses in two days and a night, gave his message word for word to the rain chief, and walked home to finish planting.

The canyon towns admire three things in him, and all three are the calm heart in practice. He runs straight, as the roads are built straight, and carries a message without adding to it. He plants deep and does not complain of the wait. He has never been heard to raise his voice. His cost is plain to everyone. A runner's body wears out young, and he will never be a chamber elder, because the elders of his town come from its great-house kin and he does not.

## The Woman Admired

[Tseyahesi]{.name data-type="being/npc"} of the Corn clan is the seed keeper of her clan in the same canyon town, and the women of the canyon towns name her when they say what a woman should be. She keeps the clan's seed corn in sealed jars in the back rooms of her house, enough for five plantings, and she is the only person who may open them. In the last dry run her own grandchildren went hungry while the jars stood full, and she did not open them. When the first wet spring came, the Corn clan planted its fields from her jars while its neighbors begged seed at the great house.

Her admiration is not a lesser form of Lumawesa's. He keeps the calm heart in the present, and she keeps the year that has not come yet. The Turquoise People say that a household eats from its storeroom and lives from its seed, and that a woman who opens the seed jars to quiet a crying child has killed the next year to save the next day. Her cost is the crying. Tseyahesi's grandchildren remember the dry winter, and so does she.

## What the Houses Keep

The Turquoise People write nothing down, and what holds them together is built, worn or remembered.

- **The great house** (_hosa_) is the first of their artifacts and the largest thing in the north made by hands. Its walls carry the year: a sun watcher stands at a fixed place each morning and marks where the sunrise falls along the rim, and on the mornings that matter a shaft of light strikes a spiral pecked into a wall. Its oldest rooms hold its dead.
- **The storeroom** (_mehu_) holds the town's corn. Since the Year of Shut Doors every head of a great house swears, on taking the office, that her storerooms open before her chambers close.
- **The navel** is the small hole in the floor of every chamber, through which the people came up into this world in the oldest songs and through which the dead go down.
- **Turquoise** (_teyu_) is worn by everyone who can get it, as beads, pendants and inlay, and is given to every dead person who can be sent with it.
- **The straight roads** (_ponu_) run between the great houses and up the cliffs by cut stairways. They are not needed for walking, since a footpath would serve, and the Turquoise People do not say what else they are for.
- **The songs of the chambers** are the people's law and history, taught by the elders to the initiates and repeated without change.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is the corn and the water. A woman rises before dawn and grinds corn at her stone (_seluki_) beside her sisters, singing, and the first pinch of meal goes outside the door toward the east for the sun. A man walks to his field and leaves cornmeal at its edge for the clouds, who are his grandparents. Nobody wastes water. A cup that is not finished is poured on a plant, never on bare ground.

The rites belong to the chambers (_pelu_), the sunken round rooms in each great house's plaza. Every adult is initiated into at least one, and each chamber keeps its own ceremonies through the year. A man spends his winter evenings in his chamber, weaving cotton on the loom that stands there and learning songs, and comes up the ladder for supper at his wife's house. A woman's societies keep their own chambers and their own rites, for corn, for childbirth and for the grinding, and a man does not go down their ladders.

## The Round of the Year

The sun watcher (_waimayo_) names every day of the year that matters, and the town does nothing important on a day he has not named.

- **The sun-turning** falls at midwinter, when the sunrise stops moving south along the rim and starts back. The chambers open the ritual year, and each household brings a prayer-feather to the great house. It is for turning the sun back toward summer.
- **The planting** is named in spring, on a sunrise the sun watcher reads off the wall. The women grind seed meal, the men plant, and the chamber elders sing over the first field. It is for beginning the year's work together.
- **The rain dances** fill the high summer. The cloud dancers (_omaloyo_) come up out of the chambers in masks and macaw feathers, with copper bells at their knees, and dance in the plaza as the dead come back as rain. The clowns (_pukiya_) dance among them and mock, by name, whoever has misbehaved since spring. It is for calling the rain, and for shaming the town into a calm heart before it comes.
- **The canyon feast** follows the harvest. Pilgrims from every town walk the straight roads to the Canyon of Houses, carrying corn, pots and turquoise. The great houses there feed them, the elders drink cacao from tall painted jars, and at the end of the feast the pilgrims break the pots they carried on the great mound outside the walls. It is for renewing the bond between the towns, and for settling what the towns quarrel about.
- **The [Far Rising]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}** comes about once in a generation, when the moon rises farther north along the rim than anyone under forty can remember. The towns add a story or a wing to a great house that year. It is for marking the generations.

## Praise, Shame and the Dry

Reward is a place in the rites and the town's regard. A child who learns well is initiated early. An initiate who keeps his chamber's songs is given a chamber name and, in time, a part in the dances. A household that gives generously to the storeroom is seated near the great house at the feasts. A person the town honors is given turquoise, and a dancer who is chosen for a cloud mask wears a great house's macaw feathers.

Punishment runs from mockery to the desert. The clowns' mockery in the plaza is the first and most common: a thief, a lazy husband or a boastful youth hears his fault sung in front of the whole town and is expected to laugh. A chamber elder's rebuke is the second, given in the chamber before the society. The third is exclusion from a chamber's rites for a season or for good. The fourth is the loss of water: a lowland household that steals from the canals loses its turn on them. The last is expulsion as one of the Dry. In a dry year there is a sentence beyond expulsion, and the Turquoise People do not agree that it is just; it is told below.

## One House, Two Hats

The Turquoise People have one institution where others have two. The great house is the town's government, its granary and its temple, and its lineage holds all three. The rain chief (_lumamota_), a man of the great-house kin, keeps the town's chief rain rites and orders its year; his sister or his mother is the head of the house (_hosamota_), who keeps the storerooms and decides who is fed. The chamber elders (_pelumota_) hear quarrels in their chambers and settle most of them there. A quarrel between chambers goes to the rain chief, and a quarrel between towns goes to the canyon feast.

Nobody keeps records. The sun watcher keeps the count of days on the walls, the house head keeps the count of the storerooms in her head, and the elders keep the law in the songs. When the rain chief and the house head disagree, they argue it inside their lineage, and the town is told the result. When they cannot agree, the storeroom question goes to the house head and the rite question to the rain chief. The town has learned to wait for the next rain.

## What the Dead Become

The dead go down through the navel of the chamber into the world below, and come back as clouds. A person who lived with a calm heart returns as a rain cloud over the fields of his kin. A person who died with a dry heart returns as dust on the wind, and brings drought with him. That belief settles an ordinary day more than any law does. A grudge is dangerous because it may outlive its owner, and so a dying person is urged, by every relative in the room, to forgive whoever wronged them before the end.

The dead are buried in the oldest rooms of the great house, wrapped in cotton, with a cotton cloth over the face to give them the shape of a cloud. A family spends on two things for its dead: cotton cloth and turquoise. Turquoise is rain made stone, and a dead person sent down with it carries the seed of rain into the world below. A family that cannot afford a bead borrows one from the great house and repays it in corn. Inheritance is simple, because the house and the fields belong to the women of the clan and pass to the daughters; a man's loom, his bow and his chamber songs go to his sister's son.

## Calling the Dead

The living call the dead at the summer dances, through the chambers. A cloud dancer in his mask is not pretending; for the length of the dance he is the dead come back, and children are told that the dancers are their grandparents. The chambers charge for this work. An initiation costs a household corn, cotton and, from a family that can manage it, a turquoise bead for the chamber's altar. Every household feeds the dancers on the dance days, and the masks need macaw feathers that only the great houses can get from the south.

The dead reach the living as rain, and the living answer by keeping their hearts calm and their fields planted. The dry-hearted dead reach the living as dust storms. A town hit by a dust storm in summer asks whose dead are angry, and the question is not idle.

## What a Person Owes

Ask a Teyumi what a person owes, and the answer comes in order: a calm heart first, because the rain depends on it; then the share of every harvest owed to the great house's storeroom; then the work days owed on the town's walls, roads and canals; then a turn in the dances; then cornmeal to the clouds at the field's edge. A person owes the clan the seed corn, untouched.

In return a person is owed what the great house gives. A townsperson is owed corn from the storeroom in a bad year, a turn of water from the canals, a place in a chamber and its teaching, a share in the feasts, and burial in the house with cotton and a bead. Since the Year of Shut Doors, the house head's oath makes the first of these a duty and not a favor.

## The Weak

The household is the first resort and the storeroom the second. Because the house belongs to the women, a widow keeps her home, her fields and her children, and loses only a pair of hands. A widower goes back to his mother's or his sister's house, and is fed there. An orphan belongs to its mother's clan, and the clan's women take it in without being asked. A household that cannot feed itself goes to the great house, and the house head feeds it from the storerooms. That is a duty, sworn, and the town watches it kept.

A stranger who reaches a great house is fed once, without question, and then asked what he wants. A stranger with no kin among the Turquoise People and no pipe of the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] is fed and sent on.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A person who cannot farm or carry is kept by the household, and finds a seat in the chambers. A man who cannot walk to the fields sits at the loom in his chamber and weaves the cotton that every household needs. A blind elder keeps songs, because a song is learned by ear. An old woman who cannot grind teaches her granddaughters to grind, and keeps the seed. The sun watcher's post is often given to a man whose legs have failed, because the work is to stand at one place every morning and remember.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the household and, past it, the great house. Teaching is the chambers' work: a child is taught to work by its parents, to behave by its mother's brother, and to sing, pray and keep the law by the chamber it is initiated into. Defense is the great house itself. Its outer walls have no doors at ground level, its rooms are entered by ladders through their roofs, and when the ladders are drawn up a house of five hundred rooms is a fortress. The signal fires on the mesa tops warn a canyon in an hour of what a runner would take a day to bring.

The Turquoise People fight with bows and stone-pointed arrows, and the richest warriors carry copper. They have heard what southern steel does, from the traders and from the war parties that have come out of the desert, and it is the reason they want the compact and the reason the other nations of it doubt them. They are the compact's early warning: a southern war party reaches Kwatuhosa first, and the first fire on the first mesa carries the news north.

## The House and the Clan

The household is a woman, her daughters, her daughters' husbands and all their children, living in joined rooms of one block. A man marries into his wife's house and works her fields, and his own clan stays his mother's. A child belongs to its mother's clan and is named, on the morning it is first shown to the sun, by the women of its father's. The clan is the unit that owns houses, fields, seed and certain rites, and its eldest women decide who marries in.

The age ladder is plain. A child is a _walayo_ and is carried, then sent to fetch water. At nine or ten it is initiated into a chamber, sees the cloud dancers unmasked for the first time, and learns that they are men of the town, which is the first secret it is trusted with. A young man runs the roads and digs the canals; a young woman grinds corn with her sisters and, when she chooses a husband, grinds for his mother for four days to show she can keep a house. Adults farm, weave, grind, dance and sit in the chambers. The old keep the songs, the seed and the sun. The dying are carried to the oldest rooms of the great house and told to forgive. The dead go down through the navel, and come back with the rain.

## The Shut Doors and the Dry Heart

The sin the Turquoise People committed is the Year of Shut Doors. It came at the end of a long dry run, generations ago, and lasted not one year but nine. As the storerooms emptied, the great houses of the canyon kept back what was left: first for the rites, because the rain chiefs held that the rain would not come to a town that let its dances lapse; then for the great-house kin, because the rites needed them. The households outside the walls were turned away at the ladders. Many starved, and many walked north and east and never came back.

The chamber elders looked, as their teaching told them to, for the dry hearts that were keeping the rain away. They found them among the hungry, who were angry, and among the old, who could not work. Those named were driven out as the Dry, and some were killed. [Hepuluma]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, whose name means Rain at the Door, was the head of a great house in the canyon. In the ninth year she let down the ladders of her own house and opened its storerooms to everyone below the walls. The rain came the next summer, and the Turquoise People still dispute whether the two are connected. The oath every house head swears comes from her.

The blind spot is still there, and thoughtful Teyumi argue over it. The doctrine of the dry heart holds that drought comes from bitterness, so a dry year always ends with the search for whose heart is at fault, and the search always lands on the hungry, the angry, the odd and the old. The town that puts one of them out has one mouth fewer and someone to blame. Some elders hold that this is how the rain is kept, and that the Year of Shut Doors was a failure of the great houses and not of the doctrine. Others answer that the doctrine has never once named a great-house kinsman, and ask what the rain is supposed to be answering. The last three summers have been short of rain.

A second argument runs alongside it. Turquoise flows south through Kwatuhosa, and macaw feathers, cacao and copper bells flow north. The cloud dancers cannot dance without the feathers, and some elders say the people are sending their rain away in turquoise to buy the masks they call it with. The other nations of the compact see the same trade and draw another conclusion, that the Turquoise People are closer to the south than an ally should be. Some Teyumi suspect it too.
