The Okhárins did not build their civilization out of canals, and they are quietly proud of it. Their neighbors upriver dug channels and made a world out of a flood; in western Okháris the rain does that work unasked, and in the drier east the people put their wealth into herds instead. The village tribes of the wet west farm, fish, tend orchards and work timber from fixed seats. Eastward, toward the savanna of the Bethua border, most of the people are semi-nomadic pastoralists moving cattle, sheep, goats and small sturdy horses between seasonal grazings, on cycles so old they carry the weight of religious obligation.

Between migrations a herding family works a small plot of sorghum, millet, pulses and tubers—enough to supplement the herds and never enough to replace them, which is exactly what everyone intends.

## The Three-Flame Settlement

Okháris is two societies that need each other, and the compact between them is the oldest thing in the realm. The three temple-cities of Zarhánis, Kaljékor and Vuthráka are the fixed points the nomadic cycles turn around, and they supply religious legitimacy, metal-craft and the entrepôt trade. The grazing clans supply the herds that feed the realm, the wool and leather that clothe it, and the warriors who defend it. Neither half can do without the other, and both know the terms exactly.

An Okhárin therefore carries two loyalties and does not experience them as competing: he belongs to a clan, which moves, and to a city, which does not, and which holds his festivals, his metalwork and his god. He will travel a long way to be at the right temple at the right season, and be irritated the whole way there by the city-dwellers' assumption that he is a rustic.

That irritation is the realm's standing political problem. The rural clans often feel neglected, the cities rarely notice they are doing it, and the tribal leaders spend most of their authority mediating between the two.

## The Flame in Three Aspects

The Okhárins worship Nkaru'thar as the eternal flame of creation, destruction and renewal, and they read its power through the three cities: Zarhánis is the flame of faith and wisdom, Kaljékor the flame of abundance, Vuthráka the flame of war and protection. This is not three gods, and it is the point foreigners most often get wrong. It is one fire seen from three sides, and there are three cities because a people needs all three and cannot get them from one place.

Beneath the flame stand the demi-gods—of fertility, of storms, of death—who act as intermediaries and whose shrines are scattered across the grazing country. These are what an ordinary Okhárin prays to on an ordinary day. The flame is addressed at festivals, by priests, in cities; a herdsman with a sick calf addresses someone closer to hand.

## The Pyramid and the Pilgrimage

The temple complexes are pyramidal, and the rural clans hold them sacred in a way the urban priesthoods sometimes underestimate. A clan that has never lived in a city will still reckon the year by which festival is next and arrive in force for it.

The festivals are where the realm is actually held together. Clan leaders converge, disputes are heard, marriages are arranged, herds are traded, and grievances against the cities are aired loudly and mostly absorbed. An Okhárin who missed a festival year would not be thought irreligious so much as unreachable—a man who has stepped outside the only calendar there is.

## What Is Owned and What Is Kept

Wealth is livestock, and livestock is mobile, which shapes almost everything about Okhárin manners. A rich man's wealth walks, can be counted at a glance, can be raided, and cannot be hidden. So an Okhárin is generous in public and careful in private, and a display of hospitality doubles as a display of accounts.

Land is not owned. Grazing is held by customary right—this clan takes that valley in that season—and the rights are ancient, minutely known, and the commonest cause of bloodshed in the realm. A clan that yields a grazing right once has yielded it, and everyone remembers.

Metal is the cities' monopoly and the clans' necessity, and that is the lever the cities actually hold. A herdsman can live his whole life without entering Kaljékor, and he cannot live it without Kaljékor's ironwork.

## Kin, and the Long Memory

An Okhárin names his clan before his city and his lineage before his clan, and a stranger is placed by all three inside a sentence. Marriage is arranged between lineages and understood as an alliance of grazing rights as much as of families; a match joining two clans whose herds want the same valley is worth more than a match joining two rich ones.

Feuds are long and settled in cattle. The cities dislike this and have never managed to stop it, partly because a clan accepting a city magistrate's ruling in place of cattle would be admitting that the cities govern it—which is exactly the thing the compact leaves unsaid.

## What a Person Owes

Ask an Okhárin what a man owes, and he answers in order: the herd first, because without it nobody owes anybody anything; then the lineage, then the clan, then the festival that must be attended, then the city that keeps the flame.

He does not put the king high on that list and will not pretend otherwise. What he will say is that the flame is owed a great deal, and that the king's business is keeping it lit.
