The Okharins did not build their civilization out of canals, and they are quietly proud of it. Their neighbors upriver dug channels and made a world out of a flood; in western [[place-okharisrgn|Okharis]] the rain does that work unasked, and in the drier east the people put their wealth into herds instead. The village tribes of the wet west farm, fish, tend orchards and work timber from fixed seats. Eastward, toward the savanna at the border of the [[place-bethuargn|Bethua border]], most of the people are semi-nomadic pastoralists moving cattle, sheep, goats and small sturdy horses between seasonal grazings, on cycles so old they carry the weight of religious obligation.

Between migrations a herding family works a small plot of sorghum, millet, pulses and tubers—enough to supplement the herds and never enough to replace them, which is exactly what everyone intends.

## The Three-Flame Settlement

Okharis is two societies that need each other, and the compact between them is the oldest thing in the realm. The three temple-cities of [[place-zarhanis|Zarhanis]], [[place-kaljekor|Kaljekor]] and [[place-vuthraka|Vuthraka]] are the fixed points the nomadic cycles turn around, and they supply religious legitimacy, metal-craft and the entrepôt trade. The grazing clans supply the herds that feed the realm, the wool and leather that clothe it, and the warriors who defend it. Neither half can do without the other, and both know the terms exactly.

An Okharin therefore carries two loyalties and does not experience them as competing: he belongs to a clan, which moves, and to a city, which does not, and which holds his festivals, his metalwork and his god. He will travel a long way to be at the right temple at the right season, and be irritated the whole way there by the city-dwellers' assumption that he is a rustic.

That irritation is the realm's standing political problem. The rural clans often feel neglected, the cities rarely notice they are doing it, and the tribal leaders spend most of their authority mediating between the two.

## The Flame in Three Aspects

The Okharins worship [[lore-nkaruthardty|Nkaru'thar]] as the eternal flame of creation, destruction and renewal, and they read its power through the three cities: Zarhanis is the flame of faith and wisdom, Kaljekor the flame of abundance, Vuthraka the flame of war and protection. This is not three gods, and it is the point foreigners most often get wrong. It is one fire seen from three sides, and there are three cities because a people needs all three and cannot get them from one place.

Beneath the flame stand the demi-gods—of fertility, of storms, of death—who act as intermediaries and whose shrines are scattered across the grazing country. These are what an ordinary Okharin prays to on an ordinary day. The flame is addressed at festivals, by priests, in cities; a herdsman with a sick calf addresses someone closer to hand.

The two are not the same kind of thing. The Flame is worshipped through the faith of [[affiliation-nkaruthar|Nkaru'thar]]: a religion with doctrine, temples, an ordained priesthood and a hierarchy, professed, and so in principle professable otherwise. The [[lore-zohira|zohira]] are what being Okharin consists of. Nobody converts to them or is received among them, because there is no membership to define, and an Okharin is of the spirits from birth in the way he is of his mother's kin. A villager goes to the pyramid at the turning of the year and to the cairn when the river is wrong, and the question which of the two he really believes has no answer, because it has no purchase.

## The Pyramid and the Pilgrimage

The temple complexes are pyramidal, and the rural clans hold them sacred in a way the urban priesthoods sometimes underestimate. A clan that has never lived in a city will still reckon the year by which festival is next and arrive in force for it.

The festivals are where the realm is actually held together. Clan leaders converge, disputes are heard, marriages are arranged, herds are traded, and grievances against the cities are aired loudly and mostly absorbed. An Okharin who missed a festival year would not be thought irreligious so much as unreachable—a man who has stepped outside the only calendar there is.

## What Is Owned and What Is Kept

Wealth is livestock, and livestock is mobile, which shapes almost everything about Okharin manners. A rich man's wealth walks, can be counted at a glance, can be raided, and cannot be hidden. So an Okharin is generous in public and careful in private, and a display of hospitality doubles as a display of accounts.

Grazing is not owned. It is held by customary right—this clan takes that valley in that season—and the rights are ancient, minutely known, and the commonest cause of bloodshed in the realm. A clan that yields a grazing right once has yielded it, and everyone remembers. The temples hold estates of their own, and the bondsmen who work them owe labor for the temple's granaries.

Metal is the cities' monopoly and the clans' necessity, and that is the lever the cities actually hold. A herdsman can live his whole life without entering Kaljekor, and he cannot live it without Kaljekor's ironwork.

## What Is Good, and What Succeeds

**Good is the fire kept burning in its right aspect and the spirit dealt with in its right domain: the rite performed, the offering given, the oath sworn on the Scales and kept. Evil is a fire let die, an oath sworn falsely, a spirit insulted or a thing wasted, because the Flame renews only what is given to it.** An Okharin does not weigh a deed by intent. The Flame is a cycle of creation, destruction and renewal, and the test is whether the deed feeds the cycle or breaks it.

Success is a herd that grows and a name the festivals know: the household whose offerings are exact, whose cattle are counted and not boasted of, whose daughters make alliances of grazing rights and whose sons reach the festival in force. In the cities success is a seat in a priesthood or a charter in [[affiliation-crwntrdskhrs|the Crown Trades]]. Failure is the herd lost to raiders, the valley yielded, the oath that ruins a merchant within a year and a day, and at the bottom the person put out of the Flame, whom no temple will receive.

The Okharins value most the herd, the lineage, the festival kept and hospitality given in public. They value least waste, boasting, and counting what has not yet come: a household that publicly counts its expected harvest or its expected children has insulted the zohira of fertility, and the harvest is watched by the neighbors with interest.

## The Man Admired

[[being-kirobashkm|Kiroba of the Shókuma]] is the Okharin's admired shaman. He is a member of the [[affiliation-shokumatrb|Shókuma Tribe]] of the river country near the rainforest, taught by the tribe's elder shamans, and he survived [The Spirit Hunt]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, tracking a guardian predator of the spirit world and consuming its heart in the rite that bound him to the zohira. He is an apothecary as well as a shaman, reserved and introspective, and he consults the spirits before he acts. Visions of a disturbance in the spirit world, centered in the [[place-yandazhrng|Yanda Zohiring]], have set him to the long journey toward [[lore-orclbonesspr|the Oracle of Bones]].

The Okharins admire in him the whole bargain of the tradition: a man who accepted a hunt in which candidates die, who holds the standards of his people in the highest esteem and keeps them whatever the cost to himself, and who puts his own safety second to the balance of the living and the spirits. His cost is the cost of the Hunt. The bond that makes him powerful makes him a loner, cautious of outsiders and hard for others to understand, and the visions that call him do not ask whether he wishes to go.

## The Woman Admired

[[being-ashendamgr|Ashenda of the Magara]] is the Okharin's admired guardian. She is a warrior of the most warlike of the village tribes, bearing one of the [Spirit Blades]{.name data-type="weapongear"} and trained hard from a young age to protect her people. Her tribe's blade chose her: it glowed faintly when she touched it at her initiation, and a member of the [[affiliation-magaratrb|Magara Tribe]] who is refused by the blade is not disgraced, but she is not going to carry it. She guards her people against raiders and against spirits alike, and when the spiritual imbalance reached her village she joined Kiroba, convinced that the tribes' fates were tied together.

The Okharins admire in her a different order of the same virtue. He holds the balance by seeking it; she holds the line that keeps it from being broken. She is calm, measured, pragmatic and fearless, not a speaker of speeches, and her loyalty runs from her tribe to the whole realm, which is what the Three-Flame Settlement most needs. Her cost is that the blade chooses and she does not: she carries a weapon that cuts spirits as easily as men, a bond she did not seek, and she sometimes questions the consequences of what it asks of her.

## What the Cities and the Clans Keep

An Okharin can point to the things that hold the faith in place. [The First Ember]{.name data-type="miscgear"} is the undying flame held to have been kindled at creation, kept in the deepest chamber of the Zarhanis temple and entered by the High Pyremant alone, and every temple fire in Okharis is symbolically descended from it. [The Scales of Kaljekor]{.name data-type="miscgear"} are the gilded balance on which offerings are weighed and trade oaths sworn, and a merchant who swears falsely upon them is said to be ruined within a year and a day. [The Warden's Brand]{.name data-type="miscgear"} is the iron blade at Vuthraka that marks warriors who have proved themselves in [The Trial of Flames]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}. The Spirit Blades are the weapons of the Magara in which [[lore-shengelspr|Zohu Shéngeling]] is seated. **The cairns**, stone piles with a flame-mark cut into the capstone, stand where the zohira are closest: a ford, a birthing-house, a hunting ground, a burial field.

Each person carries smaller things. **Pyrestone**, a fragment of volcanic glass, links the faithful to the Flame. **The three-wick lamp** is lit at prayer, and putting out any wick before the others is deeply inauspicious. **Ashmark**, hearth-ash and sacred oils, is set on the forehead and hands at passage, mourning and consecration. A shaman or warrior wears a spirit bone pendant from the Spirit Hunt.

## The Day Kept

On an ordinary day the religion is small. A household lights its three-wick lamp and carries its pyrestone, and its hearth, kindled from a flame carried from the nearest temple, is the household's own descent from the First Ember. A herdsman with a sick calf goes to the cairn. A woman near her time is attended by the household or a shaman, and the household's rite is not a lesser one, because [[lore-pemburspr|Pemburari Khutheling]] needs no intermediary; what a shaman adds is knowledge of what has been promised in that family before. A river is crossed announcing oneself, because [[lore-yombelspr|Yombel Ngoriring]] forbids crossing in silence and fouling the water above a village, and the requirements are the same at every ford. Before the season, a tribe goes to the cairn on high ground and tells [[lore-gorombespr|Gorombe]], the storm, what it means to build, plant and travel, so that what he breaks he breaks knowing. A monthly ashmark blessing at the local shrine renews the bond with the Flame.

The rest of the day is the herd: the count, the milking, the moving, the plot of sorghum and the talk. An Okharin does not experience the cairn and the pyramid as competing, because to him they are not.

## The Round of the Year

The year is told by the festival and the migration, and the festivals are held at the temple-cities, where the clans converge.

- [The Rekindling]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} falls at the summer solstice. Every temple fire in Okharis is put out and relit from embers carried from the First Ember, and pilgrims from every corner of the kingdom travel to the cities for a week of processions, feasting and tribal gatherings. It is for making the realm one fire again.
- [The Night of Ashes]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} falls at midwinter and honors the dead and the destructive aspect of the Flame. Families burn offerings to their ancestors and shamans appease restless spirits. The zohira are said to walk openly among mortals. It is for settling with the dead.
- [The Market of Flames]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} is the yearly trade festival at Kaljekor. Merchants make offerings at the Golden Flame's temple before the year's most important transactions, and oaths sworn during it are held sacred and unbreakable. It is for binding the year's bargains.
- **The festivals at each temple complex** draw pilgrims from across the kingdom and are where the clans' disputes are heard, marriages arranged and herds traded.

## Favor and Ash

Reward is the Flame's favor, shown in the herd, the harvest and the standing a person carries. The faithful walk [The Firewalk]{.name data-type="lore/custom"} and are said to have Nkaru'thar's direct favor if they cross unburnt. A warrior who proves himself in the Trial of Flames bears the Warden's Brand, the scars worn with pride. A candidate who survives the Spirit Hunt is a kwendari zohiring for life. A seeker of high priestly office fasts three days, keeping three sacred fires alight through storm and wind. A merchant who keeps his oath on the Scales prospers.

Penalty runs from the household to the cities. A debt of herds is paid in cattle, and a feud between kindreds is settled in cattle with a Keeper of the Feud recording blood owed and blood paid. A merchant who swears falsely on the Scales is ruined within a year and a day. After a great failure or transgression a penitent keeps [The Vigil of Ashes]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, covered in ash and sitting before a dying fire from dusk to dawn. A person who declines the Flame and takes up a foreign god is regarded in the tribal country as an eccentric and in the city-states as close to a traitor: the Vuthraka priesthood treats it as touching the defense of Okharis, and the line between heresy and treason has never been drawn with care. The person put out of the Flame is a **shurem**, "ash," whom no temple receives, who is given no ashmark at a passage, and whose fire is not reckoned descended from the First Ember. A person struck from the temple and village rolls is an outcast, denied their rites, grain and justice. The worst thing that can happen to an Okharin is [Spirit-Deafness]{.name data-type="lore"}, which is inflicted and not chosen, and the Okharins hold it far worse than being burned for refusing the Flame.

## The Overlord, the Priest and the Shaman

The question where civil authority ends and religious authority begins has three answers in Okharis. The Overlord, [[being-takorozanethar|Tákoro Zanethar]], is the temporal ruler and bears the religious title of Keeper of the Three Flames. He lives at Zarhanis beside the High Pyremant and relies on the cooperation of the city-states and the tribal leaders to hold power. The High Pyremant is the supreme spiritual leader, advises the Overlord, and decides doctrine, but in practice the Warden of the Flame at Vuthraka commands the armies and the council of priest-nobles at Kaljekor rules itself. The tribal chieftains and elders attend the moots held in the temple-cities, where matters affecting the tribes are decided.

A third authority stands outside both: the shamans of the [[affiliation-yenorzhrng|Yenor Zohiring]] hold no temple rank. A High Pyremant outranks every priest in Okharis and does not outrank a shaman, because they are not on the same ladder, and there is no procedure for what happens when the two disagree in a frontier village, and there never has been. Records belong to the temple scribes, who keep the registers of tribute owed, labor due and land held; the Tribute-Warden collects the tribes' dues with an escort and a scribe; the Granary-Keeper holds the stores the cities live on between harvests. Cases are heard by the elders and the clan's own custom, with the courts of the temple-cities for those entitled to them. When the questions conflict the parties go to the moot, and the temple and the tribe both treat what the moot decides as a matter of precedence and resources and never of whether the other is true.

## What the Flame and the Bones Ask of an Ordinary Day

The Okharins hold two accounts of the dead and see no contradiction. The Spirit Flame of Zarhanis governs the passage of the soul, and the temple has a full doctrine of where a soul goes and what carries it. The Oracle of Bones, the zohira of death, writes in the body what it discloses, and Bone-Readers attend the dead and read it. A widow may have both rites performed over the same husband a week apart, by people who would give incompatible accounts of where he now is, and she does not think she has a problem: he is where the Flame takes him and also what the bones say.

That is what the belief makes people do. The Flame is a cycle in which destruction feeds renewal, so loss is not resented. Yombel Ngoriring takes drownings and does not apologize for them, and a family that loses someone at a ford has been given the same warnings as everyone else. Gorombe has never taken anything he did not also give first. A household kindles its hearth from a flame carried from the nearest temple and kindles it again after a death, so that the family's bond to the Flame is renewed and not broken. Ashmark marks every passage. A man is generous in public and careful in private, because wealth walks and the account is counted by neighbors who watch. Money goes into the herd, the offering, the festival and the hospitality that doubles as a display of accounts. At the turning of the year a family goes to the pyramid; at the Night of Ashes it burns offerings to its ancestors.

## Reaching the Dead

The living reach the dead at the festival and at the cairn. On the Night of Ashes families burn offerings and shamans appease the restless, and the temple of Zarhanis holds communion with the ancestors under the Prophet of Light, the Ambelari Zheking. The Bone-Reader reads the Oracle of Bones in the body of the dead, which is the ordinary and daily form of the Oracle's service and requires no journey. A rarer form is the journey itself, undertaken by shamans who need an answer nothing nearer will give; some return with an answer, some without, and some do not return. What everyone who has reached the Oracle agrees is that it answers exactly the question asked, which is not the same as the question the asker meant.

The cost is the offering and the rite. The temple and the shaman make incompatible claims about the dead, the harvest and the blade, and the Guardian of Spirits (the Korthari Zohiring) is the temple's point of friction with the Bone-Readers. Neither side treats it as a problem requiring a solution.

## What a Person Owes

An Okharin owes in order: the herd first, because without it nobody owes anybody anything; then the lineage, then the clan, then the festival that must be attended, then the city that keeps the flame.

He does not put the king high on that list and will not pretend otherwise. What he will say is that the flame is owed a great deal, and that the king's business is keeping it lit.

In return a person is owed what each of those gives. The lineage gives its protection and its feud. The clan gives its grazing and its warriors. The festival gives a hearing for a dispute and a market for a herd. The city gives its metal, its trade and its legitimacy. The Flame gives renewal, and the zohira give the warnings their domains require.

## The Weak

The household is the first resort and the clan the second. An Okharin names his lineage before his clan and his clan before his city, and a widow, an orphan or an aged relation is placed by that sentence in a lineage and its herds. A stranger on the road is handed soured milk, cold from the shade, and the herders stand by to see whether he drinks.

Some stand outside. A bondsman is bound to a temple's estates or villages and owes labor for its granaries. The outcast is struck from the temple and village rolls and denied its rites, its grain and its justice. The shurem is put out of the Flame. In a bad year the Granary-Keeper's stores are what the cities live on, and the realm's true measure of power is the quantity of grain held in them. The clans have their herds, and a herd that walks can be moved to better grazing, though the eastern clans pay tribute to Bethua for grazing their grandfathers held free.

## Those Who Cannot Work

A shaman is called to heal and a household to keep its own. Kiroba is an apothecary as well as a shaman, and the sick calf and the sick child go to the same cairn. The zohira of fertility governs sown field, herd and birthing-house alike, and one cairn serves all three. A person who cannot herd or fight is kept by the lineage. Grazing rights are ancient and minutely known, and the old are the ones who remember them.

## Shelter, Teaching and Defense

Shelter is the lineage's and the clan's. Teaching is carried by the office: the elder shamans teach the Hunt, the temples teach the Students of the Flame, and the Blade-Singer judges whether a blade has chosen. A herder's child learns the grazing, the cairns and the rights from the household. Defense is the clans' warriors, who supply the realm's army under the Warden of the Flame, with the [Ashborn of Vuthraka]{.name data-type="affiliation/order"} as its spiritual anchor. The Captain of the Host commands armed men drawn from the cities and the tribes together, and the Overlord holds the balance among them by ceremony.

## The Line at Every Age

The household is the unit and the lineage its spine. Marriage is arranged between lineages and understood as an alliance of grazing rights as much as of families. A child is born under Pemburari Khutheling's eye, attended by the household or a shaman, and receives an ashmark at its first passage. A young person learns the herd and the cairns, and some are called to the Hunt. A warrior goes to the Trial of Flames, a priest to [The Trial of Three Flames]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}. An adult establishes a household with a hearth kindled from the temple flame, keeps the festival calendar and sits among the lineage's counsel. An elder remembers the rights. A dying person is attended by both the temple and the Bone-Reader, the hearth is lit again after the death, and the dead are fed at the Night of Ashes; a mourner may keep the Vigil of Ashes.

## What the Cities Do Not Notice

Every real culture has a blind spot its own thoughtful members argue about, and the Okharins' is the distance between the cities and the clans. The cities rarely notice that the rural clans feel neglected, and the tribal leaders spend their authority mediating a grievance the cities rarely notice. Kaljekor's trade wealth feeds its ambitions while Vuthraka demands more for defense, while the tribal chieftains, who attend the moots, carry the grievances of the herds to both. Thoughtful Okharins dispute whether the moot is where the clans are heard or where their complaints are absorbed.

The sin is the treatment of the person who declines the Flame. The Okharik faith is professed, and what is professed can be professed otherwise, but the city-states do not allow it: the Vuthraka priesthood treats refusal of the Flame as touching the defense of Okharis, and the practical difference between heresy and treason has never been drawn with any care. The person who takes up a foreign god is not leaving the zohira, who were never a matter of profession, but the cities answer as if he had left the realm. It is done, and it is rarely done twice.
