**The Factor Who Asked Twice** is the tale the port of [[place-ilovanu|Ilovanu]] tells a newcomer, usually on the first evening, usually in a factor's house above the quay and usually by a port [[lore-kaliharanclt|Kaliharan]] who has told it many times. It is told in the [[skill-valtarlng|Valtari]] of the quays, and it is the one Kaliharan telling a foreigner is sure to hear. It concerns a factor of [[place-helionis|Helionis]] who came to learn where the island's colored stones are dug, and who is not named in it; the teller says he has a name in Helionis and does not need one here.

Port children hear the same tale in Kalihari, with one difference. In the children's telling the tale stops when the factor's cargo is held for inquiry, with his question unanswered and the children laughing at him. The telling for newcomers goes on to where the factor sits down on the quay, and the teller ends it on the advice the factor gave every newcomer for the rest of his life, which is the reason the newcomer is hearing it.

: The Factor Who Asked Twice {#the-factor}

Sit down. You've had the wine, and the wine's good, and you're wondering what you can buy here that nobody else in your house has bought. I know. Everybody who comes up those steps on the first evening is wondering it. So you get the tale first, and then you get your business. That's the order.

You saw them on the way up. The gem dealers' arcades, the trays of them under the lamps, the blue ones and the green ones and the red that looks black until you hold it to the light. Those go out of this harbor to Vedyara and to the Taruzelian cities and to your own house, and they fetch what they fetch over there, and you know what that is better than I do. Everybody who has ever stood in that arcade has thought the same thing you're thinking now. If I could buy them where they're dug, before the coasters and the dealers and the arcade have had their share, I would go home a very rich man.

There was a factor from Helionis who thought it. A long time ago, before my grandmother. A clever man. Everybody says he was clever, and he was. His house had sent him with a letter of credit, a ship, and one instruction: find out where they come from.

Now, he'd done his reading. Helionis has a great many books about this island, and he'd read them all on the voyage, and they disagreed with each other about everything except one thing. The Kaliharans don't lie. Every book said so. A Kaliharan will not tell you a false thing. He'll tell you nothing, very politely, for as long as you like, but he won't tell you a false thing.

And the factor thought: well, then. If they don't lie, I only have to ask the right question, and they'll have to give me the right answer. All I need is to be exact.

He was very pleased with this. He told the captain about it at supper, the night before they raised the Ring. The captain had been to Ilovanu eleven times, and he said, ask once, and the factor laughed and said he would ask as many times as it took.

So he lands. The pilot brings him in off Telvari, the warden's clerk comes aboard and counts the cargo, and he's put in the foreign quarter like you've been, and on the second morning he goes to the warden's hall. You'll go there tomorrow. You'll see the bench. He sat on the bench all morning, and in the afternoon the Harbor-Warden saw him, and he asked his question.

He said: Warden, where do the colored stones come from?

And the Warden said: From the rivers behind Nalurithan.

Which is true. You can tell from the way I said it that it's true. The rivers behind Nalurithan, a day's sail up the coast.

The factor thanked him, very pleased, and got a letter from the House, and found a coaster going up to Nalurithan, and paid too much for the passage, and went.

At Nalurithan he went to the quay and found a man unloading baskets and asked him: Which river?

And the man said: The rivers behind the town.

The factor said: Yes, but which of them?

And the man looked at him for a while, and said: All of them, and went on unloading.

So the factor thought, I wasn't exact enough. He walked along the quay, and found an old woman mending a net, and he thought hard first, and he asked: Mother, where along the rivers are the stones found?

And she said: In the gravel.

He said: Who takes them out of the gravel?

She said: The lineage that holds the river.

He said: May I meet them?

She said: No.

And she went on with the net. The factor stood there. He hadn't been told a single thing that wasn't true. He hadn't been told a single thing.

He came back to Ilovanu and he was not pleased with himself any more. And he did a foolish thing, which is the first thing in this tale that you are not to do.

There was a clerk in the warden's hall, a young woman, very quick, who counted his cargo every time it moved and spoke four tongues to his one. He'd heard her called by a short name across the yard, by a man who was her cousin. You know about this. They told you on the ship, I hope. And one morning, to be friendly, because he wanted a friend in the warden's hall, he said: Good morning, Votha.

And she looked at him, and said: Marivotha Thavoeli.

Just that. Evenly. Her whole name, back to him, the way you'd hand a man back a coin he'd dropped.

And from that morning on, for thirty years, she called him by his full name and his house and his city, every time, and stood when he came in, and counted his cargo to the last clove, and he never got another word from her that wasn't the cargo. That's the first thing. Don't.

Now he was angry, and when a clever man is angry he gets cleverer, which is the trouble with clever men. He sat in his room in the quarter and he wrote down every answer he'd been given, exactly, because he was exact, and he looked at them, and he thought: they answer what I ask, and nothing else. So I'll ask the same thing again. A man who's asked twice thinks he didn't answer the first time, and he says more.

That's how it is in Helionis. Somebody told me. In Helionis you ask a man a thing twice and he takes it as a kindness, as if you were giving him another go.

So he went back to the warden's hall, and sat on the bench all morning, and in the afternoon the Warden saw him, and he asked: Warden, where do the colored stones come from?

And the Warden said: From the rivers behind Nalurithan.

And the factor said, very polite, smiling: Yes. But where do the colored stones come from?

And the Warden looked at him.

You've never seen a Kaliharan look at somebody. You will. It goes on a long time, and nothing moves in the face, and at the end of it you know you're somewhere you weren't when it started.

What the Warden heard was this. I told you the true thing, and you asked me again. So either you think I lied to you, or you think I left something out on purpose, and in either case you've decided you're owed something I didn't give you. And a man who thinks he's owed the interior of this island has told the wardens something about himself.

The Warden didn't say any of that. Kaliharans don't. The Warden said: Your cargo is held for inquiry.

And it was. Every bale of it, on the quay, under the clerk's eye, and you know what a warden's inquiry is. Your factor told you on the ship. Nothing moves it. Not money, not letters, not a word from his house. It's held until the warden is satisfied, and the warden is satisfied when the warden is satisfied, and that was a long time. That's the second thing.

That's where the children's tale stops. The factor sitting on his bale, held for inquiry, with his question in his mouth, and the children shouting at him: Ask again! Ask again! Ask the Warden a third time!

But you're not a child, and you're about to buy something, so you get the rest.

His cargo was held through Dry-Turn, and into Stillwater. He couldn't sell, and he couldn't buy, and he couldn't go home, because the cargo was his house's. So he did the only thing a man in Ilovanu can do for nothing. He sat on the quay.

At first he sat there because he had nowhere else to sit. After a while he sat there because it was the only thing left to do. And then, because he was clever, and because he was exact, and because there was nothing else, he began to look.

He looked at the coasters coming in. He saw which ones came from Nalurithan, because they came in round the north breakwater and their crews tied up with a different knot. He saw what came off them. Baskets, mostly. Spice. Bark. And now and then, maybe one coaster in eight, a small box carried by two men, and taken straight up the steps to the arcades, and never put down on the quay.

He began to count. He'd no papers, his papers were in the held cargo, so he counted in his head, the way they do here. He counted the boxes. He counted the coasters. He counted which days they came, and he noticed something.

They didn't come evenly. All through Dry-Turn, nothing, or next to nothing. One box in a month. He sat there through Stillwater, the long dry, and still nothing. And then the Wet-Turn came, the rains came over the Ring, and he got wet on the quay for a week and didn't move, and the rivers behind Nalurithan came down brown, you could see the brown in the sea off the point, and two weeks after the rain, the boxes started.

One coaster. Then two the same day. Then every coaster from Nalurithan for a month had its box, and some had two, and the arcades were lit up late every night, and the dealers sent their boys down to the quay to meet the boats before they'd tied up.

And he sat there with the water running off his hat and thought: the gravel. In the gravel. The old woman told me. The rains turn the gravel over, and the stones come up, and the lineage that holds the river takes them out, and they come down to Nalurithan two weeks after the rain. Every year. He'd been told everything he needed on the first day, by a woman mending a net, and he hadn't looked at it.

In the middle of Wet-Turn the Warden came down to the quay, which a Warden almost never does, and stood beside him, and looked at the sea for a while, the way he was looking at it.

And the Warden said: Your cargo is released.

And the factor said: Thank you.

He didn't ask why. That's what released it. He'd sat on that quay four months without asking anybody anything, and every clerk in the warden's hall had seen him there, every morning, at the same hour, in the same place, counting. The Warden was satisfied.

The Warden said one more thing. The Warden said: You have been looking at the sea.

And the factor said: Yes.

And the Warden went back up the steps.

The next year his house sent its ship to Nalurithan, not to Ilovanu, with a letter from the House, two weeks after the rain. He'd worked it out to the day, near enough. He didn't go up any river. He didn't ask anybody where anything was dug. He met the coasters on the Nalurithan quay as they came down, before the boys from the Ilovanu arcades had heard there was a boat in, and he bought at the quay price, with the spice and the bark, and his house did very well out of him for thirty years.

He never found out where they're dug. Nobody has. He didn't need to. He found out when they come down, and nobody told him that. He watched it.

He stayed. A lot of them do. He kept his house's warehouse here till he was old, and he had a seat on the bench outside the warden's hall that nobody else sat on, and every captain from Helionis who came up those steps on his first evening was sent to see him, the way you've been sent to me.

And every one of them asked him the question. You were going to ask me it. Where are the stones dug?

And he'd say: In the gravel.

And then he'd give them the only advice he ever gave anybody, and he gave it in Valtari so they'd understand it, and he gave it once:

Watch, and do not ask twice.

That's the tale. There's your wine. Now: what do you want to buy?
