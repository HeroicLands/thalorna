# terran_analog: "The farmer's day-book and the weather diary kept for a lifetime, and the oral household histories of island peoples recited at a death. Departs: nothing in it mourns, nothing in it explains, and the dead woman's words are said unchanged by the one who now holds them."

A [[lore-kaliharanclt|Kaliharan]] keeps a sílhari account from the evening she is given it at six or seven, adds to it every day of her life, and recites it to her household; at her death it passes into the household's recitation and is said in her words by whoever now holds her part. **The Handing of the Account** is the first saying of a dead woman's account by the one who holds it after her, at the first [Deepwater]{.name data-type="lore/calendar"} after the death, when the household keeps to its threshold and says its accounts over so that none drifts. The holder says the account through in the dead woman's words, adding nothing and leaving nothing out, and then says one entry of her own, which is the last entry of the account and the first of the holding.

The account rendered here is that of [Naeluvao Koathevoi]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, who held the lowest terrace of a household of [[place-koathevoi|Koathevoi]] on the eastern shore of [[place-klhrlake|the Bowl Lake]], as her daughter [Reithani Koathevoi]{.name data-type="being/npc"} said it at the Deepwater after she died. A full account holds an entry for every day of a life, and the saying of one takes the whole of a Deepwater. The household chose the entries rendered here as the ones it says when it says Naeluvao's account short, as households do for a guest. Where the daughter says her mother's household name, the rendering gives the full name, as the house would give it to anyone outside it.

: The Handing of the Account {#the-handing}

This is the account of Naeluvao Koathevoi, daughter of Thaelani Koathevoi, of the lowest terrace of this house, as she held it. I, Reithani Koathevoi, hold it now. I say it in her words.

_Dry-Turn, the seven hundred and ninety-fifth Walk, the first year after it._ This is the first of my account. The frogs on the lowest terrace stopped when I came down the wall. I stood still and they began again. I counted the voices I could tell apart. Six. That is what I saw.

_Dry-Turn, the same year._ The frogs. Six. The sun came over the Ring onto the water before it came onto the terrace.

_Stillwater, the same year._ My mother gave me the corner of the lowest terrace where the wall turns. It is mine to weed. There are nine rows in it.

_Wet-Turn, the second year after the Walk._ The first rain came on the fourth day after the new moon. My mother said it was two days early by her mother's account. The water came down the wall-drain of the lowest terrace and went into the lake brown, and by the evening it went in clear.

_Deepwater, the same year._ I said my account to the house. It was short. My grandmother said it was all there.

_Dry-Turn, the third year after the Walk._ At Dry-Turn the lake stood a hand below the foot of the lowest wall, at the dark line on the third stone from the corner, where my mother holds it and her mother held it. I put my hand on the line. The stone was cold.

_Wet-Turn, the fifth year after the Walk._ The neighbor of the terrace above ours, who holds the wall above our wall, came on the first day of the visits and said her frogs were early. Ours were not early. We compared until dusk.

_Stillwater, the seven hundred and ninety-sixth Walk, the year of it._ The custodians walked the terraces for the Tenth-Year Walk. The kaan'sili carried the tools behind them. One of the kaan'sili was from the next valley, and I knew her face. She did not look at me. They said the extended tending at the terrace stone of the valley, from the hour before sunrise until the sun was on the water. I watched from our wall. I was not told to go in.

_Dry-Turn, the second year after the Walk._ My mother gave me the lowest terrace, the whole of it, from the corner to the drain. It is mine to tend. She keeps the looking at it with me. The lake at the dark line on the third stone.

_Wet-Turn, the same year._ The swifts went out over the lake at dusk and came back in two flocks, not one. I counted the second flock. Forty and more. I could not hold the rest.

_Wet-Turn, the fourth year after the Walk._ I did not walk the wall this Wet-Turn. The child came on the sixth day of the rain. My mother walked the wall for me and held the drain. The drain ran clear by the third evening.

_Dry-Turn, the fifth year after the Walk._ The child came down the wall on my back. The frogs stopped and began again. The lake at the dark line on the third stone.

_Dry-Turn, the seven hundred and ninety-seventh Walk, the year of it._ The lake at the dark line on the third stone, and over it the width of a finger. I put my finger on it to be sure. My mother came down and put hers on it. Over the line, the width of a finger.

_Dry-Turn, the first year after the Walk._ The lake over the dark line the width of two fingers.

_Stillwater, the same year._ My daughter was given her first account at Dry-Turn. She counted the swifts. Nine. She says it every evening. She has not missed one.

_Wet-Turn, the same year._ The rain on its day. It came harder on the hills above than on the terrace. The drain of the lowest terrace ran brown three days, and on the fourth it ran clear.

_Dry-Turn, the second year after the Walk._ The lake at the top of the third stone. The dark line is under the water. I hold the lake by the stones of the wall from today, counting up from the third.

_Deepwater, the same year._ My mother's account came to me this Deepwater. I said it through for the house. I added nothing to it. My grandmother's is in it, at the place where my mother's mother gave her the terrace.

_Stillwater, the third year after the Walk._ The water end of the terrace is soft for the length of a row. I did not plant the first row this year. The seed would not take.

_Wet-Turn, the third year after the Walk._ The custodian who visits in rotation came on the third day. I gave her my count of the lake, stone by stone, from the dark line to the top of the third stone. She said the count back to me, stone by stone. I asked her why the lake stands higher. She said the count back to me again. Then she went up the wall to the next house.

_Dry-Turn, the fifth year after the Walk._ The lake at the top of the fourth stone. The Master of the Terraces sent clerks along the wall. They counted the lowest terrace, row by row, and the rows that would not take, and went on to the next house. They did not say anything.

_Dry-Turn, the seven hundred and ninety-eighth Walk, the year of it._ The lake at the fifth stone. Three rows soft. The frogs have gone up a terrace, to the neighbor's. I counted them from our wall. Seven voices. One I knew.

_Wet-Turn, the same year._ The swifts in one flock again. The rain a day late.

_Dry-Turn, the third year after the Walk._ The lake at the sixth stone. Half the terrace soft. The house planted the upper half, from the drain to the turn of the wall. I looked at the lower half. The water stood in the furrows and did not run off.

_Stillwater, the same year._ A reed came up in the lowest furrow. One.

_Dry-Turn, the seven hundred and ninety-ninth Walk, the year of it._ The lake at the seventh stone, which is the last stone of the wall below the top course. Reeds in the four lowest furrows. The barley in the upper half. Thin.

_Wet-Turn, the same year._ The rain on its day, and hard. The lake over the top course of the wall for nine days and back to the seventh stone after. The barley in the upper half lay flat. The house took what stood.

_Dry-Turn, the second year after the Walk._ The lake over the top course. It did not go back. The lowest terrace is under the lake from the corner to the drain. The wall stands in the water. I can see the top course from our second wall when the water is still. The house did not plant it.

_Dry-Turn, the same year._ The terrace is mine to tend. It is under the water. I do not know how a terrace under the water is tended. I looked at it from the second wall at the hour.

_Stillwater, the same year._ I looked at the terrace from the second wall. The water clear. I could see the furrows on the bottom, and the reeds standing in them, and fish between the reeds. Small. I counted eleven. That is what I saw.

_Wet-Turn, the third year after the Walk._ The custodian who visits came on the third day. I gave her the count. The terrace under the water, the top course under, the reeds, the fish. She said it back to me. She did not say anything else. I did not ask her anything.

_Stillwater, the fifth year after the Walk._ Reed herons on the terrace. Four. They stood in the water over the furrows where the seed-bed was, and fished. I watched them from the second wall from the hour until the sun was on the Massif. They took the small fish. That is what I saw.

_Deepwater, the same year._ I said my account to the house, all of it. My daughter said hers. She has the swifts and the frogs of the neighbor's terrace. Her frogs are the frogs that were ours.

_Dry-Turn, the eight hundredth Walk, the year of it._ The herons on the terrace. Six. Reeds over half the furrows. The fish more than I can hold. The top course of the wall under the water the depth of a hand.

_Stillwater, the second year after the Walk._ I did not go down to the second wall. My knee would not take the steps. My daughter took me down on her back, and I looked at the hour. Herons. Five. The water still.

_Wet-Turn, the third year after the Walk._ The neighbor came on the first day of the visits. She said her frogs were early. I said the herons. We compared until dusk.

_Dry-Turn, the fifth year after the Walk._ I looked at the terrace from the doorway. The water is to be seen from the doorway if the door is open and I sit low. Herons. Three. The Massif clear.

_Stillwater, the seventh year after the Walk._ From the doorway. The water still. Herons. Four. A fish rose over the place where the drain was.

_Deepwater, the eight hundred and first Walk, the year of it, the ninth day._ From the doorway. Rain on the water. I could not count the herons through it. The house said its accounts this evening. I said mine. It was long. My daughter said it was all there.

_Deepwater, the same Walk, the tenth day._ From the doorway. The water still. The Massif clear. Herons. Two.

That is her account, as she held it, and I have said it in her words. This is the first of my holding.

_Deepwater, the eight hundred and first Walk, the year of it, the eleventh day._ My mother did not look this morning. I looked for her, from the doorway, at the hour. The water was still. The Massif was clear. The herons: two. She died at the hour of the looking, sitting low in the doorway with the door open, and the house was with her. The lowest terrace is under the lake from the corner to the drain, and it is mine to tend. Tomorrow, at the same hour, from the doorway.
