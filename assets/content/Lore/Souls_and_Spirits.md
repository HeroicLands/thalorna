In the cosmology of Thalorna, the **soul** is the locus of identity and the connection between a being and the non-physical layer of reality. **Aura**, in game terms, is the measure of the soul's strength – its depth, coherence, and capacity to engage with the formless arcane potential.

Every living being has Aura. To have zero Aura would be to have no soul, and to have no soul would be not to live. The soul is what _makes_ the being living, in the metaphysical sense that matters. A body without a soul is meat. A soul without a body is a spirit. The two together are a mortal life.

The soul carries:

- **Identity.** What makes a person _that person_ – their continuity of self, their memory of their own existence, their sense of being one and the same across time.
- **Personality.** The character they have, the dispositions they've developed, the patterns of feeling and thought that distinguish them from anyone else.
- **Memory.** What they have done, what has happened to them, what they have learned and felt across their life.
- **Attunement.** The connection to the formless potential, to the non-physical layer of reality. The depth of this attunement is what Aura measures.

These are not separate components – they are aspects of the same thing, the soul, viewed under different lights. A damaged soul may show damage in any of these aspects; a healthy soul holds all of them together.

The body, while alive, _houses_ the soul. The two are tightly coupled but not identical. The body has its own physical state – health, age, injuries – that affects but does not fully determine what the soul does. A body with great vigor and a damaged soul produces a damaged person; a frail body with an intact soul produces a person of full identity in a failing physical frame. The relationship is dynamic.

When the body dies, the soul departs. Where it goes, what happens to it, and whether it can be reached or recovered are the substance of this document.

## The Soul-Spectrum

In Thalorna's cosmology, mortals, spirits, elementals, dragons, and gods are _all souls_. They are not different categories of being; they are different points on a single spectrum.

What varies along the spectrum is primarily the **scale and capacity of the soul itself** – the depth of its Aura, the breadth of its reasoning, the magnitude of what it can channel. A mortal human is a soul with ordinary Aura. A great Magister is a soul with high Aura, refined by training. A demi-divine entity is a soul of unusual strength, possibly augmented by partial divine inheritance. A dragon is a soul of vast strength embodied in a powerful material form. A god is a soul of such magnitude that the difference of degree becomes a difference of kind in every practical respect.

This continuity matters. It means there is no clean metaphysical line separating "natural" beings from "supernatural" ones. The peasant and the god are made of the same kind of stuff, in the same kind of structure, just at radically different scales. It also means that all the mechanisms that operate on souls – death and transit, framework-magic, predator spirits in the spirit realm, the protections of community – operate across the whole spectrum, with appropriate adjustments for scale.

The soul-spectrum permits, in principle, movement up and down it, though such movement is rare and usually one-directional. A particularly remarkable mortal soul may, through long development and unusual circumstance, become something more than mortal – a saint, a demi-divine entity, perhaps in extreme cases more. A diminished god may, conversely, fall in capacity over long ages – an entropy of divinity that no one observes directly but that the cosmology does not preclude.

## The Spirit Realm

The **spirit realm** (or simply _the Spirit world_) is the non-corporeal layer of reality. It is where souls go when bodies die, where elementals reside between manifestations, where the gods' agents move when not on errands, where ancestor spirits gather, where the fae come and go.

Critically, the spirit realm is **the connective tissue of all reality**. It is not just a part of Thalorna's cosmology; it is the layer through which all planes of existence are joined. Other planes exist – parallel material universes, the gods' own planes, perhaps stranger places besides – and the spirit realm is the only thing that connects them. Travel between planes, where it occurs, occurs through the spirit realm. There is no plane "beyond" the spirit realm except the planes of existence of the gods, which are accessed _through_ the spirit realm rather than apart from it.

This single architectural feature – _one spirit realm, many planes_ – has consequences that ripple through the rest of the cosmology.

### Structure of the Spirit Realm

The spirit realm has its own internal structure, though that structure is loose by material standards. Some features:

**It is mostly undifferentiated.** Most of the spirit realm is featureless to mortal-shaped perception. Souls there move through what feels like an open expanse – vast, fluid, dimensionless. Distance and direction operate differently than in the material world; spirits travel by intent rather than by linear movement, and the realm responds to attention more than to geography.

**Localized regions exist where presence has shaped the realm.** Where many souls have gathered, where significant events have occurred, where long traditions have established themselves, the realm takes on more defined character. The Aureldians' Field of the Blessed is a real region of the spirit realm, accreted from millennia of Aureldian death-traditions and divine attention. The Norden's Helheim is similar. The various ancestor pools of naturalistic peoples are likewise stable regions, sustained by the ongoing presence of ancestor spirits and the worship of their living descendants.

**The gods' planes are adjacent.** Each god maintains its own plane of existence. From the spirit realm, these planes are accessible – a soul guided to a god's plane crosses from the spirit realm into the god's domain. The transition is not always clean (the boundary between the spirit realm and a god's plane may be subtle, ambiguous, or contested), but the general architecture holds. The gods' planes are connected to the spirit realm rather than separate from it. To reach a god's plane, one moves through the spirit realm first.

**Other parallel material planes are also adjacent.** Thalorna's material plane is one of many. Other material planes exist, with their own physics, peoples, geography, and histories. From the spirit realm, these other planes are reachable – though only by spirits that know how, or by beings escorted by such spirits, or by mortals using thin places that connect their material plane directly to the spirit realm and thence to others. Most mortal souls never see another material plane; the journey is rare.

**Time flows differently.** Mortal time has only loose relevance in the spirit realm. A soul may experience what feels like a short period and emerge to find that years have passed in the material world; alternatively, a soul may persist in the spirit realm for what feels like ages while only a brief time has passed materially. The asymmetry is unpredictable and varies by region of the realm. This is one reason a "recently dead" soul can be sought by necromancy with reasonable hope of finding it; the soul's recency in mortal time may translate to a meaningful span in spirit-realm experience, but it remains within reach.

### The Spirit Realm Is Dangerous

Mortal traditions vary in how they describe the spirit realm. The most pious religions present it as a brief and protected passage to a god's plane. The animistic traditions present it as the home of the ancestors, a place of belonging. The philosophical and shamanic traditions tend to be more honest: the spirit realm is _vast, mostly empty, and contains predators_. A solitary soul in the spirit realm is at significant risk.

The risks come from several sources:

**Predator spirits.** Powerful, malevolent (by mortal standards) entities that roam the spirit realm hunting unprotected souls. They consume souls – actually consume them, breaking down the soul's substance and absorbing it into themselves. A consumed soul is gone: no afterlife, no reincarnation, no continuation. The predator grows incrementally stronger; the prey is ended. The transit of death, when unguided, is a window of particular vulnerability.

**Lostness.** A soul that does not know where it is going, or where it ought to go, may simply drift in the spirit realm without finding any destination. Without a god to claim it, an ancestor pool to receive it, or a totem to call it, a soul may persist in the realm for what subjectively feels like long ages, alone and unmoored. Such souls are more vulnerable to predators (no community to ward them), more likely to grow disordered (the spirit realm is not a stable environment for an isolated mind), and ultimately more likely to be consumed.

**Other spirits with hostile intent.** Not all hostile encounters in the spirit realm are with classic predators. A soul with grievances against another may pursue it across the realm. Spirits of opposing factions in life may continue their conflicts after death. Some spirits cultivate predatory habits without being native predators; they hunt and consume because they have learned to. The realm contains many kinds of dangers.

**The default fate of an unprotected soul.** This is the cosmologically grim truth that most religions either soften or deny: a soul that dies without being collected by a god, ancestor pool, totem, or other gathering force, _and_ that does not arrive in the realm with prior preparation, is most likely to persist in the spirit realm until it is found and consumed by predators. There is no peaceful neutral fate for the unaffiliated. There is no oblivion that quietly takes the unbeliever to nothingness. There is the spirit realm, alone, for as long as the soul can hold itself together – and then the predators.

This is the deepest reason religion matters in Thalorna. Beyond the blessings (real but mundane), beyond the priestly thaumaturgy (real magic), beyond the inspiration (real social and psychological benefit), religion provides _post-mortem reception_. A tradition's adherents are received and protected. The unaffiliated are not. The atheist's belief that "dead is dead" is, ironically, almost correct – for the atheist themselves. They will probably end. They are wrong only in their assumption that this is universal; people whose gods, ancestors, or totems claim them have a different fate.

The earliest and most naturalistic peoples understood this clearly. This is why ancestor worship works and why every culture that has lived close to the earth has practiced some form of it. The animistic traditions are not engaged in superstition; they are doing the genuinely necessary work of maintaining a soul-community that will receive and protect their members after death. Their cosmology is closer to the truth than many "more sophisticated" theologies'. Civilizations that have abandoned ancestor practices in favor of more institutional religions sometimes leave their members _more_ vulnerable in the after-state, not less – depending on whether the new religion's gods actually claim and protect their adherents.

### Hiding and Banding Together

A solitary soul _can_ find a hiding place in the spirit realm and persist for a time, but the hiding is fragile. Predators search the realm continuously, and a soul without a community to ward it is, eventually, found.

A _group_ of associated spirits is much safer. An ancestor pool – many spirits, gathered by lineage and tradition, often anchored by totems – has enough collective presence to ward against predators. A god's afterlife plane, populated by many received souls under divine attention, is fully protected. A totem-realm, where spirits serve a totemic patron, is also safe by the totem's strength. The protection comes from numbers and from the established relationships among the protected.

Could spirits in the realm form _new_ associations after death – banding together for mutual protection without prior affiliation? In theory, yes. In practice, almost never. The spirit realm is dangerous and disordered, and souls there do not trust strangers. Without prior bonds (lineage, religion, totemic alignment, sworn fellowship in life), souls in the realm have no basis to risk cooperation. The spirits who are trustworthy enough to band together are the spirits already part of pre-existing communities; the orphan souls find no one willing to take them in.

There are exceptions. Occasionally, particularly powerful or charismatic spirits gather small followings of orphans they have rescued from imminent predation. These are unusual and are themselves vulnerable – a small ad-hoc community has not yet built up the strength of a long-standing one. But they exist, in the deeper margins of the realm.

## Other Planes

The cosmology of Thalorna is not the cosmology of one universe. There are many.

Other parallel material planes exist, each with its own physics, peoples, history, and metaphysics. From the perspective of any one plane, the others are mostly inaccessible and largely unknown. The spirit realm connects them – a being in the spirit realm can, in principle, travel to any of them – but the connection is a thin one, and the travel is rarely undertaken.

Most mortals throughout Thalorna's history know nothing of other planes. The very concept is the property of philosophers, mystics, and the occasional foreign traveler whose claims their listeners cannot quite credit. Even the Sinalë, who know more about cross-planar reality than any human tradition, do not speak of it openly. The knowledge is reserved for those who have proven themselves able to handle it.

### Cross-Planar Travel

A spirit in the spirit realm – if it knows how – can transition to another plane. The skill is rare. Most spirits cannot do it. The spirits who can are typically those whose nature involves crossing layers – elementals (whose nature is to manifest from the spirit realm into a material plane), certain ancient or unusual beings, and a few cultivated practitioners of cross-planar magic.

The mechanisms vary:

- **Manifestation as an elemental.** A spirit of appropriate elemental affinity can manifest into another plane just as it can into Thalorna's material plane. This is the most common form of cross-planar presence: a spirit native to the spirit realm makes itself materially present in some other plane through channeling.
- **Quasi-material forms.** Some spirits have other modes of partial materialization suited to specific other planes. A spirit might appear in another plane in a form native to that plane – taking on the local physics enough to act there, while remaining fundamentally a spirit.
- **Direct passage.** Very rarely, a spirit can pass directly from the spirit realm into another plane in fully embodied form, taking on a body of that plane's physics for as long as it remains. This is unusual and typically temporary.

Mortals can also cross planes, though the mechanisms are different. A mortal whose plane has a thin place leading to another plane can, in principle, walk through. The destination is whatever lies on the other side. Such crossings are dangerous and unpredictable; few are voluntary. The mortal arrives in a foreign plane, often with no understanding of where they are, and must adapt or perish.

### Cross-Planar Visitors to Thalorna

Several categories of being native to other planes are nevertheless present on Thalorna, having come through thin places either deliberately or accidentally.

**The fae** are the most prominent example. They are not native to Thalorna's material plane; they come from another plane (or possibly several other planes, depending on the tradition asked) via thin places that connect to their home. They may stay in Thalorna briefly – a season, a year – or for long periods that mortals find difficult to track. Their nature is genuinely foreign. Their concerns, their values, their forms of intelligence are not Thalornan. They observe Thalorna from a distance even when physically present. Engagements with the fae are engagements with outsiders whose home is elsewhere and whose presence here is partial.

The fae are a varied category. Some are small and trickster-like; some are great and dangerous; some are curious and benign; some are predatory in ways that target mortals specifically. What unites them is their non-native origin. They are visitors. Their long presence in Thalorna does not naturalize them.

**The Sinalë and the Khazar peoples** are another instance of cross-planar arrival, though far older. Both peoples came originally from another plane – different planes, possibly, or perhaps the same one – through thin places, in ages so distant that for the Sinalë the journey is reverently remembered tradition rather than lived history, and for the Khazar it is mostly forgotten or mythologized.

This origin explains a great deal about both peoples. The Sinalë's longevity is not a strange variant on the human pattern; it is the natural lifespan of their species, brought from elsewhere. Their philosophical sophistication is not just the product of having lived long; it is the product of having _cross-planar perspective_. They came from somewhere else. They know that Thalorna's particular configuration of reality is one configuration among others. This is part of why their cosmological humility runs so deep: they have lived through making sense of a foreign reality, and they know that any framework – including the ones they themselves use – is always one possible framing among many.

The Khazar peoples bear similar marks of foreign origin, though differently inflected by their cultural development since arrival.

**Other beings.** Less prominently, the occasional spirit or being from yet other planes finds its way into Thalorna's material world. These are rare and usually solitary. Their presence is typically brief and unmemorable to those they encounter. Where they leave traces, the traces are difficult to interpret – strange ruins, mysterious objects, anomalous local mythologies that don't quite fit any neighboring tradition.

## Death and Transit

When a mortal body dies, the soul departs. The soul transits, first and universally, to the spirit realm. This is the universal architecture of death in Thalorna's cosmology: every soul, regardless of tradition, regardless of god, regardless of the eventual destination, passes first through the spirit realm.

What happens after that depends on what awaits the soul.

### The Window of Vulnerability

The transit itself is the most dangerous moment in a soul's existence. The soul has just been separated from its body, is disoriented, often weakened by the manner of death (illness, injury, violence), and is alone in the spirit realm without its mortal supports. Predators in the realm are most likely to find a soul in this window. A soul that lingers in the transit – confused, unprepared, unclaimed – is at acute risk.

Mortal traditions of death-rites and last-rites address exactly this danger. The dying are surrounded by family, prayed over, told what to expect, oriented toward their destination. The body is prepared in ways that the tradition says will help the soul transit safely. The community gathers to witness and support. None of this is ritual theater; it is functional preparation. A soul that has been well-prepared for transit moves through the window quickly and arrives at its reception. A soul that has died unprepared – alone, in confusion, without proper rites – is more vulnerable.

The traditions vary in the specific mechanisms they emphasize, but the underlying principle is universal: the moment of death is dangerous, and the dying need help. Cultures that have lost this knowledge have left their members at risk.

### The Pathways After Transit

Once a soul has arrived in the spirit realm, several possible pathways open up. Which pathway a soul takes depends on what receives it.

**The God's Plane.** A god who claims the soul of an adherent dispatches an agent to receive the soul in the spirit realm, escort it through the realm's hazards, and bring it to the god's own plane. The agent serves as guide, as protector, and as the god's hand in the realm. Once at the god's plane, the soul's fate is determined by the god's tradition: paradise, judgment, service, oblivion, transformation, or whatever else the religion holds. Different gods organize their afterlives differently. Some accept all their adherents; some are selective; some are predatory toward souls beyond their adherent base. A tradition's account of "what happens after death" reflects what its god (if engaged) actually does with souls of that tradition.

It is also possible – and this matters – that a god's plane includes territories for souls collected from beyond the god's own adherent base. A god of generous disposition may take in souls of related traditions, of associated peoples, even of strangers in distress. A god of greedy disposition may collect souls regardless of their tradition's claims, drawing into its plane any unprotected souls it can reach. The boundaries are not strictly tribal; they reflect the god's character.

**The Ancestor Pool.** The animistic and naturalistic traditions produce souls that flow into the spirit realm's _Ancestor_ regions – gathered communities of ancestor spirits sustained by the ongoing devotion of living descendants. These are stable regions of the spirit realm, accreted over many generations of ancestor worship, and they are protected by the sheer collective presence of the spirits within them.

Newly transitioned ancestor spirits are _coherent_. They retain personality, memory, and identity. They can communicate with the living through dreams, through shamans who visit in spirit, through hosting rites that allow them to speak briefly through the bodies of priests or kin. They are the most accessible ancestors, the ones whose grandchildren's grandchildren still know their names.

Over time – long time, sometimes generations, sometimes much longer – ancestor spirits _fade_. Their identity grows less distinct. Their memories blur. Their personality merges gradually into the broader presence of the ancestor pool. Eventually they are no longer individual spirits at all; they have become part of the undifferentiated soul-stuff of the region. The region itself is enriched by their contribution; the individual ancestor is no longer separately present.

This fading is not destruction. The soul is not consumed, not lost. It is _integrated_ – its substance contributing to the ongoing collective presence that protects future ancestors and serves the living. From the perspective of the soul, the fading is gradual and not painful; from the perspective of the living, the long-distant ancestor becomes a name in tradition rather than a contactable presence.

The Ancestor regions are among the safer places in the spirit realm, by virtue of their collective strength. Predators do not attack established ancestor pools (they are too strong; the cost is too high). The pools persist for as long as their living descendants continue to honor them. A pool whose worship has been forgotten – whose lineage has died out, whose tradition has been suppressed – gradually loses cohesion as the supporting flow of new ancestors and living devotion dries up. Such pools eventually disperse, the remaining spirits either drifting into the broader undifferentiated regions or – in worse cases – becoming vulnerable to predators that had previously been kept at bay.

**Totem Service.** Mortals deeply attuned to particular totem spirits – typically shamans, but also dedicated devotees of totemic traditions – may, on death, gravitate to the realm of their totem in the spirit realm rather than to the general ancestor pool. There they continue in service to the totem, becoming agents of it. They retain their identity and capacities, often more durably than they would in the ancestor pool, because the totem actively maintains them. They carry out the totem's purposes in the spirit realm and, occasionally, manifest into the material plane as agents of the totem.

This pathway is available primarily to those whose lives were already deeply aligned with a totem. A casual devotee will probably go to the general ancestor pool of their tradition; a shaman or dedicated practitioner of totemic relation may follow their totem instead.

**Reincarnation.** Some souls give themselves up to be _reused_ and _reforged_ into new bodies. The mechanism strips most memory and identity; the soul that emerges in the new body is mostly new, with only fragments of the old surfacing as déjà vu, dream-imagery, or unaccountable affinities. A few exceptional souls retain or recover more of their past – these are remarkable and possibly disturbing figures, and their tradition may or may not value such recall.

Different traditions hold different things about reincarnation. Some traditions hold it as the proper path, with karmic consequences shaping each new life. Some hold it as one option among several, available to souls who have not been claimed by other pathways. Some hold it as the universal default, with the apparent diversity of afterlife pathways being illusions or partial perceptions of the underlying single truth. The cosmology does not commit. Multiple traditions may be partly correct; reincarnation may be available to some souls and not to others, by mechanisms the GM can decide.

**Lingering as a Ghost.** Some souls, faced with the transit, refuse it. They cannot abide the thought of moving on. They cling to the place of their death – or to a place they loved, or to a person they cannot bear to leave – in the futile hope of returning to life. They are almost always unhappy. They cannot live again. They cannot move on. They are stuck in the worst possible state.

Lingering ghosts can persist for years, decades, sometimes longer. They are progressively damaged by the experience. Their faculties degrade – their memory grows fragmentary, their personality narrows, their capacity for action shrinks. Some become dangerous to the living, lashing out from frustration or hunger or simple incomprehension. Some become passive presences, observed but rarely engaging.

A lingering ghost can sometimes be helped to move on by mortal intervention (a properly conducted last-rites ceremony, a confrontation that resolves whatever held them, a shaman or priest with appropriate framework who guides them across) or by spiritual intervention (an agent of an interested god who has decided this soul is worth recovering). Without such help, the ghost persists until something ends them – usually a predator finding them in their long persistence, or the slow dissolution of the soul under the strain of refusing the natural transit.

**The Default Fate: Persistence and Predation.** The soul that has none of the above – no god to claim it, no ancestor pool to receive it, no totem to call it, no preparation for reincarnation, no will to linger – arrives in the spirit realm and finds no destination. It does not vanish. It does not naturally find its way somewhere. It persists, alone, in the realm.

For a time it may hide. It may find a quiet corner, a region the predators do not frequent, and persist there in solitude. But hiding is fragile, and the predators search the realm continuously. Sooner or later – sooner more often than later, for souls whose unprotectedness is also a sign of weakness – the soul is found and consumed.

This is the dark default. The unaffiliated, the unbeliever, the practitioner of a dead religion whose god has long since withdrawn or never existed – all face this fate. The atheist's expectation of "dead is dead" is, for the atheist, almost correct. They will not vanish at the moment of death; they will transit normally, persist for some span, and eventually be ended by predation. The end is real. The path to it is more drawn-out and more frightening than mere oblivion.

This is the cosmological reason religion functions in Thalorna. Not the only reason, but the deepest one. To be received after death is the most consequential gift a tradition can offer its adherents. Cultures that maintain their traditions, ancestor practices, and divine relationships are caring for their members in ways that go beyond this life. Cultures that abandon these things are leaving their members to the predators.

## The Origin of Souls

Where do new souls come from? On this question, the cosmology is genuinely unclear, and different traditions hold different things – none verifiable from inside.

**The reincarnation traditions** are the most certain. They hold that all souls are recycled. Every birth involves a soul that lived before, generally without retaining memory. The soul-pool is finite; what exists has always existed; nothing is lost and nothing is created. The cosmological elegance of this view is appealing, and traditions that hold it (including some Vedyaran lineages and parts of the Kalihara confederation) build sophisticated theologies around it.

**The animistic and ancestor traditions** sometimes hold that new souls come from the undifferentiated ancestor stuff in the spirit realm. As old ancestors fade and merge into the regional pool, the pool's substance becomes available – partly contributing to ongoing protection of newer ancestors, partly perhaps drawn upon for new births within the lineage. This view links the fading of ancestors to the arrival of new mortal lives in a single cycle. Some animistic theologies hold this explicitly; others imply it without articulating it.

**The afterlife-return traditions** hold that souls in a god's plane (or other afterlife regions) are eventually sent back – by the god's will, by some cycle of divine economy, or by some other mechanism – to be reborn into mortal lives. This is a kind of divinely-mediated reincarnation, distinct from the philosophical reincarnation traditions because it requires a god's involvement. Different religions that hold this version disagree on the mechanism, the timing, and the criteria.

**The fresh-generation traditions** hold that new souls are _created_ – generated freshly, perhaps by the gods, perhaps by the formless potential precipitating into individual identity, perhaps by some other process – when a new being is conceived or born. The soul is genuinely new. There is no recycling. The total quantity of souls is growing over time as more lives come into existence.

**The mixed-traditions** hold that more than one mechanism is in operation. Some souls are reincarnated; others are fresh; the two are not mutually exclusive. A new birth may involve either path, and which path applies in any given case may not be determinable.

**The agnostic traditions** – including, notably, the Sinalë's most sophisticated theology – hold that the question is not answerable from within the cosmology. The mechanisms by which new souls come into being are not directly observable; the reports of mortals are partial; the gods (if asked) do not answer. The honest position is that the question is open and may remain open indefinitely.

The cosmology of Thalorna does not commit. The GM may decide for any given soul or population which model applies – or may leave it open across the entire campaign, treating the question as one of the genuine mysteries of the world. None of the major game mechanics depend on the answer. Magic_Truth's account of Aura, the channeling mechanisms, and the death-and-transit architecture all work the same regardless of whether new souls are recycled or fresh. The question is theological, not mechanical.

A practical note for the GM: the _grim default fate_ described above does not depend on the origin question either. Whether souls are recycled or fresh, an unprotected soul still ends up in the spirit realm and faces predation. The horror of the unreceived soul is structural to the cosmology, not contingent on which origin theory is correct.

## Inhabitants of the Spirit Realm

The spirit realm contains many kinds of beings beyond the categories already discussed. A partial taxonomy:

**Ancestor Spirits.** Souls of the dead who have transitioned to the realm's Ancestor regions. Coherent when newly arrived, fading over time, eventually merging into the regional soul-stuff. Available to be contacted by living shamans and priests of their tradition.

**Totem Spirits.** Powerful spirits associated with kindreds, places, animals, concepts, or other anchoring entities. Some are very ancient. Their nature is collective rather than individual: a totem's substance is the accumulated weight of the relationships its devotees have maintained with it, often across many generations. A totem grows or weakens with the strength of those relationships. A totem whose devotees have all died or abandoned it eventually fades; one that has been continuously honored for a thousand years is correspondingly powerful.

Totems can be pursued by mortal shamans for guidance, contact, or alliance. They can also send manifestations into the material plane – typically as elementals (if the totem has an elemental affinity) or as visions, dreams, and other indirect contacts. Some totems are great enough to warrant their own pocket-regions in the spirit realm, populated by the spirits of their dedicated devotees.

**Lost Souls.** Souls that have transited but not been received, persisting in the realm without home. Most are eventually consumed; some hide for long periods before being found; very rarely some are recovered by an interested party (a divine agent, a shaman who has heard their cries, a remarkable saint who collects them). The lost souls are a continuing tragedy of the realm.

**Ghosts (Lingering Spirits).** Souls that refused transit and persist near the place of their death or other meaningful location. Distinct from lost souls in that ghosts are _anchored_ to a specific place rather than wandering the realm, and they may have presence in both the spirit realm and the material plane (their hauntings are perceptible to the living, though usually faintly). Ghosts degrade over time and may eventually become indistinguishable from lost souls if their anchor is destroyed.

**Predator Spirits.** Powerful malevolent (by mortal standards) entities that hunt unprotected souls. Their origins are unclear. Some may be ancient lost souls who developed predatory habits in the long isolation; some may be spirits that emerged from particularly dark thin places; some may be entities from other planes that have settled in the spirit realm because the hunting is easy. They vary in size, power, and strategy. The smallest are quick scavengers that pick off the very weakest souls; the greatest are slow vast presences that few souls escape once detected.

The realm's predator population is not static. Predators eat each other, are killed by the rare powerful spirits with reason to oppose them, are driven off by ancestor pools and divine plane defenses, and occasionally are confronted by mortal shamans on rescue missions. The total predator population fluctuates over centuries.

**Spirits of Place.** Beings associated with specific places, but unlike anchored ghosts, these are not necessarily ex-mortal souls. As described below in the discussion of place-spirits, the category is heterogeneous: some are localized elementals, some are anchored ancestor spirits, some are cross-planar visitors. What unites them is their association with a particular location.

**The Fae and Other Cross-Planar Visitors.** Beings native to other planes who have come to Thalorna's material plane via thin places. They occupy regions of the spirit realm corresponding to their cross-planar connections – sometimes establishing waypoints there, sometimes simply transiting through.

**Spirit-Agents and Demi-Divine Entities.** Agents of the gods, as developed in [[lore-divineactn#agents-of-the-gods|Divine Action]]. They are spirit-realm natives (or partially so) who serve their patron deities. Their cosmological treatment is given in that document; here it is enough to note that they are part of the realm's population and routinely move through it.

**Elementals (Between Manifestations).** Elementals reside in the spirit realm between their manifestations into the material plane. Their treatment is below.

### Place-Spirits in Three Categories

The category of place-spirits – beings associated with specific locations on the material plane – is broad and includes beings of multiple origins:

1. **Localized Elementals.** Some place-spirits are elementals whose manifestation has anchored to a specific place. The river-spirit of a particular river, the fire-spirit of a particular forge, the spirit of a sacred grove – these are often elementals whose channeling is specifically configured for that location and that elemental aspect. They behave as elementals do but are bound, by something in their nature or their long custom, to a single locality.

2. **Manifested Spirits.** Some place-spirits are former mortals – ancestor spirits who chose to anchor themselves to a place, ghosts that have settled into longer-term residence at meaningful sites. The grandmother spirit who watches over her former home; the old warrior whose presence is felt at the battlefield; the child whose laughter is heard near a long-vanished village. These are spirits with origins traceable to mortal lives.

3. **Cross-Planar Visitors.** Some place-spirits are beings from other planes who have come through thin places and remained, or who maintain ongoing presence in Thalorna by passing back and forth. The fae are the most prominent example. A fae court that has anchored itself to a particular forest is a cross-planar visitor of this kind – the court is not native, but it has made the forest its home (often without much regard for whatever was there before).

These three categories shade into one another at the edges. A long-resident fae may, over centuries, become indistinguishable in behavior from a localized native elemental of the same place. A particularly powerful ghost may take on elemental characteristics. The categories are useful for the GM to track but not always cleanly separable in practice.

## Elementals

Elementals are _spirit creatures_ – natives of the spirit realm – whose nature involves manifesting in the material plane through the continuous channeling of a single elemental aspect.

### Nature

An elemental's essence is a soul (Aura) of unusual configuration: deeply attuned to one elemental aspect (fire, water, air, earth, metal, spirit, or others), and structured in a way that drives constant channeling of that aspect. The soul's natural mode of being is to channel its element – instinctually, continuously, automatically. When the elemental manifests in the material plane, the channeling produces a _body_ of that element. The fire elemental's flames, the water elemental's waves, the earth elemental's stones – these are the soul's channeling made manifest. The body is the channeling, sustained moment to moment.

This is structurally different from how a dragon's body works (a dragon has a real physical body, separate from its channeling; see below). It is also different from how a mortal body works (a mortal body is biological, generated by the natural processes of their species). An elemental's body is _projected_ – sustained by the soul's continuous channeling, not generated and not biological.

### Manifestation, Destruction, and Reassembly

An elemental in the material plane can be **destroyed** – its material body undone, the channeling-projection dispersed. This is what happens when an elemental is "killed" in the standard fantasy sense of being defeated in material combat. The body breaks apart; the magical effect dissipates; what was a fire elemental is suddenly just smoke and embers. From the material-plane observer's perspective, the elemental has been killed. From the cosmological perspective, the elemental's _soul_ has not been killed. The soul has retreated into the spirit realm, where it persists as a powerful spirit.

After destruction, the elemental's spirit needs time to gather itself, to re-establish the configuration that allows continuous channeling, and to reassert manifestation in the material plane. The time varies enormously. A minor elemental may reassemble within minutes or hours; a great elemental may need centuries. During the period of non-manifestation, the spirit is present in the spirit realm but is not exerting itself in the material plane.

This means that combat against an elemental on the material plane is _only ever a temporary defeat_. The spirit is not gone. It will, eventually, return – usually in the same locality, often with some grievance against whoever destroyed its previous manifestation. Mortal traditions know this in folk form: the river-spirit who was driven off returns next spring; the forge-spirit who was bound and dispersed comes back generations later when the binding has weakened.

To **kill** an elemental – permanently – one must engage it in the spirit realm, where it has no material body and is simply a spirit. There it can be destroyed by another spirit, by a powerful framework-mage operating in the spirit realm, by the action of a god's agent, by a sufficiently powerful predator spirit, or by other means. Killing in the material plane is impossible; killing in the spirit realm is dangerous and rarely undertaken by mortals.

In the spirit realm, it should be noted, the elemental is not strictly an _elemental_ – it is a _spirit_. Its elemental nature is its mode of manifestation, not a property of its spirit-realm existence. A spirit-realm encounter with what is normally a fire elemental is an encounter with a powerful fire-affinity spirit, not with a creature of flames. The terminology shifts to match the realm.

### Variety and Distribution

Elementals occur with elemental affinities corresponding to the major elements of the cosmology: fire, water, air, earth, metal, spirit (or various others, depending on tradition). They vary enormously in power. The smallest are barely-conscious wisps that may exist only briefly; the greatest are entities of vast scale, capable of manifesting across whole regions and producing effects that change weather patterns, reshape coastlines, and persist for centuries.

The geographic distribution of elementals is not random. Thin places – locations where the membrane between material and spirit is permeable – concentrate elemental activity. A grove that is a thin place will often have elementals of various kinds gathered there; a sacred site, a ritual circle, a long-haunted ruin. Elementals are also concentrated in places matching their affinity: fire elementals near volcanoes and forges, water elementals at rivers and lakes, air elementals in storms and high places.

Mortals can sometimes summon elementals (see Summoning, below) or bind them (a more complex and morally fraught practice). Most mortals' encounters with elementals are passing – a brief manifestation, observed and then gone.

## Dragons

Dragons in Thalorna are mortals.

This is the crucial point that distinguishes dragons from much of the standard fantasy treatment. They are not gods; they are not demi-divine; they are not spirit-realm entities; they are not extraplanar beings. They are _mortal creatures of the material plane_ – born, living, aging (slowly), and dying like any other mortal species. They have bodies – actual, physical bodies of immense size and power. They have lifespans – long, possibly very long, but finite. They have souls, which is to say Aura, in measurable quantities. They die. When they die, their souls transit to the spirit realm, just like the souls of any other mortal.

What makes dragons unusual is _the scale of what they are_. Their souls are extraordinarily strong. Their Aura is vast – far beyond mortal-human range, approaching but not reaching the scale of gods. This is a property of their species, the way a mortal of any species has whatever Aura range their species permits. A dragon's Aura is to a human's as a great river is to a stream: the same stuff, the same kind of phenomenon, but at a different magnitude.

### Channeling at Vast Scale

The vast Aura of dragons makes their channeling correspondingly vast. Dragons channel instinctually, like other magical creatures, but the scale of their channeling exceeds anything mortals can match. Dragon-fire, dragon-flight, dragon-magic – all of this is the natural product of a dragon's Aura plus the instinctual framework dragons inherit by their nature. They do not study; they do not train; they do not need frameworks of the kind mortal mages use. Their nature does the work.

The destabilization effect is the most consequential consequence of dragon-channeling. A dragon's continuous instinctual shaping of local potential is so dominant that it occupies the elemental currents in the area. Mortal mages near a dragon cannot maintain stable frameworks because the dragon's channeling overrides theirs. The mage's spells misfire, partly channeled before the framework collapses; the framework collapses because the underlying potential is no longer where the framework expected to find it; the result is wild discharge of partially-channeled magic, often with catastrophic consequences for the mage.

This is the property the Vylarian Republic exploited in the Helionite war. It was not a weapon they invented; it was a property of dragons themselves, recognized and turned to military use.

### The Dragon Ancestor Pool

When a dragon dies, its soul transits to the spirit realm. There it joins the _dragon ancestor pool_ – a powerful collective of dragon souls that has accumulated across whatever ages dragons have existed. The pool is not the same as a human ancestor pool. It is larger in some respects, smaller in others (there have presumably been fewer dragons over history than humans), and it operates by dragon norms – whatever those are – rather than human ones.

What is certain is that the dragon ancestor pool is _powerful_. Other forces in the spirit realm do not mess with it. Predator spirits do not attack it. Other gods' agents do not interfere with it. The dragon ancestors' collective Aura, even diluted across however many dragon souls have accumulated, is enough to ward against any threat the realm contains. Dragons in death are even more imposing, as a collective, than they were individually in life.

The pool functions as the dragons' totem and afterlife in one. Surviving dragons, in life, presumably have some relationship with their ancestor pool – whether through dreams, through ritual, through the mediation of dragons who serve as bridges to the dead, or through some other means is not part of mortal-accessible knowledge. The dragons do not share their internal cosmology.

### The Vylarian Compact and Its Betrayal

In the years before the founding of the Ordo Arcanis, the Vylarian Republic, locked in the Helionite war, found itself losing to mage-warlords whose unrestricted arcane work was tearing through the Republic's legions. The Republic needed something that could break magic, and it discovered that dragons, by their nature, did exactly that.

How the Republic made contact with dragons is not part of the documented record. The Ordo's archives, suppressed and incomplete, do not say. What the records do say is that the Republic and the dragons reached some kind of agreement, and that dragons participated in the war, and that the Helionite mages were broken and the war ended.

What was promised to the dragons in exchange for their participation is not known. The agreement may have included specific lands, specific recognitions, specific freedoms, specific protections. It may have included something stranger and more particular – concessions on matters that mattered to dragons in ways the Republic understood imperfectly. Whatever was promised, it was substantial enough that the dragons agreed.

After the war, the Republic faced a new problem: with the war won, the dragons were no longer needed, and their continued presence was incompatible with the new arcane institutions the Republic was establishing. The Ordo Arcanis, founded out of the conquered Helionite College of Arcane Philosophy, could not function with dragons nearby. Their research would fail; their training would be impossible; their institutional development would collapse. A choice was forced: either give up the Ordo (and accept that the Republic's arcane future would be something else) or break faith with the dragons.

The Republic chose to break faith. The specifics – what was done, when, by whom – are among the most carefully suppressed records in Vylarian history. What seems certain: many dragons were killed. Some escaped. Some may have been captured, bound, or driven into hiding. The Dragon Riders who had bonded with dragons during the war were quietly disbanded; their dragons were taken from them, killed, or vanished.

The killed dragons' souls went to the dragon ancestor pool. Their grievances are remembered there. The surviving dragons remember directly. Wherever they are now, however many remain, they have not forgotten and they have not forgiven.

### The Dragons Now

Where the surviving dragons are, what they are doing, and what they intend are open questions. Several patterns are possible:

- **Hidden and waiting.** The surviving dragons may be in deep hiding, perhaps in remote regions of Thalorna that mortals do not visit, biding time until conditions favor their return. Centuries of suppressed grievance may translate into patient strategy.
- **Scattered and broken.** The Republic's betrayal may have done more damage than even the dragon ancestor pool can repair. The survivors may be few, isolated, and individually limited in what they can do – though even a few dragons remain enormously powerful by mortal standards.
- **In contact with mortals.** Some surviving dragons may have made cautious contact with particular mortal individuals or groups – sharing some knowledge, accepting some service, building relationships outside the Ordo's awareness. Such contacts would necessarily be discreet.
- **Preparing.** The surviving dragons, possibly in counsel with the ancestor pool through whatever means dragons use for spirit-world consultation, may be preparing some response to the betrayal. The response may be slow – dragon time-scales are not human time-scales – but it may be coming.
- **Some combination.** Different dragons in different places may be doing different things. The dragon nation, if it can be called that, is not necessarily unified in its approach to the post-betrayal era.

The Ordo and the Empire have spent five centuries hoping the dragons forget. This is a false hope. Dragons do not forget. The dragon ancestor pool does not forget. The question is not whether the betrayal will eventually have consequences but when, in what form, and whether the consequences will be addressable when they arrive.

The GM has wide latitude in deciding the dragons' current state and intentions for any given campaign. The cosmological framework supports any of the possibilities above; campaigns can be constructed around discovering which apply, around encountering specific surviving dragons, around the ancestor pool's eventual response, or around the slow return of dragons to the open world after their long withdrawal.

## Necromancy

**Necromancy** is the deliberate manipulation of souls by mortal practitioners. It is distinct from shamanic ancestor communion, which is a separate practice; the two should not be confused.

### The Distinguishing Feature: Coercion

What makes necromancy _necromancy_ is its coercive structure. The necromancer pulls a soul back from the spirit realm against the soul's will (or at least without the soul's free consent), and binds it to a corporeal medium for the necromancer's purposes. This is fundamentally an act of force on a soul that has begun its proper transit, removing it from where it should be and placing it where the necromancer wants it.

This is what virtually every culture in Thalorna recognizes as necromancy, and what virtually every culture considers – by its own moral framework – to be evil. The exceptions are narrow: certain Vedyaran traditions practice forms of brief soul-consultation that are sometimes classified as necromantic by outsiders but that the Vedyarans themselves consider distinct; certain Kheperi mortuary practices involve elements that resemble necromancy but operate within a religious framework that recasts them. These exceptions aside, necromancy is widely held to be morally serious in any culture whose moral framework recognizes souls.

### Distinction from Shamanic Practice

Shamanic ancestor communion, despite involving interaction with the souls of the dead, is _not_ necromancy. The mechanism is fundamentally different.

When a shaman seeks contact with an ancestor, the shaman _transfers their own soul_ to the spirit realm, travels to the ancestor pool of their lineage, and either:

- **Communes directly.** The shaman speaks with the ancestor in the spirit realm, returning afterward to the body to convey what was learned.
- **Hosts the ancestor.** The shaman returns to their body and opens it as a temporary vessel for the ancestor spirit, who can then speak through the shaman or otherwise act briefly through the shaman's flesh. The ancestor's stay is consensual on both sides; when the consultation ends, the ancestor returns to the spirit realm and the shaman resumes full occupancy of their body.

In neither case is the ancestor _pulled back_ against will or _bound_ to a corporeal medium. The shaman goes to them. The ancestor is consulted on their own terms. Both parties consent. The interaction respects the ancestor's continued residence in the spirit realm.

This is not necromancy. It is sacred practice, and animistic and shamanic traditions are entirely correct to defend it as something different in kind from what necromancers do. The Quaesitorium and other authorities sometimes lump the two together for political reasons (it is convenient to call all soul-related folk magic "necromancy" so as to suppress it uniformly), but the cosmological distinction is real and matters.

### Forms of Necromantic Work

Necromancy proper produces several kinds of effects, depending on the practitioner's skill, framework, and intent.

**Forced Brief Consultation.** A necromancer can pull a soul – typically a recently dead one – back to the material plane briefly, bind it temporarily to a corporeal medium (a corpse, a sympathetic object, a binding device of some kind), and compel it to communicate. The soul is held in the binding only as long as the necromancer's framework sustains it; once released, the soul returns to its transit, often weakened by the experience and possibly damaged. The work requires substantial skill: the medium must be appropriate to the specific soul, the binding must be precise enough to compel without dissipating, the questioning must be efficient enough to extract useful information before the binding fails.

This is the "I command you to speak from beyond the grave" practice that appears in folk tradition. It is real, it is illegal in most places, and it produces real but limited results. The bound soul is angry, often vague, sometimes deceptive. Information extracted under this kind of compulsion is unreliable.

**Animation of Corpses.** A necromancer can pull a soul (or a fragment of one) and bind it to a corpse, animating the body. The result is a damut – undead in the standard sense. Damutu are described in detail in [[lore-undead|Undead]].

The simplest form of this work is _fragmentation animation_: the necromancer does not care about the integrity of the soul being pulled and just grabs enough soul-stuff to drive a corpse into motion. The result is an unintelligent damut – a zombie or similar. This is the easiest form of necromancy, achievable by relatively unskilled practitioners. It also damages the soul severely; what was pulled is fragmented, and whatever remains of the original person is destroyed in the process.

More skillful animation seeks to retrieve the soul intact (or nearly so) and bind it to the corpse with more delicacy. This is much harder. It produces damutu further along the spectrum of degradation – the Recently Returned, who retain identity and personality, at least at first. This kind of work is what people commission when they want a specific dead person back, knowing the result will be diminished. The diminishment is not optional; even the most skillful necromantic work cannot fully restore the soul to its body's animation. The soul has departed, and the framework binding it back is a poor substitute for the natural body-soul coupling.

**Sponsored Tereb Creation.** The most sophisticated form of necromantic work is the creation of a tereb. This requires not just necromantic skill but a willing or unwilling subject who has undergone the preparatory rituals during life. Terebu are described in detail in [[lore-undead|Undead]].

### What Necromancy Costs the Practitioner

The cosmology does not impose automatic soul damage on the practitioner of necromancy. A necromancer's own soul is not eroded by the work itself, in metaphysical terms. The cosmology of Thalorna is generally non-moralistic at the metaphysical level; good and evil are cultural categories, not features of the cosmos's accounting (see [[lore-godsrelign#order-and-chaos-are-objective-good-and-evil-are-not|Gods and Religion]]).

What the necromancer does pay:

- **Ordinary framework-magic costs.** Necromancy is framework-magic, and operating it requires the practitioner's Aura and reasoning, with the standard fatigue and concentration costs. A sustained necromantic session is exhausting, like any major working.
- **Moral and theological consequences.** If the necromancer's god (if any) considers necromancy a violation, the god's response – withdrawn favor, dispatched agents to oppose, refusal of post-mortem reception – can be severe. Many gods do consider necromancy a grave matter and treat practitioners accordingly.
- **Cultural and legal consequences.** Most cultures forbid necromancy. The Quaesitorium hunts practitioners aggressively. Folk communities respond to discovered necromancers with violence. Operating as a necromancer requires either deep concealment or operating in a context where it is tolerated (rare).
- **Conscience.** A necromancer who does retain ordinary moral feeling experiences the work as morally serious. The souls they manipulate are persons, and treating them as raw material – even in the necromancer's own framework – produces psychological costs. Many necromancers eventually develop the kind of moral hardness that lets them ignore this; the few who do not often stop practicing or come to bad ends.

These costs are sufficient to make necromancy difficult and dangerous as a practice without requiring additional metaphysical penalties. The cosmology lets necromancy be evil by every culture's standards while not being evil at the metaphysical level – consistent with the document's general approach to morality.

## The Undead

The undead are beings that should be dead but are not, animated through necromantic or related work. The category includes everything from the simplest reanimated zombies (**damutu**) to the most powerful undying lords (**terebu**); the two learned terms are Kheperi, and every scholarly tradition uses them.

The undead are produced by mechanisms grounded in the soul-cosmology established in this document – the pulling-back of souls from the spirit realm, the binding of soul-substance to material bodies, the elaborate rituals by which powerful individuals arrange to persist past death. The cosmological foundation belongs here; the development of the undead themselves – damutu along their spectrum of degradation, terebu with their consent-and-quality dynamics, the social and political structures of undead communities, and practical guidance for running them – is treated in detail in [[lore-undead|Undead]].

The key cosmological points to keep in mind when reading other documents:

- **No mortal can make themselves undead.** The transformation always requires action by an external agent on a soul that has died or is dying. Self-application is structurally impossible.
- **All the undead have creators**, who typically retain some degree of control over them.
- **Damutu are produced by necromantic animation** – the binding of a soul, or fragments of one, to a corpse. The result varies enormously in quality depending on the necromancer's skill and the soul's state.
- **Terebu are produced by elaborate ritual during life**, ending in the candidate's death and a delayed arising. The ritual does not require the original performer to be present at the moment of arising.
- **All the undead degrade over time**, though terebu degrade much more slowly than damutu.
- **Terebu regenerate predatorily**, by harming the living. There is no benign feeding pattern.

For the full treatment, see [[lore-undead|Undead]].

## Aura Damage and Soul Damage

A soul can be damaged. The damage shows up in life as reduced Aura – which translates mechanically into lower attribute scores, reduced framework-magic capacity, and various other diminishments – and in death as compromised post-mortem state, with damaged souls being more vulnerable to predation, less able to cohere in ancestor pools, and so on.

### Causes of Aura Damage

Several mechanisms can damage a mortal's Aura:

**Magical attack on the soul directly.** Certain framework-magic can target the soul rather than the body. Aural Shock effects in HMK terms; soul-strike abilities of certain magical creatures (particularly terebu of regeneration-via-Aura traditions); curses that specifically attack the victim's connection to the potential. These are the most direct cause of soul damage.

**Necromantic involvement.** A soul that has been pulled back, even briefly, even with some attempt at care, takes damage. The pulling itself disturbs the soul's coherence. Souls that have been animated as damutu and then somehow released back to their proper transit are severely damaged. Souls used in necromantic consultation are damaged less but still meaningfully.

**Trauma so severe it reaches the soul.** Most ordinary trauma (physical injury, common emotional pain, social loss) damages the body or the personality but does not reach the soul. Some kinds of trauma do reach the soul – encounters with predator spirits that fail to consume the victim, exposure to certain dark magics, prolonged contact with terebu that drain Aura, the experience of losing a deeply bonded companion or shared soul-substance partner. Soul-deep trauma is rarer than ordinary trauma but more durable.

**Long-term proximity to soul-damaging environments.** Certain places and conditions slowly erode souls. Long captivity by terebu. Long residence in places where predators have fed and left residue. Long use of certain forbidden frameworks. The damage in these cases accumulates over time and may be unnoticed until it has become significant.

**Self-inflicted damage from repeated frame-collapse.** Mages who repeatedly suffer critical failures with significant magical workings damage their own souls – the discharge of unchanneled potential, repeatedly directed back through the failing framework into the mage's own being, leaves marks. Reckless practitioners who push wide channels they cannot reliably control are at greatest risk.

### Recovery

Aura damage can be partially recovered through several mechanisms:

- **Time and rest.** The soul's natural resilience can mend small damages over months or years of healthy life.
- **Framework-magic from healers.** Some thaumaturgic priests of healing traditions, and some framework-mages who have specifically trained in soul-work, can mend Aura damage. The work is delicate, expensive, and risky to perform.
- **Divine intervention.** A god who has reason to favor the damaged soul may dispatch an agent to perform soul-mending. This is rare and typically reserved for figures the god has reason to value.
- **Time in protective environments.** Long residence in a thin place with positive ambient character (a sacred grove, a holy site, a long-tended ancestor shrine of one's own lineage) can support gradual recovery beyond what time alone provides.

Recovery is rarely complete. A soul that has suffered significant damage usually retains some residue of it for the rest of the body's life. Permanent Aura loss is the typical outcome of severe damage. The damaged person remains diminished – sometimes mildly, sometimes severely.

### Death and Damaged Souls

A soul that dies damaged carries the damage into the spirit realm. Damaged souls are:

- **More vulnerable to predators.** They lack the coherence and presence to defend themselves; predators recognize them as easier prey.
- **Less able to integrate into ancestor pools.** A soul too damaged to maintain identity may not be coherent enough to join the protected community; it may instead drift in the realm's outer regions.
- **Less able to be received by gods.** A god whose reception requires a candidate of certain coherence may not accept a severely damaged soul; the soul is either turned away (and faces the default fate) or accepted in some lesser capacity.
- **Less able to reincarnate cleanly.** Damaged souls that are recycled into new bodies sometimes carry the damage forward – producing new lives that begin already diminished, with reduced Aura, possible inherited troubles, and other compromises.

The damaged soul's fate is therefore proportionally worse, on average, than the intact soul's. This is part of why protecting one's own soul during life – through right practice, through avoidance of soul-damaging activities, through recovery when damage occurs – is a serious matter in the cosmology, beyond the in-life consequences of reduced Aura.

## GM Notes: Running Soul Material

Several practical implications follow from the cosmology established here.

**The dark default is significant.** The cosmological position that unprotected souls face predation is one of the most consequential pieces of Thalorna's metaphysics. It changes the moral and existential weight of religion, ancestor practices, and other tradition-maintenance work. The GM should keep this in mind when running campaigns: characters who think about death seriously should think about _what awaits them_. Characters who are atheists in a real sense (not just irreligious in passing but committed unbelievers) should know – or should be confronted with – the cosmological implications. The GM does not need to make every campaign about post-mortem destinations, but the option is always there.

**Most NPCs do not know all of this.** The dark default, the spirit realm's structure, the universal first transit, the existence of cross-planar visitors – these are not commonly known. Most of Thalorna's mortals know what their tradition teaches them, which is partial and possibly mistaken. A wise village priest will know roughly what their religion holds; a Sinalë sage will know much more; a peasant who has never thought about the question will know almost nothing. The GM should let the setting's knowledge be unevenly distributed.

**The Sinalë are again the cosmological-honest narrators.** When the GM needs an NPC to articulate the cosmology accurately, a Sinalë sage is the best source. They will speak with appropriate care – they know that telling unprotected mortals about the dark default can be cruel – but they will not deceive a sincere inquirer. A campaign that brings characters into contact with a Sinalë sage on these matters can be transformative for the characters' worldview.

**Death is consequential and should feel so.** The cosmology supports treating mortal death – the death of player characters, of beloved NPCs, of major figures – as cosmically weighty. The death of a beloved ally is not just emotionally significant; it is the start of that ally's own transit, with whatever uncertainty that involves. The GM should consider letting the players think about what happens to their characters' souls, and to NPCs they care about, when those souls' transits are at issue.

**Necromancy is serious.** Beyond the legal and theological consequences, the metaphysical reality of necromancy – that it damages souls, that it produces beings whose suffering is real, that it interferes with the proper transit of the dead – is something the GM can convey through encounters. A damut that retains fragments of identity is genuinely tragic. A tereb is genuinely a person whose existence is built on continuing harm. The cosmology supports treating these as moral weights, not just mechanical challenges.

**Shamanism is sacred.** When players encounter shamanic traditions or undertake shamanic practices, the GM should distinguish them sharply from necromancy. The shaman's spirit-travel to the ancestor pool is not in the same category as a necromancer's pulling-back of a recalcitrant soul. The shaman's tradition – and the cosmology – treats these as morally and metaphysically different. The GM should respect that.

**Cross-planar elements add depth without requiring detail.** The fact that Thalorna's cosmology includes other planes, the fae, the Sinalë's non-native origin, and other cross-planar features adds richness without requiring the GM to develop those other planes in detail. A campaign can mention, hint at, gesture toward these things without having to flesh them out. The fae who appear briefly are foreigners whose home is elsewhere; that is enough. The Sinalë's origin is tradition that informs how the Sinalë are; the original plane never needs to be visited.

**Dragons are a special story material.** The Vylarian betrayal and its potential aftermath are a substantial cosmological hook for major campaigns. The GM should consider whether a particular campaign engages with this hook (in which case it deserves significant development) or merely acknowledges it (in which case dragons are background ancient history). Both modes work; mixed treatment in a single campaign is risky because the betrayal is either central to a story arc or it isn't.

**The spirit realm itself can be a setting.** Campaigns can involve travel to the spirit realm – by shamans, by mortals using thin places, by characters undergoing near-death experiences. Such episodes are dangerous (predators, lost-soul vulnerability) and require careful framing. Few mortals can survive prolonged time in the spirit realm; the GM should set the limits clearly.

**Some questions remain deliberately open.** Where new souls come from. What lies in the gods' planes specifically. The full structure of other planes. How dragons reproduce, how long dragons live, what dragons think. The cosmology supports multiple answers; the GM should choose what works for the campaign and leave the rest unsettled.
