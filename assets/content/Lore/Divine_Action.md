> **GM-only document.** This file describes how the gods of Thalorna engage with the material world—through what mechanisms, under what constraints, with what limitations—given the non-intervention principle established in [[lore-godsrelign|Gods and Religion]]. Where that document focuses on what religions are and how they function, this document focuses on the divine layer itself: the politics, agents, and structural constraints that shape what the gods can and cannot do. The document assumes familiarity with [[lore-magictruth|Magic Truth]] and [[lore-godsrelign|Gods and Religion]]; the cosmology established there is presupposed throughout.

## The Problem This Document Solves

The non-intervention principle, as stated in [[lore-godsrelign#the-non-intervention-of-the-gods|Gods and Religion]], holds that the gods of Thalorna do not directly intervene in the affairs of mortals. Their actions on the material plane take only two forms: naturalistic interventions that look like coincidence, and mediated interventions through human agents (typically priests) that look like spellcasting. The principle is presented as universal—applying to _all_ gods regardless of their disposition.

This raises a problem that the previous document acknowledged but did not fully resolve: gods differ widely in disposition. Some want worship; some are aloof; some are indifferent; some are actively hostile to particular religions or practices. Given this diversity, why does the principle hold so absolutely? One might naively expect a worship-desiring god to push harder against the limit, or a hostile god to manifest its displeasure more directly than a tolerant one. The historical record shows no such variation: even the most extreme religious claims describe events that remain naturalistically deniable.

This document develops the answer in detail, and in doing so describes the apparatus by which the gods _do_ engage with the world—through agents and proxies, with limitations on their knowledge and reach, within a Balance that the gods themselves maintain. The principle is not a single restraint imposed by metaphysics; it is a complex political and structural achievement, sustained by the gods' active participation. Understanding how it works gives the GM the tools to run divine action coherently in a campaign.

## The Compact and Its Enforcement

The non-intervention principle holds because the gods enforce it on each other.

Whatever the historical origin of the constraint—the _Compact_, as some traditions name it, possibly the result of an ancient catastrophe whose lessons the gods absorbed; possibly an arrangement reached after the gods' own consideration of what their direct action does to the worlds they inhabit; possibly something else—the principle is now sustained as an active equilibrium among the gods themselves. Any god who attempts to break the Compact is met with counter-action by other gods. The Balance is restored.

This is the answer to why the principle holds despite divine diversity: a single god willing to act directly, against the restraint of all the others, would not produce a world _visibly_ shaped by direct divine intervention, because the other gods would respond. The historical record does not contain visible apparitions, unambiguous miracles, or gods walking among mortals not because no god has ever wanted to act this way, but because the system of mutual policing has prevented such acts from leaving lasting marks. A flicker of direct intervention may have occurred—perhaps frequently—but each flicker has been damped, the Balance restored, the historical record left looking unbroken.

Several features of this enforcement are worth being explicit about, because they shape how the GM should run divine action.

**The enforcement is not always immediate.** When one god moves to break the Compact, the others may not respond instantly. They may need to detect the violation, evaluate it, decide on a response, and deploy it. Depending on the speed of divine perception and decision (questions explored later in this document), a god's transgression may have a brief window of effect before the response arrives. During that window, the transgressing god may have produced visible interventions in the material world. The response, when it comes, may itself include naturalistic effects that _undo_ or _counter_ the transgression—making the historical record appear unbroken even when, briefly, it had been.

**The enforcement is sometimes mutual escalation.** A god who breaks compact may not be quietly corrected; the others may respond with their own actions, producing a cascade. The cascade is itself bounded—the gods, collectively, want the Balance to hold—but during a cascade, the world may experience an unusual concentration of divine activity that mortals cannot easily explain. Such periods may be the historical core of _heroic ages_, in which mortals remember unusual numbers of miracles, unusual quantities of strange phenomena, unusual interactions with what their religions call "the divine."

**The enforcement is achieved through agents, not direct action.** This is the crucial point that opens the next section. Because the gods themselves are bound by the Compact, they cannot directly police violations through direct action either. The policing is itself mediated: through agents, through the deployment of demi-divine and mortal proxies, through the careful arrangement of naturalistic events. A god who is being policed by others does not see those others manifest in their own glory; the god experiences the others' counter-action through the intermediation of agents and circumstances. The Balance maintains itself through a complex web of proxy action, not through simple top-level enforcement.

**The enforcement is imperfect.** Like any political equilibrium, the Compact's stability depends on the gods' continued willingness to participate in its enforcement. A god who decides the Balance is no longer worth maintaining, or who has become powerful enough that the others cannot effectively counter it, could in principle break the equilibrium. Such a moment has not occurred in recorded history, but the system contains the structural possibility. The Compact is not a metaphysical given; it is an ongoing achievement.

**Why the gods enforce it.** The motivations are not uniform. Some gods may enforce because they remember (or have been taught) the consequences of failing to. Some may enforce because they prefer the world that the Balance produces—a world where mortal action matters, where civilizations rise and fall under their own efforts, where the divine is felt but never demonstrated. Some may enforce because they fear what would happen to them if they were the ones being policed and the system did not hold. The mixture of motivations is itself a source of stability: even if a particular god disagreed with the Compact in principle, breaking it would invite responses they would not enjoy.

The Compact-as-enforcement framing absorbs and combines several of the theoretical hypotheses listed in [[lore-godsrelign#the-non-intervention-of-the-gods|Gods and Religion]]. It is consistent with **The Compact** (the gods agreed to restrain themselves), with **Mutual constraint** (any god's intervention is matched by others'), and with parts of **Metaphysical limit** (the gods' power, however vast, must operate through agents because the system has shaped itself this way). It explains why the principle holds universally—not because the gods all happen to agree but because they actively prevent each other from breaking ranks. And it sets up the agent typology that the rest of this document develops.

## Agents of the Gods {#agents-of-the-gods}

If the gods themselves cannot act directly in the material world, but the gods nevertheless engage with the world (favorable or hostile, attentive or aloof, generative or dissolving), the engagement must be mediated. The gods act through _agents_: beings who can act in the material world directly, who serve a god's purposes, and who are not themselves bound by the Compact that binds the gods.

Agents come in three broad layers, ranging from beings of nearly-divine power to fully mortal humans operating with divine sanction. Each layer has its own characteristics, its own visibility, and its own role in the system.

### Spirit-Agents

The first and most powerful layer of agents are _spirit-agents_—beings native to the Spirit world (developed in [[lore-soulspirts|Souls and Spirits]], forthcoming) who have been bound to or aligned with specific gods. They are not gods themselves; they are beings of intermediate scale, more powerful than mortals but less than the gods they serve.

Different traditions know them by different names. The Aureldians speak of _angels_ (servants of the Beings of Light, in their tradition's adaptation of older Helionite concepts) and _devils_ (servants of beings the Aureldians consider hostile, often equated with the Sinalë's Beings of Dark). The Itzani know _mensajeros_ (messengers) and _acechadores_ (the lurking ones). Other cultures have their own taxonomies, their own names, their own assumed hierarchies of these beings. The cosmology does not validate any particular framework: what one tradition calls an angel another might call a demon, and a third might call a sacred beast or a guardian-spirit. The labels are cultural; the underlying beings are real.

The defining features of spirit-agents:

**They can manifest directly in the material world.** Unlike their patron gods, spirit-agents can take physical form, appear to mortals, deliver messages, fight, protect, destroy, or otherwise act with full visibility. They are not bound by the Compact. When the agents of two opposed gods clash, the clash can be entirely visible to mortals—armies of angels and demons fighting over a battlefield, two great spirits dueling above a city, a host of lesser servants harrying a hero across a wilderness.

**They range enormously in power.** The most powerful spirit-agents are beings of near-divine capacity—figures whose interventions can reshape regions, command armies, terrorize populations, or sustain entire civilizations. The least powerful are local guardians, minor messengers, lesser servants whose effects are modest and bounded. The power range parallels the variation in any other category: a few are great, many are middling, the largest number are small.

The most powerful spirit-agents—the equivalent in Thalorna of figures like Sauron or the Balrogs in Tolkien's cosmology—are themselves significant historical actors. Their direct involvement in mortal affairs has shaped epochs. The corresponding _modest_ spirit-agents—the equivalent of Gandalf or Glorfindel in Tolkien—operate with deliberate restraint, often under explicit instruction from their patron gods to limit their visible power and to work through teaching, counsel, and presence rather than overwhelming force. Whether a particular spirit-agent operates at full power or restrained capacity is a function of its god's instructions, the situation it is deployed into, and the response it draws from opposing agents.

**They can be opposed.** A spirit-agent of one god can be defeated, bound, killed, banished, or otherwise countered by a spirit-agent of another god. The cosmology does not protect them—they are mortal, in the sense that they can be ended, even if "ended" means something different for a non-corporeal being than for a human. A great spirit-agent's "death" may mean dispersal back to the Spirit world; a lesser one's may mean genuine destruction. The details depend on the specific being and the means of opposition.

**Their appearance can be subtle or unmistakable.** A spirit-agent need not always manifest as a glowing figure declaring its name. Many operate subtly: the stranger who arrives at the right moment with the right counsel and is gone by morning; the dream that brings exactly the warning the dreamer needed; the unusual storm that gathers around a particular ship; the cat that watches the council and is not quite a cat. The full range exists, and the GM can pick any visibility level for any agent in play. Subtle appearances allow the gods to act without provoking immediate counter-deployment by rivals; overt appearances are reserved for moments of high consequence, where the cost of visibility is justified by the gain.

### Demi-Divine Entities

The second layer of agents are _demi-divine entities_—beings of partly-divine origin or status, embedded in the material world in ways that pure spirit-agents are not. Where spirit-agents are visitors from the Spirit world, demi-divine entities are residents of the material plane, with bodies (sometimes) and lineages (sometimes) and forms of mortality (sometimes), but with capacities that exceed ordinary mortal limits.

The category includes:

**Half-divine offspring.** Children of a god and a mortal—or, more properly, children of a god's _agent_ and a mortal, since the gods themselves do not engage in such relationships. The children are genuinely half-something: half-mortal, half whatever the agent was. Their lifespans are unusual. Their capacities, particularly in domains aligned with their divine parentage, exceed the mortal range. Religious tradition often calls them heroes, demigods, or saints, depending on the culture and circumstances.

**Deified mortals.** Mortals who have been elevated to a higher state by long association with a god, by deliberate apotheosis at the hand of an agent, or by some other process. These are former mortals who have crossed the threshold into something more—not gods, not even spirit-agents, but beings of unusual power and longevity who continue to embody what they were as mortals while having transcended ordinary mortality.

**Founder-heroes whose blood persists.** Mortal heroes of unusual capacity who, by the time of their deaths, have become more than fully mortal—and whose descendants carry traces of what they became. Royal lineages claiming divine ancestry sometimes have such truth at their root. The descendants are mostly ordinary mortals, but every few generations a child is born whose capacities mark them as inheritors of more than common blood.

**Ancient beings of accumulated divine character.** Beings who, over millennia of association with a particular god—perhaps as the god's chosen servant, perhaps as the priest of a long line, perhaps as the guardian of a particular place—have accumulated something of the god's character within themselves. They are not the god, but they have become god-touched in a way that ordinary mortals are not.

Demi-divine entities operate visibly in the world, often at the center of major historical events. Heroic ages are largely the work of demi-divine entities: figures like Beowulf or Gilgamesh in real-world myth, or the foundational heroes of Thalorna's various cultural traditions. They are powerful enough to shape kingdoms, fight monsters that ordinary humans cannot fight, accomplish quests that ordinary humans cannot accomplish—but their power is not so overwhelming that their actions feel like direct divine intervention. They are still recognisably persons, with names and stories and ends.

### Mortal Agents

The third layer consists of _mortal agents_: fully mortal beings (in soul-terms) who have been chosen, directed, blessed, or claimed by a god to act on its behalf. They are not metaphysically different from other mortals—they have ordinary souls, ordinary Aura, ordinary bodies—but they have been granted the status of agency: a relationship with the god in which they act with the god's sanction and (when appropriate) the god's specific intent.

The category includes:

**Heroes.** Mortals who undertake great deeds at the prompting or with the support of a deity. The classical hero pattern: a mortal who acts in the world more decisively than any spirit-agent could, _because_ they are mortal. Heroes' actions cannot be dismissed as merely divine puppetry; they are the genuine choices and efforts of human beings (or other mortal beings), even when those choices and efforts have been shaped by divine encouragement.

**Prophets.** Mortals who carry a god's messages to other mortals, with greater clarity and authority than ordinary priests. Prophets are typically marked by direct contact with the deity's agents (or, in rare cases, with the deity's attention) and by the burden of the messages they carry. Their lives are often hard. The distinction between a true prophet and a false one (or a true prophet and a mystic experiencing internal altered states; see [[lore-godsrelign#saints-prophets-and-mystics|Gods and Religion]]) is rarely clear from outside.

**Champions.** Designated bearers of a god's favor, often in conflict or struggle. The chosen warrior, the appointed priest-king, the temple-trained avenger. Champions know they are champions; their religion frames them this way; the deity (if real) has acknowledged them through agents or visions or some other mediated form of contact. They go forth with full institutional support.

**Saints.** As described in [[lore-godsrelign#saints-prophets-and-mystics|Gods and Religion]], saints are figures of unusual concentration of divine attention or thaumaturgic capacity. The previous document developed several possible underlying mechanisms; in the agent-typology of this document, the _real_ saints (the ones whose religious reputations are not fully explicable by ordinary thaumaturgic priestly capacity) are typically mortal agents in the sense developed here. They have been chosen, marked, attended-to in ways that make them more than ordinary priests of their religion. The religion calls them saints; the cosmology calls them mortal agents.

Mortal agents are the most numerous and the most narratively load-bearing category of divine action. They are the primary vehicle through which gods affect mortal history, _because_ they can act decisively without provoking immediate counter-deployment of full divine power. A god who works through mortal heroes is operating well within the spirit of the Compact: the heroes' actions are the heroes' own, even when divinely encouraged, and the gods themselves remain unmanifest.

This is also why the rival agents most commonly opposing a god's mortal heroes are _other mortal heroes_, raised up by other gods. The escalation logic operates within layers: mortal champions are countered by mortal champions; demi-divine entities by demi-divine entities; spirit-agents by spirit-agents. Crossing layers—sending a great spirit-agent against an ordinary mortal hero—would constitute a kind of escalation that would itself draw counter-deployment. The Balance is layered.

## The Balance

The gods, through their agents at all three layers, maintain what may collectively be called the **Balance**—the dynamic equilibrium of divine engagement with the world. The Balance is not a state of zero activity; it is a state in which activity is matched, contained, and prevented from cascading into a world reshaped by overt divine action. Within the Balance, there is room for enormous variation: mortal heroes accomplishing great deeds, spirit-agents fighting in the high places, demi-divine entities founding kingdoms, naturalistic interventions by gods themselves shaping particular events. What the Balance prevents is the _unilateral_ dominance of any one god's agents—the kind of dominance that would amount, in effect, to direct divine intervention.

The Balance operates on several levels.

**Within layers.** When the agents of one god begin to dominate at a particular layer, the agents of opposing gods are deployed to that layer in response. A mortal champion of one god rising too far is opposed by a mortal champion of another. A great spirit-agent acting too freely is met by a counter-agent of comparable power.

**Across layers.** Escalation across layers is itself constrained. Sending a great spirit-agent against ordinary mortal opposition is the kind of imbalance that draws not just counter-deployment but recalibration of the entire engagement. A god who pushes too hard at a high layer finds the other gods becoming more attentive across all layers—including more naturalistic intervention against the transgressing god's interests, more agent-deployment in regions the transgressing god cares about, more general wariness in the council of divine attention.

**Geographically.** A god's agents may operate freely in regions where the god has long held influence and may be more constrained in regions where opposing gods are dominant. The local Balance can shift over time, and shifts are themselves negotiated through ongoing agent-level activity. A region in which a single god has achieved unusual dominance is a region where opposing gods will, eventually, push back.

**Across time.** Periods of unusual agent-activity tend to be self-correcting. A heroic age in which many mortal heroes and demi-divine entities walk openly will, eventually, settle into a quieter age in which agent-activity drops to lower levels. The mechanism is partly that the gods themselves prefer the quieter state (less agent-attention required, less risk of cascading escalation) and partly that the heroic age tends to _consume_ the agents involved—heroes die, demi-divine entities are slain or withdraw, spirit-agents are bound or destroyed. The natural depletion of agents is itself a re-balancing force.

**The current age in Thalorna.** As of the campaign's present, Thalorna is in a relatively quiet phase. The great heroic ages of the past are remembered in religious tradition and historical record, but the present-day frequency of overt agent-activity is low compared to those earlier periods. Spirit-agents do appear, occasionally; demi-divine entities do walk the world, rarely; mortal champions do arise, in moments of unusual need. But the _typical_ day of the _typical_ mortal involves no encounter with any of these. The gods are present but mostly through their religions and pastoral effects, not through their agents.

This is by design. The Balance favors the quieter state, and the gods collectively work to keep it. A campaign that begins in a quiet age and pushes toward a more active one is, narratively, a campaign about the destabilization of the Balance—and the destabilization is itself a serious matter that the gods, through their agents, will address.

## Non-Omniscience: The Gods Are Not Omnipotent

A point of crucial importance for running divine action: **the gods are not omniscient.** They are vast—supra-beings of immense Aura and reasoning, capable of channeling potential at scales no mortal can match—but their knowledge is bounded.

Specifically, a god knows:

**What it directly experiences.** The god's own consciousness, attention, and contemplation. What it has done, what it has decided, what it has perceived through its own senses (whatever the divine equivalent of senses is).

**What its adherents know.** For gods that care to attend to their adherents, there is some channel of attention or sympathy that lets the god draw on the experiences and knowledge of those who worship it. The mechanism is not omniscient mind-reading; it is the god's ability to follow the attention of its adherents, learn what they have learned, see what they have seen. A god whose adherents are watching a battle can know what the battle looks like to them. A god whose adherents have traveled to a foreign land can know what those adherents have observed there.

**What its agents report.** Spirit-agents, demi-divine entities, and mortal agents return to (or remain in contact with) their patron gods, and through that contact, the gods learn what the agents have done, seen, and discovered. This is the primary intelligence-gathering channel for divine politics. A god deploying a spirit-agent into rival territory is partly trying to act in that territory and partly trying to _learn_ what is there.

**What other gods choose to communicate.** The gods communicate with each other—somehow, by means mortals cannot describe—and through that communication exchange information they consider worth sharing. The communication is selective. Gods do not tell each other everything; they tell each other what serves their purposes.

A god does _not_ know:

**What its non-adherents experience.** The minds and lives of those who do not worship the god are opaque to it. A god of one tradition cannot read the mind of a foreign priest, an unbeliever, an atheist, or an adherent of an opposing tradition. The opacity is not absolute—agents may report on these people, adherents may observe them—but the direct line of attention does not extend to them.

**What it has not been told or shown.** A god whose adherents do not attend to a region cannot know what is happening there. A god whose agents have not penetrated some location cannot know its details. A god whose rivals have successfully concealed their preparations does not know about them. Information requires acquisition, and acquisition is mediated.

**The future, except by projection.** The gods may have unusual capacity to project from present knowledge—to read patterns, to perceive likely outcomes, to model the consequences of their own and others' actions. They cannot _see_ the future. Prophecies that come true do so because they were good projections, or because the prophecy itself shaped the outcome (self-fulfilling), or because a god's agent acted to bring them about. Prophecies that fail to come true are equally common and equally meaningful; the cosmology does not require divine prophecy to be reliable.

**Some implications worth tracking:**

**A god can be deceived.** If the god's adherents are deceived—if they have been misled about something, or if their reports are systematically wrong—the god shares their mistake. A widespread deception within a religion produces a god who shares the deception, until and unless something corrects it.

**A god can be deliberately blinded.** Cutting off a religion from a region—destroying its shrines, killing its priests, scattering its adherents—actually reduces the corresponding god's knowledge of that region. A rival god seeking to operate in some territory may benefit from suppressing the local religion of an opposing god. The suppression is not just a matter of weakening the rival's influence; it is a matter of blinding the rival to what is being done in that territory.

**A god can be surprised.** Even by mortals, even by other gods. A gambit that succeeds because the opposing god did not see it coming is genuinely possible. The divine politics is not a game of perfect information.

**Gods bluff and dissemble with each other.** Because the gods do not have perfect information about each other, deception between gods is possible. A god may lead its rivals to believe its strength lies in one place while it is actually building up agents elsewhere; a god may misdirect attention; a god may pretend to be more or less capable than it is. The political layer of divine action is genuinely political, with intelligence asymmetries, deceptions, alliances, and betrayals.

**The Quaesitorium has divine analogues.** Just as the Ordo's enforcement arm hunts unsanctioned mortal practitioners, the agents of various gods may hunt the agents of opposing gods, trying to identify them, disrupt their plans, kill them, capture them. These hunts can be visible or covert; they can be conducted at any of the three agent layers. A campaign about a band of heroes opposing a hostile god's interests is, partly, a campaign about being the hunted in such a hunt, and possibly the hunters in another.

**The Sinalë's information advantage.** The Sinalë's exceptional longevity gives them—collectively—an unusual capacity to accumulate information about divine action over time. A Sinalë sage who has lived for two thousand years has watched many cycles of agent-activity, observed many heroic ages and quiet ages, witnessed many deceptions revealed. Their tradition's wisdom about how the gods actually engage with the world is genuinely better-informed than any human tradition's, _because_ they have had time to gather and analyze the relevant evidence. This is part of why a Sinalë sage is the most useful in-world voice for articulating the actual cosmology.

## The Heroic Age and the Quiet Age

The cosmology produces characteristic patterns of intensity and quiescence in agent-level activity. These patterns are useful to the GM as story material.

A **Heroic Age** is a period in which agent-level activity is unusually intense. Many mortal heroes walk the world, accomplishing deeds beyond ordinary capacity. Demi-divine entities are encountered with unusual frequency. Spirit-agents appear openly, sometimes in conflict with each other, sometimes guiding particular mortals through quests of cosmic consequence. The world feels alive with divine attention. Religions are correspondingly active: prophecies are made and tested, miracles are reported with unusual frequency, the boundaries between the material and the Spirit world feel thinner.

Heroic Ages typically arise from imbalances in the divine equilibrium. Some triggering event—a god making an unusually aggressive deployment of agents, the rise of a new and dangerous power that the existing Balance must adapt to absorb, the emergence of a movement (mortal or otherwise) that draws unusual divine attention—causes the gods collectively to engage more actively with the world. Once engaged, the engagement tends to escalate as opposing gods deploy counter-agents, until the situation reaches a dramatic crisis: a great war of agents, a quest of cosmic significance, a confrontation that resolves the imbalance.

Heroic Ages tend to be self-limiting. The intense agent activity consumes the agents involved: heroes die, demi-divine entities are slain or transformed, spirit-agents are bound or banished. The cosmological cost of the engagement accumulates until the gods, collectively, withdraw to allow the system to settle. The age ends, and a Quiet Age begins.

A **Quiet Age** is the cosmology's resting state. Agent-level activity drops to a baseline of routine engagement—pastoral religion functions, occasional thaumaturgic priestly miracles occur, naturalistic divine interventions happen rarely—but overt agent encounters are uncommon. Most mortals live their lives without ever seeing a spirit-agent or a demi-divine entity. Heroes still arise, occasionally, but they are figures of regional rather than cosmic significance. The world feels quiet, in divine terms. The gods are present, but at a remove.

Quiet Ages last longer than Heroic Ages, in general. They are the equilibrium; Heroic Ages are perturbations of it. A given Quiet Age may last centuries or millennia; a Heroic Age may last decades to a few centuries.

**The current age of Thalorna is a Quiet Age**, deep enough that most mortals have no direct experience of agent-level divine action and the very concept feels mythological. The most recent Heroic Age in Thalorna's recorded history is—the GM should decide. It may be the founding period of one of the major civilizations, or the period of the Vylarian-Helionite wars (which involved dragons; see [[lore-soulspirts|Souls and Spirits]] forthcoming), or some earlier age of which only fragmentary records remain. The GM's decision about which periods of past history were Heroic and which were Quiet shapes the texture of the world's deep past considerably.

**Campaigns can be set in either type of age**, and the texture of play differs accordingly.

A **Quiet Age campaign** is a campaign in which divine action is felt mostly through religion's pastoral and thaumaturgic effects, occasional naturalistic interventions, and rare encounters with spirit-agents or demi-divine entities. The cosmology is mostly background. The players' characters operate primarily through their own efforts, with occasional help or hindrance from the divine layer. This is the default mode for most fantasy campaigns and matches the present-day texture of Thalorna.

A **Heroic Age campaign** is a campaign in which divine action is more prominent and the players' characters may themselves be agents—chosen heroes, prophets, champions of particular gods—whose actions matter at the cosmic level. The cosmology is foreground. The players' characters routinely encounter spirit-agents (allied and opposed), interact with demi-divine entities, and operate within the larger pattern of agent-level engagement. This mode is more demanding for the GM but produces a particular kind of mythic-flavor campaign.

A **transitional campaign**—set during the destabilization of a Quiet Age into a Heroic Age, or during the consolidation of a Heroic Age back into a Quiet one—has the texture of cosmic stakes emerging in a previously stable world, or of cosmic stakes resolving into peace. These are particularly rich narrative spaces, because the players' characters may witness the change directly: the first signs that something is shifting, the rising tide of agent-activity, the dramatic peak, the long settling. Many of the great fantasy stories in real-world literature are transitional in this sense.

## What This All Means for Running Campaigns

The cosmology established in this document has several practical implications.

**Mortal heroes are how gods act.** When the GM wants a god to be active in a campaign, the natural mechanism is through mortal agents—heroes, prophets, champions—whose actions the players' characters can encounter, oppose, or join. The god itself remains unmanifest. The agents do the visible work.

**Spirit-agents are reserved for moments of weight.** A spirit-agent's appearance is a serious event. The GM should not deploy them casually. When a spirit-agent does appear, the encounter should feel weighty, consequential, and rare. Routine encounters with angels and demons cheapen the cosmology and erase the principle of divine restraint. The economy of spirit-agent appearances is itself part of how the world communicates its theology.

**Demi-divine entities are historical figures more often than active ones.** Most demi-divine entities in Thalorna's world are figures of past ages, encountered through stories, ruins, surviving artifacts, and inherited consequences. A campaign that involves an active demi-divine entity is unusual; the entity itself is unusual; the situation calls for unusual responses.

**Cross-tradition divine politics is real.** The agents of different gods are aware of each other, sometimes at war with each other, sometimes in alliance, sometimes in negotiation. A campaign involving multiple religions can include a layer of divine politics in which the players' characters affect the engagements between agents of different gods. This layer is distinct from the human politics of the religions themselves; both can be active simultaneously.

**Information is power.** Because the gods are not omniscient, knowledge has cosmic stakes. A player's character who acquires information that a god's enemies do not want the god to have is a player's character whose life is now interesting in ways that may exceed their control. Conversely, a player's character who can prevent a god from learning something—by killing the agent who would have reported it, by misleading an adherent, by destroying the relevant shrine—has done something that registers at the divine layer.

**Prophecies are projections, not omniscient seeing.** When a campaign involves prophecy, the GM should remember that the prophecy is the god's (or its agent's) projection from current knowledge, not a glimpse of the future. The prophecy can be wrong. The prophecy can be wrong because the prophecy itself changed the future, or because the projection was based on bad information, or because the gods' political situation shifted, or simply because the gods do not have perfect models. A campaign in which a prophesied event fails to come true is consistent with the cosmology—the failure can be a campaign event in itself, raising the question of why the prophecy missed.

**The Balance is itself a story tool.** When the players' characters' actions threaten to destabilize the Balance—when their successes or failures reach a scale that draws cosmic attention—they have entered the transitional space between Quiet and Heroic Ages. What happens next depends on how the gods respond to the destabilization. The campaign's cosmic stakes can be exactly the question of how the destabilization will resolve.

**Dragons are a special case.** The historical destabilization that the Republic exploited in its war against the Helionite mages—the dragons' presence breaking framework-magic—was itself an instance of agent-level activity at the highest scale. Dragons are developed in [[lore-soulspirts|Souls and Spirits]], but they should be understood as falling somewhere in the spirit-agent / demi-divine entity range, with capacities that put them among the most powerful non-divine beings in the cosmology. Their relationship to gods, their place in the Balance, and their role in any future Heroic Age that might arise are all questions the GM should consider carefully when developing campaign material.
