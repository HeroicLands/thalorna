# terran_analog: Haradi reads an Akkadian-and-Phoenician register of a Semitic maritime trade tongue. Most names in use are real Akkadian and Hebrew words or names (Ishtarûna, Nadûmar, the endings -ûm and -ash); the roots coined here are made to the rules and are not the Akkadian, Phoenician, Arabic or Hebrew word for their meaning.
---

A harbor notary at [[affiliation-kethara|Kethara]] greets every new clerk with the same exercise: she reads a contract aloud and asks which word in it is the price. None of them is. In Haradi the price is a _pashînû_, a fair price, and that is only what two people agree to leave the table with; the contract rests on _mardû_, the credit of the names that sign it, and on the _qatûsh_, the holding-fast that each of them swears to. Everything a newcomer needs to know about the language of the [[affiliation-cnfdrtnhrdnstts|Confederation of Haradian States]] is in that exercise: [[skill-haradilng|Haradi]] is a tongue of bargains, and its words are built to say who holds what.

A Haradi root names an act, and an ending says who stands in it. _Telm-_ is to bind in writing; _telmum_ is the notary who writes the bond, and _telmûsh_ is the contract itself. The same five endings are laid on every root, which is why a clerk who has met a word once can read its relatives, and why a stranger who learns the endings reads half a harbor sign on the first day. This page is the word-hoard of those roots: the stems the quay uses, what each ending makes of them, the rules a new word is built by, and the register of every name the setting gives in the Haradian lands.

The sound of Haradi is rolling and full-voiced. Vowels come short or long, the long ones written with a circumflex, and a consonant doubled inside a word is held and pressed: _Tarrûk_, _Namrûsh_, _Kadûmû_. Stress falls on the second-last syllable, and a word never opens on two consonants.

## How to read the tables

Each word table has these columns:

- **Form** is the word as it is cited: a noun in its plain form, a verb in its _-am_ or _-ûsh_ form. A form that closes on a hyphen is a bound stem, which never stands alone.
- **Class** says what kind of word it is, from the list below.
- **Gloss** is its meaning in brief.
- **Built from** is `—` for a root word. Otherwise it names the stem and ending the form is made of, joined by `+`, and the form is exactly what the suffix table makes of them.
- **Attested** names the page that uses the word, or `—`.

## Building words

Two moves make every built word on this page. **An ending on a stem:** the stem is bound, and each ending says what part the stem's act plays. **A name-ending on a root:** a person's name is a root with an ending the tongue keeps for names, and the family name is a root with a lineage ending; the endings stand under Making names.

## Words

### The bargain and the word

A Haradian price is the end of a conversation, and the words show it. The seller opens with a _bashîmûsh_ that nobody expects to stand; the buyer answers with a _nibûrûsh_, a refusal that opens the real talk; and a price is settled only when both sides leave the table with a _pashînû_, a fair one. What holds the result is a name: _mardû_, the credit of the person who gave his word. A hired notary writes the _telmûsh_, but the bond a Haradi trusts is the _qatûsh_, the thing a person holds fast to, and a guarantor is a _rumûkum_ who stands for someone else.

### Reckoning, credit and debt

A Haradi cannot speak of money without saying who stands behind it. A debt is a _fusânûsh_ and the debtor a _fusânum_, but the third person in every loan is the _rumûkum_, the surety, because a Haradian house lends against a name more readily than against a pledge. The words for profit and for justice come from the same stem family as the tide: _dammûsh_ is the sea giving and taking back, and _kûrrîm_ is the merchant's word for justice, the return of what is owed. A cheat is not a villain here but a person whose accounts have not come round.

### Hull, tide and knot

Haradi has a noun for every part of a ship that a captain must pay for, and a verb for every motion of the water that a captain must survive. The tide is a _visîzûsh_ and a ship that works with it is _visîzan_; a harbor, _besîkû_, is named for what it does to the swell. A captain who owns his hull is a _jakûdum_, the characteristic figure of the coast, and the word has no more to say of his debts than that he is a person with a hull. The sea itself is _manî_, one word and no endings, because it is the one thing that does not belong to anyone.

### The house, the firm and the family

A Haradian does not separate the family from the firm, and the vocabulary never did. _Tarûzî_ means the houses of a line and the firms they keep, with no word to tell the two apart; _tarûzûnî_ is the whole company, kin and clerks and hired captains together. A marriage is a _teshûdûsh_, a joining of two houses that is also a merger, and an heir is a _qizîkum_ in the same breath as he is an apprentice and a hostage to the alliance. _Ilammû_, the honor of a house, is spoken of like a balance in the strongroom: something that can be spent, and that a careful house does not spend.

### Law, oath and the Confederation

Haradi law is the custom of the quay set down in writing, and the vocabulary for it comes from the quay. A _dapûmûsh_ is an oath sworn before witnesses who have touched the clasped hands; a _khurûtum_ is a judge, which in most harbors means a retired captain who has settled enough disputes to be believed; a _sazûmûsh_ is a toll, and the person who keeps the gate is the nearest thing the coast has to a tax collector. _Zabûzû_, freedom, is the word the veterans of the war of independence use, and the root means to go unbound, which a Haradian understands as having no debts to the wrong creditor.

### Doing and being

Almost every Haradi verb has two faces. The root with _-am_ is the plain act, done as a matter of course: _qubâsam_, he counts. The same root with _-ûsh_ is the act as it settles, or the thing it leaves behind: _yutâlûsh_, a payment. An adjective is the root with _-an_, so a bargain is judged _latâran_ when it is honest, _lartan_ when it is dear and _temâdan_ when the goods are sound; those three words do most of a trader's judging.

## Making names

**A Haradi person** carries a given name and a family name, the given name first: [[being-esharumtrz|Eshârum Tarûzî]] is Eshârum of the houses of Tarûz. A given name is a root with a personal ending. A man's closes on _-um_, _-ûsh_, _-an_ or _-û_, and the ending carries what the parents hope of him: _Namrûsh_ holds a matter settled, _Arûzan_ is full of his root. A woman's closes on _-a_ or _-ûya_, which run softer and carry fewer doubled consonants: _Kasûra_, _Kûrûya_. A family name is a root with _-î_, _-ûnî_ or _-ûm_, and says whose firm a person belongs to; the root is nearly always the name of a founder, so _Tarûzî_ and _Damrûnî_ name houses that outlast any man in them. The name is also a credit line: a person's _mardû_ is the worth of the whole name said aloud, and a Haradian who changes either part of it has changed what a creditor can read.

Among friends a given name is cut to its stressed syllable with the vowel held long: _Eshârum_ is _Shâ_. A short name is used of a person already named in full, never in a contract.

**A place** is a root with _-un_, _-ar_, _-el_ or _-an_: _Sulûn_, _Tamavar_, _Ashkabel_, _Varoshan_. A harbor town often keeps a coast name that no root explains, and the League writes it as the harbor-master spells it.

**A house or a body** is named for its founder or its work, with the lineage ending: the Auric Compact is a banking cartel named in the reader's tongue for the gold it holds.
