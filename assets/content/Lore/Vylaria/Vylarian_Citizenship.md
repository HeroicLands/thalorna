The deepest division in Vylarian society is not between rich and poor but between **citizen** and **resident**. The great mass of the people under imperial rule are _residents_—subjects of the Empire, born to it, taxed by it, protected (after a fashion) by it, but holding no share in it. **Citizens** are a privileged minority who have _earned_ a stake, and the gap between the two is one of the central facts of life in [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Vylaria]].

## Citizen and Resident

Citizenship is a **personal, earned, and largely non-heritable** status. A man is not a citizen because his father was; with a single exception he must win it himself, and he cannot pass it down. The exception is the **nobility**, who hold citizenship by station and transmit it heir to heir—which is part of what _makes_ them the nobility. For everyone else, the citizen body renews itself each generation out of those who have served.

## Earning the Citizenship

There is, in practice, one road to citizenship for the common-born: **the legions.**

The army recognizes no man as a true soldier before his **fifteenth birthday**—boys may follow the camps and even fight, but they hold no rank and their service does not count until then—and a full term is **twenty years**. A commoner who takes the oath at fifteen and lives to discharge leaves the legions a **citizen of the Empire at thirty-five**, which in this world is already a middle-aged man. This is the deal on which the whole imperial military rests: the meritocratic commoner track of the [[affiliation-vylrnmltry|legions]] is, among other things, a citizenship-making engine.

The status may also be **granted** for some signal service to the Empire—a great civic benefaction, a decisive turn of intelligence, an act of loyalty the throne wishes to reward—but such grants are rare and conspicuous, and the overwhelming majority of new citizens are simply **veterans**. The citizen body, taken as a whole, is at its core an old soldiers' class.

## The Rights of a Citizen

To be a citizen is to hold a bundle of privileges no resident enjoys:

- **The dole**—a guaranteed ration of grain (and, in [[place-magnapolis|Magnápolis]], oil and the price of the public games besides). It is a subsistence _floor_, not a living; see below.
- **The right of residence in Magnápolis**—only citizens may dwell within the capital's pomerium by right. Non-citizens require a registered, employer-tied waiver from the [[affiliation-curiaurbis|courts]] (see the [[place-magnapolis|Magnápolis]] note).
- **Legal standing**—a citizen stands before the courts as a person of standing, with the **right of appeal** rising ultimately to the imperial tribunals, and is spared the summary and degrading punishments dealt to residents: a condemned citizen is owed trial and, at worst, a clean execution, not the lash or the arena without sentence.
- **Eligibility**—for the franchise and standing in civic life, for civic office, and for the salaried imperial posts (the [[affiliation-vylrnmltry|City Watch]] above all) that the Empire reserves, by preference, for the men who served it.

## The Dole, and What Comes After

The dole keeps a citizen from starving; it does not keep him in any comfort, and few who can do better choose to live on it alone. A discharged veteran of five-and-thirty, still hale and freshly trained, generally wants work—and the Empire, which has just spent twenty years making him, is glad to find him some. The **City Watch** recruits its ranks straight from the veteran-citizens; others take posts as guards, overseers, bailiffs, drovers, household stewards, and drill-instructors, and the shrewder or luckier buy into a trade or a shop. Beneath all of them the dole remains as the one thing that cannot be taken away.

## Why the Empire Bears the Cost

The dole and its privileges are expensive, and the Empire is emphatically **not a charity**. It carries the cost because the arrangement does three things no cheaper system could:

1. **It recruits the army.** The promise of citizenship—the dole, the standing, the residence, the protection of the law—is what persuades commoners to give the legions their best twenty years and to die for the borders. Take it away and the meritocratic backbone of the army, the part that actually wins wars, stops enlisting and stops trusting the throne.
2. **It leashes the dangerous.** A discharged legionary is trained, networked, and lethal, and the Empire has tens of thousands of them. Settling them as citizens with a daily, tangible stake in imperial stability turns the Empire's most dangerous class into its loyal core. A hungry peasant is a nuisance; a hungry veteran is how dynasties fall.
3. **It is a counterweight.** A throne-loyal citizen-veteran body, concentrated in the capital, balances both the senatorial aristocracy above and the unenfranchised resident multitude below.

## The Citizenship in Decline

As the Empire's reach and revenue contract, the citizenship has become a burden the throne can neither comfortably afford nor safely cut. The dole is among the largest standing charges on a shrinking treasury, and the temptations of economy show first here: **arrears, short measure, and cheaper grain in the ration**. Each is a quiet admission of decline—and each is dangerous, for the citizen-veterans know exactly what they are owed and exactly how to make their displeasure felt. Reform factions argue for trimming the rolls or means-testing the dole; the conservatives, the veterans, and plain political prudence defend it; and a _missed_ dole is the single spark every cautious Augustar most dreads.
