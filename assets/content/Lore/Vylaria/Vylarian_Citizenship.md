A centurion puts the bargain to each year's recruits on the morning they swear the oath, in words something like these: "Look at the old soldiers by the gate, lads. Every one of them stood where you stand, fifteen years old in a borrowed tunic, and every one is a citizen of the Empire today because he lived through twenty years of this. His father was not a citizen. His sons will not be, unless they walk the same road. There is one door, and the army holds it open."

The speech holds the whole system: the age, the term, the prize and the fact that it cannot be handed down. The rest of this note fills it in.

## Citizen and Resident

The deepest division in Vylarian society is not between rich and poor but between **citizen** and **resident**. The great mass of the people under imperial rule are residents: subjects of the [[affiliation-vylarinmpr|Empire]], born to it, taxed by it and protected by it after a fashion, who hold no share in it. Citizens are the privileged minority who have _earned_ a stake, and the gap between the two is one of the central facts of life in Vylaria.

Citizenship is a personal, earned and largely non-heritable status. A man is not a citizen because his father was, and with one exception he must win it himself and cannot pass it down. The exception is the **nobility**, who hold citizenship by station and transmit it heir to heir, which is part of what makes them the nobility. For everyone else the citizen body renews itself each generation out of those who have served.

## Earning the Citizenship

For the common-born there is, in practice, one road: **the legions**.

The army counts no one as a soldier before his **fifteenth birthday**. Boys may follow the camps and even fight, but they hold no rank and their service does not start the clock until then. A full term is **twenty years**, so a commoner who takes the oath at fifteen and lives to be discharged leaves the legions a **citizen of the Empire at thirty-five**, which in Vylaria is already a middle-aged man. The whole of the imperial military rests on this deal, and the commoner track of the [[affiliation-vylrnmltry|legions]] is, among other things, a machine for making citizens.

The status may also be **granted** for some signal service to the Empire: a great civic benefaction, a decisive piece of intelligence, an act of loyalty the throne wishes to reward. Such grants are rare and conspicuous. Nearly every new citizen is a veteran, and the citizen body as a whole is at its core an old soldiers' class.

## The Rights of a Citizen

A citizen holds a bundle of privileges that no resident enjoys:

- **The dole.** A guaranteed ration of grain and, in [[place-magnapolis|Magnápolis]], oil and the price of the public games besides. It is a subsistence _floor_, not a living (see below).
- **The right of residence in Magnápolis.** Only citizens may dwell within the capital's pomerium by right. A resident needs a registered waiver, tied to an employer, from the [[affiliation-curiaurbis|courts]]; the Magnápolis note describes how the waivers work.
- **Legal standing.** A citizen stands before the courts as a person of standing, with a **right of appeal** that rises to the imperial tribunals. He is spared the summary and degrading punishments dealt to residents: a condemned citizen is owed a trial and, at worst, a clean execution, never the lash or the arena without sentence.
- **Eligibility.** Citizens may vote and hold civic office, and they have first claim on the salaried imperial posts the Empire reserves for the men who served it, the [[affiliation-vylrnmltry|City Watch]] above all.

## The Dole, and What Comes After

The dole keeps a citizen from starving. It does not keep him in comfort, and few who can do better live on it alone. A discharged veteran of thirty-five, hale and freshly trained, generally wants work, and the Empire, which has just spent twenty years making him, is glad to find him some. The City Watch recruits its ranks straight from the veteran-citizens. Others take posts as guards, overseers, bailiffs, drovers, household stewards and drill-instructors, and the shrewder or luckier buy into a trade or a shop. Beneath all of them the dole remains, the one thing that cannot be taken away.

## Why the Empire Bears the Cost

The dole and its privileges are expensive, and the Empire is emphatically not running a charity. It carries the cost because the arrangement does three things no cheaper system could:

1. **It recruits the army.** The promise of citizenship (the dole, the standing, the residence, the protection of the law) is what persuades commoners to give the legions their best twenty years and to die on the borders. Take it away and the meritocratic backbone of the army, the part that wins wars, stops enlisting and stops trusting the throne.
2. **It leashes the dangerous.** A discharged legionary is trained, networked and lethal, and the Empire has tens of thousands of them. Settling them as citizens with a daily, tangible stake in imperial stability turns its most dangerous class into its loyal core. A hungry peasant is a nuisance; a hungry veteran is how dynasties fall.
3. **It balances the city.** A throne-loyal body of citizen-veterans, concentrated in the capital, offsets both the senatorial aristocracy above it and the unenfranchised resident multitude below.

## The Citizenship in Decline

As the Empire's reach and revenue shrink, the citizenship has become a burden the throne can neither comfortably afford nor safely cut. The dole is among the largest standing charges on a shrinking treasury, and the temptations of economy show first there: **arrears, short measure and cheaper grain in the ration**. Each is a quiet admission of decline, and each is dangerous, because the citizen-veterans know exactly what they are owed and exactly how to make their displeasure felt. Reform factions argue for trimming the rolls or means-testing the dole. The conservatives, the veterans and plain political prudence defend it. A _missed_ dole is the single spark every cautious Augustar (emperor) most dreads.
