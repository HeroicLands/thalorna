# terran_analog: Vedyari reads Indian. Most words harvested from the names in use are Sanskrit words with their Sanskrit senses (sūrya, chandra, giri, pur, grāma, rāja, deva, dharma, sabhā, janapada, vrata, ācārya), and the joining rules are Sanskrit vowel sandhi; the corpus relies on that resemblance for common nouns, titles and offices. It keeps clear of it for the four stations (Karmāja, Dhanāja, Senāja, Ritūja stand where the Sanskrit varṇa names would), for the word for station (tharana, not varṇa), and for the gods, whose names are coined. The coined roots in this lexicon (every row attested `—`) are made to the rules and are not the Sanskrit, Hindi or Tamil word for their meaning; tri and the harvested roots are the exceptions the corpus already carries.
---

A temple scribe in [[place-rajapur|Rājapur]] who teaches a newcomer to read the names on a map begins with the doors. _Dvāra_ is a door, and the passes through the [[place-sthrnwall|Southern Wall]] are doors: [[place-suryadvara|Sūryadvāra]] the sun's door, [[place-meghadvara|Meghadvāra]] the storm's, [[place-tamradvara|Tāmradvāra]] the copper road's. Learn twenty such words and the map of [[place-vedyarargn|Vedyara]] reads itself, because almost every Vedyari name is two or three ordinary words set side by side.

This is the word-hoard of the [[skill-vedyarlng|Vedyari]] tongue: the words a player or a GM reaches for when a Vedyari names a child, a village, an office or a god, the rules those words obey, and the way two of them join into a third. A word made from these by those rules is a Vedyari word. The last section is a register of every Vedyari name the setting uses, with the note where each is met and the words it is made from.

Every word here is written as names are written across the setting: length with the macron, the retroflex and palatal letters without the scholars' dots. The language page gives the scholars' spelling beside it.

## How to read the tables

Each word table has five columns:

- **Form** is the word as it is cited: a noun in its plain form, a verb as its bare stem with a hyphen after it, since a stem never stands alone.
- **Class** says what kind of word it is, from the list below.
- **Gloss** is its meaning in brief.
- **Built from** is `—` for a root word, made from nothing smaller. Otherwise it names the words and suffixes the form is made of, joined by `+`, and the form is exactly what the rules below make of them.
- **Attested** names a page that uses the word, or `—` for a word no page uses yet.

### Word edges

A word opens on a vowel, on a single consonant or on one of the pairs below, and closes on a vowel or on one of the consonants below. A verb stem is bound and never closes a word, so it may end on any consonant.

| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

## Building words

Two moves make every built word in this lexicon, and a new word made the same way is as good a Vedyari word as any here.

**A compound.** Two words set side by side make one, and the first describes the second: _sūrya_ "the sun" and _dvāra_ "a door" give _sūryadvāra_, the sun's door. Most names of places and offices are compounds, and many have three or four members: _megha_, _mukha_ and _kāla_ give _meghamukhakāla_, the month of the mouth of the clouds. A hyphen may stand at a seam, and where it does the two words keep their own shapes: _Sūrya-samudra_.

**A suffix.** A word or a verb stem takes one of the suffixes below: _gan-_ "count" takes _-aka_ to give _ganaka_, a reckoner, and _vrata_ "a vow" takes _-in_ to give _vratin_, one under a vow.

### Joining

Where the first word of a compound closes on a vowel and the second opens on one, the two vowels meet as the table says. Every other seam writes both words whole.

| First closes on | Second opens on              | Written | Example                                          |
| --------------- | ---------------------------- | ------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| `a`, `ā`        | `a`, `ā`                     | `ā`     | _padma_ + _āvali_ = _padmāvali_                  |
| `a`, `ā`        | `i`, `ī`                     | `e`     | _vyāla_ + _indra_ = _vyālendra_                  |
| `a`, `ā`        | `u`, `ū`                     | `o`     | —                                                |
| `i`, `ī`        | `a`, `ā`, `u`, `ū`, `e`, `o` | `y+`    | _tri_ + _āchārya_ = _triyāchārya_, a _y_ between |
| `u`, `ū`        | `a`, `ā`, `i`, `ī`, `e`, `o` | `v+`    | —                                                |

| Suffix | Stem                       | Makes                                          | Example                      |

## Making names

**A person** carries a given name and the name of a clan, the given name first: [[being-nrvsrytjmhnnd|Nárava Sūryatejamahānanda]] is Nárava of the clan of the sun's brilliance and great joy. A given name is one word or a compound of two or three, wishing something on the child: a virtue, a god's favor, a quality of the sun or the moon. _Chandrakīrtisundarī_ is "moon-fame, the beautiful."

### Given names

| Ending           | Given to                        |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------- |
| `-ī`             | a woman's name only             |
| `-ika`, `-ikā`   | a woman's name only             |
| `-ā`             | a woman's name only             |
| `-a`, `-i`       | the names of both men and women |
| `-n`, `-m`, `-r` | the names of both, rarely       |

A woman's name is often a man's word with _-ī_ in place of its closing _a_: _sundara_ "beautiful" gives _sundarī_.

**A clan** is named by a longer compound, three and four members being common, which keeps the founder's name or the virtue the lineage claims: _Dhanurvedakīrtirāja_ is bow-lore, fame and king, and _Vidyāsāgaramandala_ is the circle of the ocean of learning. A clan name does not change with the gender of the person who carries it.

A person from a place takes _-i_: a [[place-rajapur|Rājapur]] man is a Rājapuri, a woman of [[affiliation-vyalendra2|Vyālendra]] a Vyālendri.

**An office** is a compound whose last member says what the holder does: _pāla_ keeps (_kshetrapāla_, a keeper of fields), _pati_ presides (_sabhāpati_, the presiding officer of a sabhā), _nāyaka_ leads, _adhyaksha_ oversees, _āchārya_ teaches. A title from a verb takes _-aka_, as _darshaka_, a seer, or _-in_, as _vratin_, one under a vow. The great titles are compounds with _mahā_: _mahārāja_, _mahāprasāda_.

**A god** of the [[affiliation-varakpnthn|Varnaka]] is named by a compound that says what the god keeps or does: [[affiliation-kalavrata|Kālavrata]] is the vow of death, [[affiliation-meghanatha|Meghanātha]] the lord of the clouds, [[affiliation-vyalendra|Vyālendra]] the chief of beasts. A god's adjective takes _-an_, and a god's day takes _vāra_.

**A work** of literature or law is named in the reader's tongue by what it does, as [[lore-rcitkngsmhnd|The Recitation of the Kings of Mahānadi]] is.

### Calling names

A woman entered on the temple roll as _Padmāvali_ answers to _Padmi_ in her mother's kitchen. The long name is the formal one: it stands in records, in the temple and before a court, and it is the name a stranger uses. The short one is the _calling name_, used by kin and friends, and it is offered, never taken; the [[lore-vedyariclt|Vedyari]] culture note says what follows from that. Every calling name is cut from the given name by one rule, and it is never made from the clan name.

**Counting syllables.** A syllable is counted by its vowel. Each short vowel, long vowel and diphthong is one syllable, and so is an `r` standing between two consonants, so _Prthīmāja_ has four: _pr·thī·mā·ja_. A single consonant between two vowels opens the second syllable; where several stand together, the first closes the syllable before and the rest open the next, as in _chan·dra·kīr·ti·sun·da·rī_. A seam adds nothing of its own: where the joining has merged two vowels into one, as _padma_ and _āvali_ merge in _padmāvali_, the merged vowel is a single syllable, _pad·mā·va·li_.

**Cutting the name.** Keep the first one or two syllables of the given name whole, carry over the consonants that open the next syllable, and close on `-u`, `-i` or `-a` where that syllable's vowel stood. Put another way, the given name is cut just before its second or third vowel and a closing is added: _Rāmachandra_ is cut to _Rām-_ and gives _Rāmu_, and _Padmāvali_ is cut to _Padm-_ and gives _Padmi_. The letters before the cut are the given name's own, long vowels long; where a name is written with a stress mark the pages do not use, the vowel beneath it may be read short or long. The other way to make a calling name is to double the first syllable, writing its opening consonants and its vowel twice: _Suvaratika_ gives _Susu_. A name that opens on a vowel has no doubled form, since two vowels never stand together.

A calling name always closes on a vowel, and everything before the closing comes from the given name, so it keeps the word rules without effort: _Drkshu_, cut from _Drkshārana_, opens on _d_ and its vowel _r_ exactly as the given name does.

| Closing                    | Usual for |
| -------------------------- | --------- |
| `-u`                       | a man     |
| `-i`                       | a woman   |
| `-a`                       | either    |
| the first syllable doubled | either    |

A calling name in _-u_ is a man's and one in _-i_ a woman's, so _Rāmu_ names a man and _Padmi_ a woman. The given names themselves are not divided this way: a short _-i_ closes a given name of either.

Every Vedyari has a calling name, and the pages record it wherever the given name is long. A person whose given name runs to the number of syllables below or more carries the calling name as an alias, and the register lists it in the tongue `calling`, with the given name it is cut from in its **Built from** cell.

| Rule                                                           | Count |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----- |
| Fewest syllables in a given name the pages give a calling name | 4     |
