In a merchant's house in [[place-jalnazdal|Jalnazdàl]], a boy of twelve is learning to say the same thing twice. His tutor gives him a word, _nazdàl_, the court where his father's suits are heard, and then its learned twin, _nazdàlo_, the court of record the notaries write in; then _dochème_, a speech, and _dochèmo_, an oration of the kind the academies teach. By the end of the lesson he can hear which of the two a man has chosen, and why, and what it says about him. That is most of what a Provenzian education is for.

This is the word-hoard of [[skill-provnzlng|Provenzal]], the tongue of the [[lore-provenzianclt|Provenzians]]. It is the daughter of ancient Vylari that changed most in its sounds. It lost every case ending, made articles of all three of the old pointing words, softened its consonants between vowels and let the `n` between two vowels fall, leaving the vowel before it nasal. It has taken words twice from outside. The empire's law books gave it a learned layer, so that many words stand in two forms, the inherited one and the one the notaries took back. The fey-touched north gave it the words for light, dream, longing and song, which is why its lyric sounds like no other tongue's.

The words are arranged by what they say about the people who use them: the city first, because a Provenzian names his city before his family; then patronage, love, song and learning, which are the same subject in Provènzia; then the law, the market and the crafts that pay for all of it.

## How to read the tables

Each word table has six columns:

- **Form** is the Provenzal word as it is cited: a noun or an adjective in its one form, a verb in its infinitive.
- **Source** says where the word came from, as the table of sources below sets out. An inherited word names its ancient Vylari form, a loan names the imperial Vylari or Élavendri word it was taken from, and the Form is exactly what the changes make of it.
- **Class** gives the word's kind and, for a noun, its gender.
- **Gloss** is its meaning in Provenzal.
- **Built from** is `—` for a word inherited or borrowed whole. Otherwise it names the Provenzal words and suffixes it is made of.
- **Attested** names a page that uses the word, or `—`.

## Sounds and spelling

Provenzal is written in these pages as its own scribes write it in the [[skill-semrnscrpt|Sêmarion]]. A grave marks the stressed vowel of every word, a tilde marks a nasal vowel, and a stressed nasal vowel takes only its tilde. The stress falls on the next-to-last syllable of a word that ends on a vowel and on the last syllable of a word that ends on a consonant, so the grave is the only mark a reader needs to say a word aloud, and a reader who has the rule never needs it.

The nasal vowels are the tongue's own. Wherever the ancient tongue set an `n` between two vowels, the `n` fell and left the vowel before it nasal: the ancient _yûnâkhôs_, a recruit, accusative _yûnâkhon_, is _jõàche_, said in three syllables. The words borrowed from Élavendri, whose own nasals are written with an `n`, reinforced the sound.

### Word edges

A word opens on a vowel, on one consonant, or on a stop or `f` with `r` or `l` after it. It closes on a vowel or on the consonants below; anything else the old endings left took a support vowel, _-e_.

| Edge                         | Sounds                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pairs that may open a word   | `pr`, `pl`, `tr`, `kr`, `kl`, `br`, `bl`, `dr`, `gr`, `gl`, `fr`, `fl`, `thr` |
| Consonants that close a word | `l`, `n`, `r`, `s`, `th`                                                      |

### Clusters

No more than three consonants stand together, and a pair of letters such as `ch` counts as one.

| Rule                                   | Count |
| -------------------------------------- | ----- |
| Most consonants together inside a word | 3     |

## From ancient Vylari

Provenzal takes each word from its ancient accusative, the case of the object, as the Romance tongues of another world took theirs from the accusative of their ancestor. Fourteen changes, in this order, make a Provenzal word of it. The clicked stops lost their click; the breathed stops became `f`, `ch` and plain `g`, and `h` fell; `th` and `dh` lost their breath at the start of a word, so that no native word opens on them; `w` became `v` and `y` became `j` at the start of a word and `i` elsewhere. Then the consonants softened between vowels: `p` and `b` became `v`, `t` became `d`, `d` became `dh`, and `k` and `g` became `v`. An `n` between two vowels fell and left the vowel before it nasal. The accusative endings fell, the feminine keeping its _-a_; a word left closing on two consonants, or on a consonant the tongue does not close on, took a support _-e_; the long vowels shortened; and the stress moved to the next-to-last syllable of a word ending on a vowel, or the last of one ending on a consonant.

So the ancient _wêrâmis_ (a city), accusative _wêrâmisen_, is _veramìs_, and the ancient _yûnâkhôs_ (a recruit), accusative _yûnâkhon_, is _jõàche_.

## The learned words

The empire's law books, its coins and its University gave Provenzal a second layer of words, taken from imperial Vylari as it is written and not as it was inherited. A learned word keeps its consonants hard between vowels and keeps a final _-o_ where the inherited word lost its ending, so the two forms of one root stand side by side: _nazdàl_ and _nazdàlo_, _marendàl_ and _marendàlo_. The inherited word belongs to the street and the household, the learned one to the notary and the academy.

## The words from the north

Provenzal had little to say about light, dream, longing or the uncanny, and it borrowed what it lacked from [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] across the northern border. The loans came with the songs, and the lyric is still where they live. Every Provenzal word that opens on `th-` is one of them, because the inherited `th` lost its breath at the start of a word; so a word in _th-_ carries the north with it, and the lyric uses them for exactly that.

## Words

### The city and its standing

A Provenzian names his city before his family, and the city words are the oldest civic vocabulary in the tongue, inherited from the speech of the Migration and worn smooth by a thousand years of council meetings. _Veramìs_ is a city, a walled place of citizens, and it is the word a Provenzian means when he says where he is from. The council that governs it, whether of nobles, of rich citizens or of a guild, is _kurthèma_, and the square where it does its business in sight of everyone is _rembalìs_.

Every office in this field ends in _-ànde_, the old agent ending, and the same offices exist in imperial Vylari with the learned ending _-àndo_. The native word names the city's officer, and the learned one the empire's.

### Patron and client

The relation that organizes a Provenzian life is patron and client, and its words are inherited whole. A patron is _daimèla_, a word of the feminine class that takes a man's agreement, as it did in the ancient tongue; his patronage is _daimelàl_, and to give as a patron gives is _daimàthe_. The client's attendance at the patron's door at first light is _pramòlza_, the morning call, and a city's streets are full of men making it.

A man with no patron is _anziròthe_, unplaced, which in Provènzia is not a description of freedom but of exposure. The debts a client runs up are _orfẽàthe_, to owe, and its learned cousin _nezòra_, a debt in money, is a different thing entirely: a Provenzian can repay the second.

### Love and its conduct

Courtly love is a code of conduct, and its vocabulary is borrowed from the north and from the law in roughly equal parts. A lover's vow is _tholmèsa_, an Élavendri word whose _th-_ marks it as poetic; the native _tolmèza_ is the same word as it came down in the south, and it means a vow made before a notary. A _tholmèsa_ cannot be sued on, and that is its point.

The ache of absence, the longing for a person or a place or a time that is not coming back, is _solmère_, which in Élavendri means the smell of rain on its way. Provenzal took the word for its weather and made it a feeling, and it is the word every lyric in the tongue is built around.

### Song and the síladòrs

The great Provenzal lyric is sung by a síladòr, and most of the words for what he does came from the north. The song itself, _olnàre_, the turn of a long song, _aurènthe_, and the tune he hums before he finds the words are Élavendri, and so is the singer: _sourimànde_, the Élavendri word, stands beside the native _sorimànde_, a poet who writes for the page. The difference is the one the tongue cares about. A poet makes a thing a patron can own; a singer makes a thing that exists only while he is singing it.

Rhetoric is the skill the culture rewards most, and its words are inherited and learned at once. An oration is _dochème_, an orator _dochànde_, and the learned forms _dochèmo_ and _dochàndo_ belong to the academies, where a merchant's son learns to tell the two apart.

### The house and the street

### Learning and the academies

Education is valued in Provènzia and the valuing is sincere, and the academy words came in twice: once inherited, once borrowed back from the empire's University. _Marendìs_ is wisdom and _marendàl_ a school, the native forms. The academies at the great cities use the learned _marendàlo_ and _zetmarendàlo_, the University, and a family that can afford it puts a son through one whatever he means to do afterward.

Magic is taught there too. The native word is _tordhìma_; the Élavendri _thordìma_ is the same word with the northern breath, kept for the art as the fey-touched north practices it.

### The house, kin and the name

### Law and the written act
