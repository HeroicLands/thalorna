Sélmoros, the Silent Judge, is the god of death, the underworld and corruption in the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aureldian Pantheon]]. His symbol is a black gate adorned with skeletal vines. The [[affiliation-selmoros|Faith of Sélmoros]] serves him.

## The Silent Judge

The faith teaches that Sélmoros is the one truth that cannot be denied or negotiated: every mortal must pass through his gate. In its teaching he is a fearsome figure, implacable and terrible, but he is respected and not hated, as a necessary force. He is depicted as receiving the dead and weighing them without speaking, and the faith holds that he judges the soul and assigns eternal consequence, where [[lore-ardavondty|Árdavon]] judges conduct among the living. It teaches that the god accepts offerings made in acceptance of death and rejects those made in desperate bargaining, because he cannot be appeased into granting longer life.

[[lore-lusiniadty|Lusinia]] and he are taught as complementary: without his claim mortals would dread an endless extension of life, and the two keep the cycle, Lusinia weaving the thread and Sélmoros cutting it at the appointed hour. [[lore-morvanadty|Morvana]] is his closest partner in the faith's teaching, with decay and death as adjacent mysteries; he judges and claims the soul, and she governs the body's transformation into dust, so that he is the gate and she the passage. His relation to Árdavon is a managed tension, since Árdavon fights chaos and decay while Sélmoros accepts them as inevitable, and the priesthoods of both understand that they serve different aspects of one order. The faith holds that any practice that promises to defeat or circumvent death offends him directly, and it counts necromancers and those who raise the dead as his enemies.

## Worship

Worship is conducted in whispers and shadows and never in open exultation. The faithful come to his shrines with reverence and dread, aware that to invoke the god of death is to acknowledge mortality. Offerings are sparse and austere: withered flowers, rotted grain, fruit past ripeness. Some noble families keep his shrine in the family crypt and pour wine on the graves of their ancestors each year, asking him to shelter their spirits.

Devotion is largely private. The gravely ill and those who sense death approaching make pilgrimage to his temples in their last days to seek absolution and peace, and a priest sits with the dying and recites prayers that affirm the god's justice and the peace the faith teaches lies beyond the gate. The dying speak their final testimony, confessing wrongs and affirming good, so they may face him with an unburdened heart; the priesthood is counselor to the dying and keeper of deathbed confessions.

Kalmethandi, the tenth month of the [[lore-commoncal|Vilérian Calendar]], is the month of going to the ancestors and belongs to him; the winter solstice falls on its first day. The three-day [[lore-silentpassage|Festival of the Silent Passage]] runs through the last three nights of Morvanandi and ends at dawn on the first of Kalmethandi. The temples close to the living and the priesthood performs elaborate funerary rites for the year's dead whose families ask for them. At dusk masked priests bearing torches go through the streets, and those who see them fall silent. Families set black candles in their windows and speak the names of the dead aloud, in the belief that the god will guide them safely through the underworld. The festival does not celebrate death; it affirms his sovereignty and submits to his claim.

## Priesthood and Temples

The priesthood is smaller and more secretive than those of the other gods, and its hierarchy is measured by wisdom and philosophical depth more than administrative rank. The Kolsarnor at its apex is rarely the same person for more than a decade, since the office demands a contemplation of death few can sustain, and settles doctrine and oversees the great temple complex where the most sacred rites are performed. Below the Kolsarnor the Limori, the Threshold Keepers, are ordained priests scattered through the major cities of the west who perform funeral rites, keep the temple records of the dead and counsel the dying. They are scholars of the underworld, and the nobility consult them on succession and inheritance because they understand what death means for the passing of power. The Novices of the Silent Watch, often people who have survived near-death and feel called, train in funerary practice and theology and serve as gravediggers, tomb-keepers and assistants at funerals; many stay in the rank for life.

His temples stand on the edges of cities, near cemeteries or in quiet groves, places the faith treats as natural transitions between the living world and the realm of death. They are built of dark wood and worked stone in an understated style meant to blend with the landscape, lit by oil lamps and heavy with incense and myrrh. The great temples keep necropoli and libraries of the dead, with underground chambers where the honored dead lie and where families pay for the perpetual care of their tombs. Endowments from families with private crypts, fees for funeral rites and the gifts of the pious support the priesthood and keep it independent of any kingdom or noble house, and its priests perform final rites for paupers and the unclaimed dead without payment.

## Present

The priests of the Faith of Sélmoros and the [[affiliation-lusinia|Faith of Lusinia]] coordinate in blessing newborns and the dying, and the priesthoods of Sélmoros and the [[affiliation-morvana|Faith of Morvana]] work together to honor the dead, sometimes in temples built close together. Even the followers of [[affiliation-blackflame|the Black Flame]], despite their contempt for the faiths of order, respect Sélmoros as a force that cannot be bargained with or overcome by strength of will. Priests of the [[affiliation-ardavon|Faith of Árdavon]] and of Sélmoros cooperate in matters of oaths sworn by the dying and of inheritance disputes where events suggest divine judgment.
