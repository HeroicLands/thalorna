_The Gatekeeper of Death—a shrouded figure, faceless, with a black conch at its lips._

Kālavrata is the warden of the threshold between the life that has ended and the life to come, and he keeps it with a rigor the rest of the pantheon does not share. He demands no devotion, grants no personal favor, and answers no petition for long life or safe passage through danger—what he does is guide the soul onward, impartially, and he does that whether or not he is asked. The Ordeals undertaken in his name—the Vigil of Final Breath kept at a stranger's bedside, the Walk Between Fires among three villages' cremation grounds, the Silent Year that speaks only the rites of passage—earn the undertaker nothing from him. They are, the theology insists, what a person does _for_ the threshold, not what is done to move him.

No face is carved for him, because the tradition holds that no living soul may look upon it before the hour of its own passage. His black conch is sounded at every threshold-moment a Vedyari household keeps—birth, coming-of-age, the final breath—and a black conch shell hangs above most doorposts for exactly that reason. A threshold lamp burns through the night a family member is dying, kept alight until the passage is complete, and a coin of Passage is set in the hand of the dead at cremation: not an offering to him, the priests say, but a reminder to the soul of what it is bringing to the gate.

Where [[affiliation-rasikara|Rásikara]] is the fire that unmakes the body, Kālavrata is what happens after—the cremation-fires end; his conch calls the soul on. His mountain is [[place-kalashkhra|Kālaśikhara]], the one great summit of the Eastern Reach the Pass-Shrines have never built on, bare enough that snow will not hold on its upper faces. No shrine stands there because none is needed: the older explanation, that the peak is already his and wants no house built on it, is younger than the silence it explains, but nobody has ever offered a better one.
