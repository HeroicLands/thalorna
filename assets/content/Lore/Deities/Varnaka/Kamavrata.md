_The Celestial Lover — a youth garlanded with flowers, a sugarcane bow strung with five flower-arrows._

Kāmavrata is desire and the creative joy that issues from it — the god of lovers, of the newly married, of those hoping for a child, and of every art by which the beauty of the world is drawn out and celebrated: poetry, music, dance, sculpture, the preparation of scented oils, the keeping of gardens. In the pantheon's own reckoning his work is the counterpart of [[affiliation-vyalendra|Vyālendra]]'s: where Vyālendra gives the world its form, Kāmavrata gives it its delight, and Vedyari theology treats the two as necessary to each other rather than as rivals for a worshipper's attention.

A floral garland hangs fresh on his households' doorposts through spring and summer and dried through the dry season, and it is a floral garland, not a crown or a ring, that is set on the shoulders of a newly married couple. A phial of scented oil, consecrated in his temples, anoints the couple again on the wedding night and on each anniversary after; the sugarcane bow and flower-arrow his clergy carry at festival are ceremonial rather than a weapon, though the theology is careful to note that desire, like an arrow, still lands somewhere.

He is the most widely loved of the ten and the least institutionally demanding. His shrines cluster at gardens and rivers, his festivals are the year's most popular, and his clergy are famous for turning no one away — a fair number of Vedyarans who would not call themselves Kāmavratan nevertheless keep at least one of his festivals, on the reasoning that a god who asks so little should not be refused the little he asks.
