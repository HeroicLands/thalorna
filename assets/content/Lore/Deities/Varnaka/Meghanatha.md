_Lord of Thunder—a dark, wild-haired figure with a great drum under one arm and a lightning-spear raised in the other._

Meghanātha is not a gentle god, and his worshippers do not pretend otherwise. He is the monsoon itself—the storm that feeds the plain and the storm that shuts the mountain road are, to a Vedyari farmer or sailor, the same storm taken from two sides, and his gifts arrive with the same violence whichever side receives them. Fishermen bless their boats with his first rain of the season; farmers time a planting to his drums; caravan-masters read his temper before committing a season's goods to a pass. All three are asking the same god for the same thing: to be spared this once.

A temple drum is beaten at the monsoon's opening and at the climax of every major rite in his name, and the first stroke of the season is answered, household by household, with a wooden spoon beaten against a pot—an old, unpriested custom that acknowledges his arrival without waiting for a temple to announce it. Sailors and farmers wear a lightning amulet, a stylized silver bolt, as a sign of his protection, and a rain bowl is set out on the roof at the first sign of the monsoon; the first rain to fall into it is kept as blessed water for the year. An object struck by lightning near a household is carried to the village shrine and dedicated, and it is considered poor form ever to put it to profane use again.

[[place-meghashkhr|Meghaśikhara]] is his mountain in more than name: the wet air comes up from the south, meets the peak, and stops, so that what falls out of the cloud falls on the Vedyari side and the country beyond the ridge gets nothing. The window at Meghadvāra beneath it—the shortest of the six passes—is short because he makes it short, and the Ösket who read the cloud on his summit at first light are, in effect, reading his mood for the day.
