_Lord of Thunder — a dark, wild-haired figure with a great drum under one arm and a lightning-spear raised in the other._

Meghanātha is not a gentle god, and his worshippers do not pretend otherwise. He is the monsoon. The storm that feeds the plain and the storm that shuts the mountain road are one storm taken from two sides, and his gifts arrive with the same violence on either side.

Fishermen bless their boats with his first rain of the season. Farmers time a planting to his drums. Caravan-masters read his temper before committing a season's goods to a pass. All three ask the same god to be spared this once.

A temple drum is beaten at the monsoon's opening and at the climax of every major rite in his name. Household by household, the first stroke of the season is answered with a wooden spoon beaten against a pot. The custom is old and has no priest in it, and it greets his arrival without waiting on a temple.

Sailors and farmers wear a lightning amulet, a stylised silver bolt, as a sign of his protection. A rain bowl goes out on the roof at the first sign of the monsoon, and the first rain to fall into it is kept as blessed water for the year. An object struck by lightning near a household is carried to the village shrine and dedicated there, and to put it afterward to profane use is poor form.

[[place-meghashkhr|Meghaśikhara]] is his mountain. The wet air comes up from the south, meets the peak and stops; what falls out of the cloud falls on the Vedyari side, and the country beyond the ridge gets nothing.

Meghadvāra beneath it has the shortest window of the six passes, and he is held to be the reason for it. The Ösket read the cloud on his summit at first light, which is to say they read his mood for the day.
