_The Shaper of Worlds — a four-armed figure of serene focus, holding a compass, a measuring cord, a chisel and a golden lotus._

Vyālendra is the architect of existence, the god who gave shape to earth, sky and every living thing, and who continues to give shape to every new work of human making. Vedyari theology holds that every founded city, every raised temple, every bridge and irrigation channel repeats in miniature the original act of cosmic shaping — to build well is to serve him, to build poorly is to mar the pattern the world itself was made from. [[affiliation-vyalendravada|Vyālendravāda]] takes the doctrine to its furthest point, holding him the single deity from whom all the others emanate; the [[affiliation-trimurtisampradaya|Trimūrti-sampradāya]] reads him instead as one face of a triad with [[affiliation-mahajaya|Mahájaya]] and [[affiliation-rasikara|Rásikara]] — but both traditions agree that no city stands without his shaping and no builder works without, at some level, invoking him.

A golden lotus medallion is worn by master craftsmen and senior clergy as a mark of vocation; a measuring cord, knotted, consecrates the foundation of every major work; a carved foundation-stone, incised with his invocation, is the first stone laid in any construction of consequence, and a household founding its own home buries a smaller version beneath the first pillar in echo of the public rite. Artisans bless the tools of their trade each morning and offer a pinch of powdered gold or saffron before beginning a new commission.

[[place-sarvaprbhv|Sarvaprabhava]], the source-temple at the head of the Sarvada in Dhanurkota's own upper valley, is his: rebuilt on the same footing eleven times in the temple's record, each rebuilding completed within a single open season, which its masons read as a fact about the mountain rather than about their own predecessors' workmanship. It is also, among the villages downstream of it, a popular pilgrimage for those hoping for a child — the god of foundations doing double duty as the god of beginnings of every kind. On the Mahānadi plain, Rājapur keeps its own great Vyālendra temple at the janapada's centre, raised on the ruins of the kingdom that once stood there; its Spring Festival is the year's great pilgrimage draw, in testimony to the god who gave the ruin a second shape.
