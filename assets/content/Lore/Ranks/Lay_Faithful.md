Of a tradition by observance, keeping what it asks of a household and holding no office in it.

## What This Standing Is

The ordinary observant, and the standing the overwhelming majority of a faith's
people hold for life. What makes someone lay faithful is the practice rather than
any act of admission: a fire kindled at sunrise and sunset, a lamp tended, a mark
set at a doorpost, a ledger kept, blood given on the days a calendar appoints, a
fast observed, an offering brought to a shrine. A household can do all of it
without a priest, and in most traditions a household does.

This is a standing in the faith and not merely a presence near it. The lay faithful
are who the faith is for; its officers exist to serve them; and a faith's reckoning
of its own strength is a reckoning of them. What they hold no part in is office—the
rites performed on the tradition's behalf, the teaching held in confidence, the
governance of its temples.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

The faith's sanctions reach a person here, which is what distinguishes this
standing from a bystander's. Barring from the rites is the sanction, and it bites
because the rites include the ones at the end of a life: in most traditions the
funerary rite is the one every member eventually requires, and refusing it is the
one sentence that outlives the body.

## Privileges

The rites of a life—birth, marriage, healing, burial—performed by whoever holds the
office for them. Access to a temple or a shrine on the terms it offers everyone, a
hearing for a petition, and the faith's account of what is happening when something
inexplicable happens.

## Obligations

The observance itself, kept at the household's own expense and on the calendar's
own schedule. Dues and offerings where the tradition levies them, a share of what a
season produces where it takes one, and the prohibitions—what may not be eaten,
touched, said or done—which are the part of the standing a stranger notices first.
