Vowed to an order for life, holding no property and owing obedience to its officers.

## What This Standing Is

The full member of a religious order, admitted by profession at the end of a term
of formation. Three things are given up at once and all three are the standing:
property, which the professed holds none of and maintains nothing beyond what the
house requires; self-direction, which passes to the order's officers; and the
possibility of leaving, because the vow is for life.

What is received in exchange is the order's work and the order's keeping. A
professed member is maintained by the house for as long as he lives, and the
house's business becomes his—a grove to keep, a discipline to practise, a charge to
hold when one is given.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

The vow is the law here, and it reaches further than a contract would: an order can
direct where a professed member lives, what he does with his days and what he may
own, and he has agreed to all of it. Against that stands the order's own rule, which
binds its officers as well, and a professed member's appeal runs to whoever enforces
the rule rather than to any authority outside the order.

Expulsion takes the habit back and declares the vows void, and no house of the
order receives the person again—which is the sanction the vow's permanence makes
possible.

## Privileges

Maintenance for life and the order's protection. A voice in the house's chapter
where the rule gives its professed members one, and eligibility for the charges the
order has to fill, which are filled from this standing and no lower.

## Obligations

Poverty, obedience and the rule, kept without term. Whatever labour, observance or
silence the order's discipline imposes, and whatever charge its officers assign.
