Head of a junior line of a house, holding lands or offices of its own and standing behind the senior line.

## What This Standing Is

A house that has lasted several generations throws off lines. A younger son is
settled with land or an office, his descendants keep it, and what began as a
household inside the house becomes a branch with property, dependents and dealings
of its own. The head of such a branch holds this standing: of the house's blood,
above every other kinsman in it, and under the senior line in every matter where
the two could differ.

The branch is not a separate house. It takes the house's name, it is counted in the
house's strength, and its lands were the house's grant.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

The order of claims is the whole of this standing. A cadet head commands his own
line and answers for it, and he stands behind the senior line in succession, in
precedence and in any dispute between the two—which is what keeps a house with
several branches from being several houses. Where the senior line fails entirely,
the claim falls to the cadet heads in order, and this is the standing from which a
house is continued.

## Privileges

Command of a branch and of what it holds: its lands, its offices, its people, its
income. A seat where the house's kinsmen gather, ahead of every kinsman who heads
nothing.

## Obligations

To answer the house's head with the branch's strength when called, to render what
the original grant reserved, and to press no claim ahead of the senior line. A
cadet head who does press one is understood to be attempting a separate house, and
the house treats it as that rather than as a dispute.
