A woman comes to the door of a Mahájayan household and says she has finished the **Fair Measure**, a year of public weighing with no complaint of short weight from anyone. "You take her word," the keeper of the village shrine tells the householder's son, who has never met a **Vratin** and is wondering where the proof is kept. "There is no register. There is no priest who watched. If she says she is under vow, she is, and you receive her as you would a guest the god sent."

Bound for life by a vow completed in the ordeal a god sets, in a devotion that confers no office: that is the standing of a **Vow-Bound Devotee**.

## What This Standing Is

Some devotions have no priesthood, no temple establishment and no ladder of office. What such a devotion confers is a vow. The devotee undertakes an ordeal the god is held to have set, completes it, and is under vow from then on. The ordeal is the whole of the standing. There is no renewal, no advancement, and nothing above it to reach.

Because the devotion has no officers, nobody administers the vow and nobody witnesses it on the god's behalf. Households and shrines go on the devotee's own account of having completed the ordeal, so the standing is held in the way a reputation is held.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

The vow is never released, because no office holds the power to release it. A devotee who abandons the observance is under vow still and failing it. That is a matter between the devotee and the god, and it reaches no tribunal. What the devotion can withhold is recognition, and recognition is the whole of what it was giving.

## Privileges

The first privilege is the favor the ordeal was undertaken for, which is the point of the undertaking and is held to be the god's to grant or withhold. Beyond that, a Vratin is received as one in any household that honors the god, and is asked to perform the god's observance wherever a household wants it performed by someone bound.

## Obligations

The obligation is the vow's own terms, for life, whatever those terms were. Where the ordeal imposed a continuing abstinence, a continuing practice or a continuing service, that is the obligation, and it does not lapse with age or with distance from where it was undertaken.
