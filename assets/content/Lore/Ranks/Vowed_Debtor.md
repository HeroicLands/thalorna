Bound to a faith by a debt pledged or received beyond what its ordinary observance requires.

## What This Standing Is

An observant who has taken on something the calendar does not ask. The debt is
pledged—a captive dedicated in advance, a pilgrimage undertaken, a season of
offering vowed—or it is incurred by accepting something the faith gave: a returned
relative, an intercession granted, a thing nobody else could have supplied. Either
way it is specific, it has terms, and it stands until the terms are met.

This is one rung above the ordinary observant and well below any office. What
raises it is the debt, not any instruction received or rite performed, and when the
debt is discharged the holder does not fall back to where he was: a faith reckons
someone who has borne a debt as having borne it.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

A debtor who defaults is not merely lapsed. The faith holds a claim and will
pursue it—through a household, through a temple, through the ledger a soul is
reckoned on—and a debt unmet is held to follow the debtor past the end of the life
it was incurred in. Debtors are accordingly the most reliable adherents a faith
has, and the most desperate.

## Privileges

Precedence over the ordinary observant in the faith's own reckoning, access to
whoever administers the debt, and the thing the debt was incurred for, which in
most cases has already been received.

## Obligations

The debt's own terms: shelter, silence and food owed for as long as what was given
lasts; blood given beyond the appointed days; a journey completed; a dedication
honored when the time comes. Nothing releases the terms early, and the faith is the
party that judges when they are met.
