Holding a seat on the senior body that decides what an order teaches, whom it admits and who heads it.

## What This Standing Is

Above an order's officers sits a small body that decides the things outlasting
them: what the order teaches, whom it admits, what its standards are, what its
terms of service are, and who presides over it. A seat on that body is this
standing.

Seats are few and the ways to one are narrow. Some come by appointment of the
sitting members, some by election from a constituent chapter, grove or college,
and some attach to a particular charge—one seat to each recognized house, one to
each great grove, a fixed number at the centre. The seat is held for a term or for
life according to the order's rule, and the body is almost always small enough for
every member to be known to every other.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

What this body decides binds the officers beneath it, including officers whose rank
reads higher: a head of an order presides over it rather than overruling it, and in
orders where the head is elected, this is the body that elects and constrains him.
A seated elder is answerable to the body and to nobody else in the order.

## Privileges

A vote on doctrine, on admission and on succession, and a voice in whatever
conclave chooses the chair. A seated elder sees what the body sees, which is
ordinarily more of the order's business than any officer sees.

## Obligations

To attend when the body sits, to be bound by what it decides even where the
decision went against the vote cast, and to keep its deliberations where they
belong. Where the order admits by examination, the examining is this body's work.
