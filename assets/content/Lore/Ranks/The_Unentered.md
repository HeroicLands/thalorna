Under a tradition's observances without having entered it, holding nothing it confers and owing it nothing.

## What This Standing Is

Most of a tradition's people stand here, and most of them stand here all their
lives. They live inside its reach—the observances a household keeps, the
prohibitions a village respects, the pacts a clan inherits, the calendar the
season turns on—and they have passed through no act of admission. Some have
approached: they sit in a hall where teaching is given, or in a grove a loss has
brought them to, and are asked nothing for it. Some have heard the tradition's
case and not accepted it. Some are outside it entirely, served by it and told
nothing of how the service is done.

What all of them share is that the tradition has conferred nothing. There is no
oath, no instruction given in confidence, no mystery held, and no name on any
roll.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

A tradition's internal discipline cannot reach a person at this standing,
because it has nothing to take back. Permission to be present is withdrawn at a
word and needs no cause, and withdrawing it is the whole of the sanction
available. A grievance against someone here is a matter for whatever civil
authority covers the ground.

## Privileges

To keep the observances, to attend what is public, and to ask the tradition's
officers for the ordinary services it gives anyone—a rite at a birth, a burial,
a reading, a ruling on a dispute. Where a body permits attendance at its
teaching, attendance costs nothing and commits to nothing.

## Obligations

None the tradition can enforce. What is owed at this standing is owed to the
custom of the place and to the neighbours who keep it.
