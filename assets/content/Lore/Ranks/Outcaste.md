Outside a society's order by birth or by sentence, excluded from its wells, its temples and its courts.

## What This Standing Is

A society that sorts its people into orders also has a place outside them, and
people are put there two ways. Some are sentenced to it for a transgression the
order cannot absorb. Some are born to it, because the exclusion is inherited and a
child of the excluded begins where the parent ended.

That inheritance is what distinguishes this standing from an expulsion. An
expulsion reaches the person expelled; this reaches a lineage. In societies that
keep such a category, the people in it are numerous, permanent and necessary—they
do the work the orders will not do—and they are nonetheless held to be outside the
thing the orders collectively are.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

The exclusions are concrete rather than ceremonial: the common wells, the temples
and the courts are all shut. Shut courts are the heart of it, because a person who
cannot plead cannot be wronged in any way the law will hear, and anything done to
them is answered for only where someone else's interest was touched. Where the
society recognizes names at law, this standing holds none that it recognizes.

## Privileges

None the society confers. What people here hold, they hold among themselves and by
whatever arrangements they make with the orders that need their labour.

## Obligations

Every prohibition the orders attach to contact: which water may be drawn, which
ground may be entered, which work must be done and which must not be touched. The
prohibitions are enforced by the orders rather than by any officer, which makes
them harder to appeal than a sentence.
