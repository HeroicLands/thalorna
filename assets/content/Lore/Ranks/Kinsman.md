Of a household's blood or sworn following, entitled to its protection, its quarrels and its share.

## What This Standing Is

Full membership of a kin group—a household, a hearth, a princely house—held by
descent from it or by having sworn into its following. In societies built on kin
rather than on office this is the standing that matters most, because what a person
can expect from anybody else is settled by which kin group will answer for them.

Birth is the ordinary door and it needs no act: a child of the blood holds this
standing from the first. Where a house takes sworn men into its following, their
oath puts them here too, and the house's own reckoning makes no practical
distinction afterwards.

## How the Law Treats a Person Here

Three entitlements come together and they are the standing: protection, meaning the
kin group answers for harm done to its own; the quarrel, meaning it takes up that
answer as a matter the whole group is party to; and the share, meaning a claim on
what the group holds—its fire, its winter store, its herds, its fortune, a portion
of what a season brings in.

The reciprocal is exact. A kinsman is liable for the group's quarrels as well as
protected by them, and an injury done by him is answered for out of the group's
means. This is why kin groups watch their own: a single member can commit everyone.

## Privileges

A place at the hearth, a share of the stores and the increase, the group's name in
dealings with strangers, and the group's arms behind any claim made. Where the
group trades, its credit is available to its kinsmen.

## Obligations

To answer the group's call, to bear its quarrels, and to bring in rather than draw
down—a kinsman who consumes without contributing is kept, and is also talked about.
Marriages, oaths and dealings that bind the group are the head's business rather
than the individual kinsman's.
