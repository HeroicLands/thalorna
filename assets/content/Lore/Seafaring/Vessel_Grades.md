A vessel is rated by two grades. Each runs from A, the best, to E, the worst, and a vessel's grades are written as one letter each.

**Sailing grade.** The vessel's quality under sail, taking in how fast it goes, how close to the wind it can hold and how it behaves in a storm. A dash means it has no sail. A battened rig lifts the grade one step ([[doc-vesselrigs|Vessel Rigs]]); the grade as written already includes it.

**Oar grade.** The vessel's quality when rowed. A dash means it is not built to be rowed, though it can have a few long sweeps for moving about in harbor.

## What a Grade Gives

The grades set two figures for a day's travel, described in [[doc-seatravel|Travel at Sea]]. Distances are in leagues; a league is 3 nautical miles, about 3.45 miles.

| Grade | Becalmed or headed day under sail | Typical moving day under sail | Typical day under oar |
| ----- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------- |
| A     | 0%                                | 46 leagues (159 miles)        | 18 leagues (62 miles) |
| B     | 0%                                | 39 leagues (135 miles)        | 15 leagues (52 miles) |
| C     | 5%                                | 34 leagues (117 miles)        | 13 leagues (45 miles) |
| D     | 10%                               | 32 leagues (110 miles)        | 11 leagues (38 miles) |
| E     | 20%                               | 28 leagues (97 miles)         | 9 leagues (31 miles)  |

The chance of a day without progress under sail is adjusted for the rig. Every oar grade has a 5 percent chance of a day without progress, because the limit on rowing is how long the rowers can keep it up, and the wind does not matter.

## Hull Shape

The slenderness of a hull changes the typical sailing day. Two leagues are added for a fine hull, with a length of five times its beam or more, and taken away for a bluff hull, with a length of three times its beam or less.

## Using Grades in Play

A vessel that has both sail and oars uses its sailing grade for a day under way, because oars add little speed once the sails are drawing, and the rowers rest until the wind fails. A vessel with oars and no sail uses its oar grade for every day. A vessel with sail and no oars never rows.
