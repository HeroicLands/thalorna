# terran_analog: Vylari reads Latin in its grammar and nowhere in its words. Ancient Vylari carries the Latin pattern its grammar brief sets (gender carried by agreement, case endings with deliberate syncretism, two aspect stems, deponents, the subjunctive as free indirect discourse, three demonstratives of person and attitude) plus a fourth, hallowed gender of its own; imperial Vylari loosens it as spoken Latin loosened toward the Romance tongues. Every root here is coined to the tongue's rules and checked against Latin, ancient Greek, Occitan, Spanish and other word lists; none is the Latin, Greek or Romance word for its meaning. The patron and client, the morning call, the wax ancestor masks, the hallowed city boundary and the legion's eagle are Roman institutions the empire shares.

A grammar master in [[place-magnapolis|Magnápolis]] begins every new class the same way. On the board he writes three words—_thrâkhon_, _vêrôsâ_ and _ôrzâs_, a slave, a citizen and the sovereign—and declines each of them down the board while the boys copy. The slave declines as a pot or a plow declines, in the neuter. The citizen declines as a man or a woman does. The sovereign declines as a god does, in endings no other living person may take. By noon the class has learned the four genders of the old tongue, and the master has taught them the empire.

This is the word-hoard of [[skill-vylarilng|Vylari]] in its two stages. **Ancient Vylari** is the formal tongue of the [[lore-pelwarpepl|Pelwar]] who settled the western coast in the [[lore-pelwarmigr|Migration]], reconstructed by the empire's scholars from the oldest inscriptions and from the tongues descended from it. Imperial Vylari is the empire's living speech, looser in its grammar and lighter in its sounds, and its high register keeps the ancient endings in law, liturgy and oratory. The tables give both forms of every word, the rules that turn the one into the other, the endings and suffixes that make new words, and the way a Vylarian builds a name. A word made from these by those rules is a Vylari word.

The language page sets out the grammar in full. This page carries the words, arranged by what they say about the people who use them: the longest tables are the ones on precedence, law and the legions, and the shortest is the one on weather.

## How to read the tables

Each word table has six columns:

- **Form** is the imperial word as it is cited: a noun in its nominative, an adjective in its masculine, a verb in its infinitive.
- **Ancient** is the same word in ancient Vylari. The imperial form is exactly what the sound changes below make of it.
- **Class** gives the word's kind and, for a noun, its class and gender, from the list below.
- **Gloss** is its meaning in brief.
- **Built from** is `—` for a root, made from nothing smaller. Otherwise it names the imperial words, stems and suffixes the form is made of, joined by `+`, and the form is exactly what they give. A stem is written with a hyphen after it.
- **Attested** names a page that uses the word, or `—` for a word no page uses yet.

## Sounds and spelling

Vylari is written in these pages as its own scribes romanize it: a circumflex marks a long vowel, and nothing marks the stress, because the stress follows from the lengths. The [[skill-semrnscrpt|Sêmarion]] does not mark length at all in ordinary writing. A schoolbook or a lawyer's fair copy sets a small bar over a long vowel where a misreading is possible, and a poet leaves it off, so that a word whose cases differ only in the length of a vowel can be read two ways on the page.

### Word edges

A word opens on a vowel, on one consonant, or on a stop followed by `l` or `r`, and the table names those pairs. A word closes on a vowel or on one of five consonants. A verb stem is bound and never closes a word, so it may end on any consonant. The edges are the same in both stages.

| Edge                         | Sounds                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pairs that may open a word   | `pr`, `pl`, `tr`, `kr`, `kl`, `br`, `bl`, `dr`, `gr`, `gl`, `phr`, `phl`, `thr`, `khr`, `khl` |
| Consonants that close a word | `s`, `n`, `r`, `l`, `x`                                                                       |

### Clusters

No more than three consonants stand together inside a word, and a pair of letters such as `th` counts as one. Two vowels never stand side by side except as one of the four diphthongs; where an ending would bring two vowels together, the stem's vowel gives way, as the suffix table shows.

| Rule                                   | Count |
| -------------------------------------- | ----- |
| Most consonants together inside a word | 3     |

### Stress

The stress falls on the next-to-last syllable when that syllable is long—when it holds a long vowel or a diphthong, or closes on a consonant—and otherwise on the one before it. _Sôrimâ_ is stressed on its first syllable, since _ri_ is short; _zarândôs_ on its second, since _rân_ is long. A word of two syllables stresses its first. Nobody writes the stress, and nobody needs to.

| Rule            | Value     |
| --------------- | --------- |
| Stress falls on | by weight |

## From ancient to imperial

Four changes, in this order, turn every ancient word into its imperial form, and the lexicon's Form column is what they give. The clicked stops lost their click; the breathed voiced stops became `z` and `g`; `w` became `v`; and `b` softened to `v` between two vowels. So _dharon_ (an oath) became _zaron_, _k'arâlzon_ (the kept fire) became _karâlzon_, and _lâbûrâ_ (an ancestor's mask) became _lâvûrâ_.

### Sound changes

| Step | Change                    | Where          |
| ---- | ------------------------- | -------------- |
| V1   | `t' d' k' g'` → `t d k g` | anywhere       |
| V2   | `dh` → `z`; `gh` → `g`    | anywhere       |
| V3   | `w` → `v`                 | anywhere       |
| V4   | `b` → `v`                 | between vowels |

Two more changes belong to speech and are never written. In ordinary talk a long vowel in an unstressed syllable is said short, so the street hears _zarandos_ where the page writes _zarândôs_; a speaker of High Vylari holds every length, and the difference marks his education as clearly as his clothes. In the [[place-hylen|Hylen]] lowlands `th` is said `s`, and a Hylen man's _sôrimândôs_ (a poet) and his _thîlândôs_ (a scribe) begin alike, with a hiss.

## The four genders

Ancient Vylari sorts every noun into one of four genders, and an adjective, a pointing word and a participle take the gender of the noun they go with. Three of the genders are the familiar ones, assigned mostly by a noun's class and only loosely by sense. The fourth is the one that makes the language Vylari.

| Gender    | What takes it                                                                                             | Examples                                                           |
| --------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| neuter    | things, tools, beasts of burden, children not yet of age, and slaves                                      | _thrâkhon_ (a slave), _nûrizon_ (a child), _zaron_ (an oath)       |
| masculine | most words of class II and many of class III, persons and offices among them                              | _thelmôs_ (a client), _kalmêthar_ (an ancestor)                    |
| feminine  | most words of class I and class V, and some trees of class II                                             | _vêrôsâ_ (a citizen), _azênê_ (a line), _pîrkêlôs_ (an olive tree) |
| hallowed  | the gods, the sovereign, the founder of a house, the great river, the gold coin, and anything consecrated | _ulthâs_ (a god), _ôrzâs_ (the sovereign), _zêrthâs_ (a founder)   |

The hallowed gender has no sex. A god and a goddess decline alike, and so do an emperor and an empress, both _ôrzâs_. It has no dual either, since nothing hallowed is counted in pairs.

**Moving a word between the genders is a legal act**, and the language has a verb for each move. A slave freed by _lîskaimâ_ (manumission) is given a gender for the first time: the freedman is _lîskôthôs_, and his name is declined as a man's. A family the Senate ennobles is raised by _ulthâthen_ (to hallow): its founder's name takes the hallowed endings, and every member of the house carries them in the given name from then on. A house disgraced is unhallowed by _anulthâthen_, and its names go back to the ordinary declensions. The gravest act of all, _nêhârlon_, takes the name out of the grammar altogether: an unnamed man's name is struck from every roll and declined in no gender, because it is not said.

**What a GM can do with it.** Imperial Vylari lost the hallowed gender in ordinary speech, but its endings live on in names, and every Vylarian hears them:

- A given name ending in _-âs_ claims a noble house. A commoner who takes one is claiming what he does not have, and a magistrate can fine him for it.
- The ending says nothing of sex. _Thêlvarâs_ may be a lord or a lady, and a stranger learns which from the title before the name.
- To speak of a free person in the neuter—_that thing_, in the old endings—calls him a slave or a child. Between citizens it is the gravest insult the grammar allows, and it has started duels.
- A sovereign is never "he" or "she" in formal speech. Officials, priests and anyone addressing the throne use the hallowed forms throughout, and a provincial who slips into the masculine marks himself at once.

### Suffixes

Most new words are a stem and a suffix. An office is a verb stem with _-ândôs_ (a man who does it) or _-ândâ_ (a woman), and the office itself takes _-âlon_: from _ûsâthen_ (to set over) come _ûsândôs_, a prefect, and _ûsâlon_, a prefecture. A suffix that opens on a vowel replaces the stem's last vowel, so _sârkhenâ_ (a temple) and _-ândôs_ give _sârkhenândôs_, a priest.

| Suffix   | Stem                     | Makes                                               | Example                     |
| -------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| `-âs`    | replaces the final vowel | a hallowed noun: a god, a founder, a hallowed thing | _ulth-_ > _ulthâs_          |
| `-ândôs` | replaces the final vowel | a man who does it, or holds the office of it        | _ûs-_ > _ûsândôs_           |
| `-ândâ`  | replaces the final vowel | a woman who does it, or holds the office of it      | _sârkhenâ_ > _sârkhenândâ_  |
| `-âlon`  | replaces the final vowel | the office, the body or the whole of it             | _ôrz-_ > _ôrzâlon_          |
| `-ândon` | replaces the final vowel | a thing done or made                                | _tâmb-_ > _tâmbândon_       |
| `-ândar` | replaces the final vowel | doing it: the present participle                    | _hûmelth-_ > _hûmelthândar_ |
| `-thôs`  | added                    | done, suffered: the past participle                 | _khêlmâr-_ > _khêlmârthôs_  |
| `-ênôs`  | replaces the final vowel | of it, from it: said of a place or a people         | _ôrz-_ > _ôrzênôs_          |
| `-âra`   | replaces the final vowel | a town of it                                        | _zêmrôl-_ > _zêmrôlâra_     |
| `-enna`  | replaces the final vowel | a farm, an estate or a village of it                | —                           |
| `-ôra`   | replaces the final vowel | a height or a stronghold of it                      | —                           |
| `-ândis` | replaces the final vowel | those of it: a house name                           | _karâlz-_ > _karâlzândis_   |
| `-âtês`  | replaces the final vowel | of the line of it: a house name                     | —                           |

A compound is the stem of one word set before a whole second word, the first describing the second: _ûlmêr-_ (water) and _pôzêrâ_ (a road) give _ûlmêrpôzêrâ_, the aqueduct.

## Making names

**A person** carries a given name and a house name, the given name first, and a man of note adds an earned name after both: a name given by a city or an army after a deed, or by a family after a death. The house name never changes with the bearer's sex.

### Given names

A given name is a root of the lexicon with a gender ending. Parents choose the root for what they wish on the child—a quality, a virtue, a god's favor—and the ending says whether the child is a son, a daughter or of a noble house.

| Ending              | Who                               | Class     |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------- |
| `-ôs`               | a man                             | class II  |
| `-ar`, `-is`, `-en` | a man or a woman                  | class III |
| `-â`                | a woman                           | class I   |
| `-ê`                | a woman                           | class V   |
| `-âs`               | a man or a woman of a noble house | hallowed  |

A noble child's given name takes the hallowed _-âs_ whatever its sex, so _Thêlvarâs_ may be the heir or his sister. The same root serves both: _Sôlkimôs_ and _Sôlkimâ_ are a brother and a sister named for gravity, and their cousin in the senior line is _Sôlkimâs_.

### House names

A house name says whose descendants the bearer belongs to. The oldest houses name themselves from their founder with the genitive plural _-ândis_, "those of": a house founded by a man called _Kalmêthar_ is the _Kalmêtharândis_. Houses the sovereign has raised in the last few generations take _-âtês_, "of the line of", and a provincial family of standing is often named for its estate with _-ênôs_.

| Ending   | Who                                                | Class                      |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| `-ândis` | a house of the old nobility, named for its founder | class III, genitive plural |
| `-âtês`  | a house raised by the sovereign                    | class III                  |
| `-ênôs`  | a provincial family named for its estate           | class II                   |

After a vowel the genitive plural is _-ndis_, and after an `n` it is _-is_, so a founder _Thêlvarân_ gives the _Thêlvarânis_.

### Calling names

A Vylari calling name is a child's name. It is the given name cut before its second vowel, with the neuter ending _-izon_ added: _Sôlkimâ_ is _Sôlkizon_ to her mother and her brothers, and _Thêlvarâs_ is _Thêlvizon_ in his nursery. The neuter is the gender of a child not yet of age, so the calling name keeps something of the nursery about it for life.

| Ending  | Who                                       | Class            |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------- |
| `-izon` | anyone, by family and the closest friends | class II, neuter |

A calling name is used only downward and inward: by parents, by brothers and sisters, by a husband or a wife, and by friends of long standing and equal rank. A client never uses his patron's, and a junior officer never uses his commander's. A Vylarian who hears his calling name from a stranger hears himself called a child, and answers accordingly. The pages give a person's full name, and his house name alone wherever a short form reads naturally, as a Vylarian of standing is addressed.

**A place** is a root with a place suffix: _-âra_ for a town, _-enna_ for a farm, an estate or the village around one, _-ôra_ for a height or a stronghold. A frozen locative ending in _-âri_ serves as a place name too, "at the place of", and several old towns carry one.

**An office or a body** takes _-ândôs_ or _-âlon_, as the suffix table shows. A law is named by _vôrnon_ (a law) with its subject in the genitive.

## Words

### The hallowed and the gods

Every word in this table that names a god, and a handful that name other things, belongs to the hallowed gender, and the verb at its head is the one that puts them there. _Ulthâthen_ (to hallow) is what a priest does to an altar and what the Senate does to a family it ennobles; _anulthâthen_ undoes it, and the empire has used that verb on more great houses than it likes to remember. A god is _ulthâs_, which is the same word made a noun: the hallowed one.

The vocabulary of the faith is the vocabulary of an institution. The imperial cult, _zalvon_, has a word to itself, distinct from a rite and from a prayer, because it is a department of state with revenues and a seat at public ceremonies. Its high priest, the _hôlzathôs_, is a public officer named by the Senate, and an oath sworn before him binds in the courts as well as before the gods. The oldest words in the table belong to the house: _karâlzon_, the fire that is never let go out, and _karâlzândis_, the household gods who are "those of the kept fire".

### Ancestors, the house and the name

A Vylari house keeps its dead in the hall. Each ancestor who held office has a _lâvûrâ_, a wax mask of his face, in a cupboard beside the door, and on a funeral day the masks are worn by living men of the right build so that the whole line walks behind the bier. A line is an _azênê_, and the word means the row of masks as much as the descent it records.

The household, _zôrkhâ_, is everyone under one roof and one master: wife, children, freedmen, slaves and the clients who come to the door each morning. Its master, the _zôrkhândôs_, answers for all of them at law and holds a power over his children that ends only at his death. So the words for kin are many and exact, and so is the vocabulary of inheritance: an heir is named in a will, a son may be adopted from another house when a line runs short, and _aipsênâ_ (adoption) is as honorable a way to get an heir as any other. The worst thing that can happen to a name has a word of its own: _nêhârlon_, the unnaming, which strikes a person from every roll and forbids anyone to say the name aloud.

### Precedence, patronage and what is owed

No table in this lexicon is longer for its subject, and that is the plainest thing the language says about its speakers. A Vylarian _places_ a stranger before anything else is said—_zîrôthâthen_, to settle his step of rank—and the word for a person whose rank nobody knows, _anzîrôthôs_, is an uneasy one, used of foreigners and of guests who will not give their patron's name.

The patron, _daimêlâ_, is a class I noun that takes masculine agreement, and the grammarians explain the exception by the office: a patron is a lord, whatever his ending. The client, _thelmôs_, attends him at the morning call, the _prâmôlzâ_, and every favor done in either direction leaves an _ôrphênon_, a debt that is real though no court enforces it. The order of what is owed has one word, _yêrpâlon_: the gods, the ancestors, the house, the patron, the city, the empire, in that order and no other.

The words for persons follow the genders. A citizen, _vêrôsâ_, is a feminine noun whether the citizen is a man or a woman, because the word is old and formal. A slave, _thrâkhon_, is neuter, as a tool is. A freedman, _lîskôthôs_, has a gender because manumission gave him one, and _lîskaimâ_ (manumission) is the act that does it.

### Law, the court and the written act

The Vylarian reflex in a quarrel is to sue, and the lexicon is built for it. There are words for the suit and the act of bringing it, for the advocate who carries another's case, the witness who saw, the judgment, the appeal that carries it upward, and the penalty laid at the end. A writing becomes an act when it carries a seal, _thîzâron_, and the keeper of a house's seals is a trusted officer, since a document sealed in his absence is a forgery.

The words for searching out a matter—_sêkhêrâ_ (an inquiry), the inquirer and the body that inquires—belong to law and to the arcane order alike. Exile, _vênthôdis_, is the gravest sentence a court gives a citizen; the unnaming is graver, and only the Senate gives it.

### The state and its offices

The sovereign is _Ôrzâs_, declined as a god is declined, and the word has no sex: an empress is _Ôrzâs_ too. Most of the empire's offices are a verb with _-ândôs_ after it, so their names say what the holder does. A prefect is one set over a thing (_ûsândôs_, from _ûsâthen_), a tribune one who protects (_lûsândôs_), a steward or a provincial agent one who sees to another's property (_kharêzândôs_), a legate one who is sent (_phêlzândôs_). The office itself takes _-âlon_: a prefecture is _ûsâlon_ and the empire is _ôrzâlon_, the whole of what is commanded.

The words these pages use for the senior magistracies—Senate, senator, consul, tribune—translate Vylari titles: the Senate is _kurthêmâ_, a senator _kurthândôs_ and a tribune _lûsândôs_. A consul is _tâvândôs_, from the root of _tâvemêr_ (two), because there are always two of them and the year is named for the pair.

### The legions and the frontier

The army's words are hard, short and numerous, and every one of them names a thing a soldier can point at. A legion is _thrâmezôs_, and its soldiers are _thrâmezândôs_, the sworn: a recruit, _yûnâkhôs_, is not one until he has taken the soldier's oath, the _thrâmezon_, and the oath, not the pay, is what makes desertion a crime against the gods. The eagle, _ôrkhaimâ_, is consecrated, and a legion that loses it is disbanded.

The frontier, _ûthrimâ_, shares its root with _thîrûthrimâ_, the hallowed boundary of a city, and the two words are the empire's whole theory of itself: a line inside which the law runs, held by men sworn to hold it. Outside it lives the _althêrôs_, the outsider, a word that means "one who lives without the law" and is never quite polite.

### The city: stone, water and the street

A Vylari city is _vêrâmis_, the walled place of citizens, and a citizen is _vêrôsâ_: the two words share a root, and the old grammar reads the one as the place of the other. Around the city runs the _thîrûthrimâ_, the hallowed boundary inside which no legion may march under arms.

The words for the city's works are proud and particular: the aqueduct is _ûlmêrpôzêrâ_, a road for water, and it is built like one. The words for its decay are courteous. A building nobody has repaired in two generations is _hûmelthândar_, resting, and a Vylarian walking a visitor past a roofless temple calls it resting with a straight face. The city has no plain word for a ruin inside its own walls, and a visitor who supplies one gives offense.

### Craft, guild and the kept secret

A guild is _khelron_, a body chartered for one craft, and the guilds of a city together are the _khelrâlon_. The word that explains them is _kêlthon_: a kept secret, a technique held as property of the same order as land, inherited by an heir and sued over in court. The grades of a craft are three: the apprentice bound to a bench for seven years, the journeyman who works another's bench for a wage, and the master, _gârtândôs_, who holds a bench of his own, which is to say a share in the secret. Steel, _thalbêzon_, is the most famous secret of all, and its word is never used of the common iron a village smith beats out.

### Coin, trade and the counting-table

The empire's money has three names, each a step down. The gold coin is _Elthêzâs_, hallowed like a god and struck only at the capital with the sovereign's face on it; the silver coin, _ankhrâzôs_, is the coin of account in which prices are reckoned; and the _khezilon_, the wedge, is one eighth of a silver coin, cut from it with a chisel when change is wanted. A gold coin's hallowed ending is the reason an ordinary Vylarian who handles one says its name with a little ceremony.

The moneylender, _phêzûmândôs_, lends at interest, _phêzûmâlon_, and issues paper notes against the treasury's reserves. The merchant, _dêlâmôs_, is respectable and rich and is never quite a gentleman, since a man of standing lives from land.

### Land, the vine and the river

The great river is _Vîlarâs_, and it is hallowed: the only river in the language declined as a god is, because the empire was born beside it, and the land and the people take their name from it. A field is worked by a tenant farmer, _pôlthândôs_, for a share of what it bears, and the vine, the olive and the grain are the three crops whose words appear in the old oaths. The words for weather are few and plain, and the words for land, its boundaries and its standing stones are many, because land is what a family of standing lives on and what its suits are about.

### The sea and the sailing year

The Vylarian Sea has its year, and the year turns on the _aistroi_, the steady northerlies of late summer that carry the grain fleets home. ### The body, life and death

Death has a word, _khôlvâzis_, and the language uses it as seldom as it can. A Vylarian who has died _goes to the ancestors_, _kalmêthâthen_, and takes his place among the masks in the hall. The tombs of a city stand along the roads outside its hallowed boundary, where the dead may lie, and the line of them is the _zêmrôlâra_, the town of the tombs.

The hand, _dêkhazû_, is also the word for the power a master holds over a person, and to be "in the hand" of someone is to be his ward, his slave or his wife.

### Learning, speech and the letters

The words of learning were made for the Senate and the courts first and the schools second. An oration, _dôkhêmon_, is a speech made in public to persuade, and the orator who makes one is the highest kind of educated man the language names. The school, _mârendâlon_, and the University, _zêtmârendâlon_ (the school of every learning), train him; the master licensed to teach is a _mârendândôs_, and a lecturer is a reader, _lôrêzândôs_, because the lecture is a reading aloud from a book with a commentary.

The arcane order borrows the vocabulary of the guilds and the schools, and its grades read as a career: an initiate, an adept licensed to work alone, a master, and a prelate with a seat on the order's council. Magic itself is _thôrdimâ_, the art, and a mage is one who practices it.

## Shared roots

Ancient Vylari is the mother of five tongues, and the scholars of [[place-magnapolis|Magnápolis]] named it for the one of them the empire speaks. The [[lore-aelendnppl|Áelendan]] reject the name; to them the old tongue is their own, and the empire's speech its worn-down coastal cousin.

- [[skill-aelendlng|Áelendi]] split first, carried over the eastern pass into the highlands, and kept the old sounds best of all: it still clicks its stops. It lost the lip sounds, took a great layer of words from [[skill-sinalelng|Sinalë]], and kept the dual and the old wishing mood in its oaths.
- [[skill-elvndrlng|Élavendri]] is the lowland daughter of Áelendi, softened and sung, and it kept the dual.
- Imperial Vylari stayed on the coast, kept the most grammar and lightened its sounds.
- [[skill-provnzlng|Provenzal]] lost its cases, made articles of all three pointing words, and turned its consonants soft between vowels.
- [[skill-tarvenlng|Tarveni]] kept three cases, three genders and the dual, held up by the older mountain speech it settled over.

[[skill-nordmalng|Nordmal]] and [[skill-varokhlng|Varokhi]] are Pelwar tongues too, from the eastern branch that never came west, and they stand outside this family. The table below gives some of the roots all five daughters inherited. Each daughter takes the root from a different case—Provenzal from the accusative, the others from the nominative—and changes it by its own rules, which its own lexicon sets out.

### Retired names

A retired name is one the setting does not write: a name from Earth, or a form the rules refuse. Each stands below with the name written in its place, and no page writes it, however accented. A literature note quotes its work in the work's own words and a poem keeps its own, so a retired name may stand in either, and a `terran_analog` comment names the Earth source on purpose.
