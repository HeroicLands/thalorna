## Overview

Every morning the sun comes up over the coastal mountains and gives the world a day: light to fish by, warmth for the acorns, a dry beach for the canoes. The [[lore-shuqashclt|Shuqash]] hold that nothing is given for nothing, and that the sky keeps count of every day it lends. They call their faith the Sky's Count (_tiyup pilikin_). Its first teaching is that the world runs on credit, and its second is that the debt falls due.

The people who keep the count are the astronomer-priests (_altiyup_), one sworn society across all the towns, whose eldest, the [Sky Elder]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, sits at [[place-kulaywil|Kulaiwil]]. A household's own part is small and constant: a pinch of acorn meal on the fire at dawn, a bead dropped past the kelp line before a crossing, a few seeds at the feathered pole on the hill. These are payments, and the Shell-Bead People make them in the same frame of mind in which they pay a basket maker. The priests' part is the rest of the sky.

## The Sky People

The sky is a country with towns in it, above the one the people live in, and its people keep accounts as the coast does.

**The Sun** (_hiluq_) is an old man who walks the sky every day carrying a torch, and he is the richest and least patient creditor there is. Everything he lights owes him. He collects at the end of each year, and what he collects is lives: the drowned, the fevered, the old who do not see another spring. The priests do not call him cruel. They say he is owed, and that a creditor who is never paid stops lending, which is what the short days of midwinter are.

**The Moon** (_ayul_) is the Sun's sister and the sky's counter. She keeps the tally of every month in her waxing and waning, and on her own nights she watches the women of the towns, who keep the household counts as she keeps the sky's.

**The Morning Star** (_sitiq tahay_) is the people's friend in the sky, a gambler and a joker who rises before the Sun and is still in the sky when the Sun arrives. At the end of every year he plays the Sun for the coming year's lives.

**The fallen stars** are the beads. In the oldest songs the sky once held more stars than it does now, and the stars that fell into the western sea became the olive snails whose shells the islanders drill. A strand of beads is a little of the sky's own wealth, and counting it is a small copy of the work the priests do.

## The Game at Midwinter

At the [Holding of the Sun]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, when the sunrise has walked as far south along the mountains as it goes and seems ready to leave, the Sun and the Morning Star sit down in the sky's town to play the hand game for the year. Each holds two short bones, one plain and one banded, hidden in his fists, and each guesses which hand of the other's holds the plain bone. The Moon holds the counting sticks between them. The game lasts the four nights the priests sing at the sun pole.

If the Morning Star wins, the Sun pays: a year of rain in the winter, fish in the kelp and acorns in the valleys, and the Sun comes back north along the mountains. If the Sun wins, he takes his winnings in the year that follows, a life at a time, from whoever he chooses.

The priests at the pole are not idle spectators. They sing to steady the Morning Star's hands, and their songs are counted as stakes on his side. A priest who knows the game well can tell from the first morning's sunrise how it is going, and from the third whether the towns should lay up acorns. The Sky Elder announces the result at dawn on the fourth morning, and the chiefs of the coast go home with it. A year the Sun has won is a year in which no town launches a canoe in doubtful weather, and the chiefs give more feasts than usual, to keep the town's count square in case the Sun comes to collect.

## The Guardian

Once in a life, at coming of age, a boy or a girl takes the dream drink (_lipush_), brewed from the root of the thorn-apple under an astronomer-priest's eye. They sleep a day and a night, and something comes to them in the dream: a hawk, a bear, a swordfish, a star, the Moon herself. That being is the sleeper's guardian for life. It gives them a song, and sometimes a rule, such as a food never to be eaten, a beach never to be landed on or a word never to be said, and it stands by them in danger for as long as the rule is kept.

The Shell-Bead People speak of a guardian as a partner in an account. It gives help, and it is owed: its song sung, its rule kept, a bead thrown into the sea for it after a narrow escape. A person who breaks the rule has defaulted, and the guardian stops coming. Most people never speak of their guardian at all, except to an astronomer-priest in sickness, because a guardian whose name is known can be bargained with by someone else.

The drink is dangerous. The priests mix it to the weight and age of the one who will drink it, and a cup too strong kills. Nobody drinks it twice except in mortal sickness, when a priest may give a second cup so that the sick person can go and find their guardian and ask it why it has stopped coming.

## The Work of the Priests

The astronomer-priests learn the sky over many years: the rising points of the sun along the mountains, the moon's months, the stars that rise before dawn in each season. Entry costs forty strands, so nearly all of them are chief's kin. Their work is the count, in all its forms.

- **They name the days.** Every launch, marriage and burial waits for a day they have called good, and a canoe master who launches on a bad one pays the priests to sing over his crew when he comes back.
- **They name the children,** on the fourth day after birth, from the sky of the birth night: a being of the sky joined to an act, as Moon Rising or Star Walking.
- **They paint.** At midwinter the priests climb to the caves in the mountains, take the dream drink there, and paint what they see on the rock in red, black and white: wheels, suns, the sky people and the guardians of the priests themselves. The greatest of these is [Tishal Hapuq]{.name data-type="place/site"}, the Painted Cave above [[place-tulishwil|Tulishwil]]. A painting records a vision and a day.
- **They hang the rain stones.** A rain stone is a smooth stone ground to a spindle, wide at the middle and narrowing to both ends, with a hole drilled through one end. In a dry winter a town pays the priests to hang one on a cord in its spring, or in the surf at the river mouth for the fish, and to sing over it. A rain stone belongs to the priests, never to the town that pays for it.
- **They heal.** A sickness is a debt the body has been made to carry. A priest called to the sick sings, sweats them, gives them herbs, and sucks the sickness out of them through a short tube of soapstone. He spits it into his hand, a pebble, a thorn or a knot of hair, and shows it to the family before he names his fee.
- **They judge sorcery.** It is told below.

## The Short Strand

A sorcerer (_alpashaq_) is someone who collects a debt that was never owed. The coast holds that a person with a strong guardian, or with a priest's learning and none of a priest's oath, can send sickness into another person and draw their luck, their health or their life out of them, as a creditor draws beads. The only motive the coast recognizes is the count: envy of a neighbor's catch, a grudge over a bride-price, a debt the sorcerer believes he is owed and could not collect.

A family that believes a sickness was sent asks the astronomer-priests whose it was. The priest takes the dream drink or reads the sky, and names a person or declines to. A person named is brought before the chief and the counter, who hear what the named person is owed by the sick one and what the sick one owes the named person, and the count is settled first. The sorcerer pays back twice what the sickness took, as a thief does. A sorcerer whose sickness killed has run up a life, and pays twenty strands for it, and the town remembers. A second naming is exile.

The priests are the only judges of sorcery, and they are paid for each judgment. Nobody on the coast has ever heard of a priest being named as a sorcerer.

## The Grandmothers' Cup

The dream drink is the priests' by the society's oath, and it is not only theirs in fact. In the [[place-tulishlumuqrgn|Oak Valleys]] the grandmothers of the villages give it to their own grandchildren, as they say their grandmothers did, in a basket by a night fire, without beads and without a priest. A valley child who has drunk the grandmother's cup counts the guardian that came to them as theirs. The coast towns call it a short strand, a guardian bought with no count kept.

This autumn a girl of a valley village above Tulishwil died of a cup too strong, given her by her mother's mother, [[being-ayulkal|Ayulkal]], Moon Arriving, who had given it to thirty children before her. At the [Acorn Gathering]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} the [Sky Elder]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, [[being-tiyupliq|Tiyupliq]], Clear Sky, asked the headmen of the valleys to forbid the grandmothers' cup. [[being-ayullesh|Ayullesh]], Moon Running, the headman of Tulishwil, said that he would forbid it in the year a priest gave the drink to a valley child for the price of a basket. The priests have not lowered their price, and Ayulkal is grinding the root again.
