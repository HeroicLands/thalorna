## Overview

A Spruce People hunter does not say that he killed a caribou. He says that one came to him. The [[lore-kawiskakclt|Kawiskak]] hold that every animal in the forest is a person, with its own country, its own kin and its own mind, and that an animal dies under a hunter's arrow only because it agreed to. They call the animals the Givers (_ostinak_), and the faith of the northern forest is the whole of what a band does to be worth giving to.

There is nothing to it that a band cannot do for itself. There are no priests, no temples and no great rites, only a long list of ways to be courteous to the Givers, kept by everyone, and a few people who can do more: the lodge-shakers, and any old person who can read a shoulder blade. The forest is too wide and the bands too small for anything else.

## The Keepers

Each kind of animal has a keeper, who decides which of its people go to which hunter. The keeper of the caribou lives out past the treeline, in the country where the herds calve and the dead go, and sends the great herds south into the trees each winter or holds them back. The keeper of the beaver lives in a lodge of his own at the bottom of the deepest lake, and the keeper of the fish under the weirs at the summer lakes. The bands name a dozen keepers and argue about more.

None of them is a god, and none is worshipped. They are elders of their own kinds, and like any elders they listen to what the young tell them. Every animal killed goes home in spirit to its keeper and says how it was treated. A keeper who hears that its people were wasted, mocked, or thrown to the dogs keeps them away from that hunter, and in a bad case from his whole band. That is the reason for every rule of the forest: the fish bones back in the water, the beaver bones back in the stream, the caribou bones tied up in a tree, the first meat to the old people and the widows.

## Dreams

The Givers speak to a hunter in his sleep. A hunter who dreams of a moose standing in a certain bend of a certain river goes to that bend in the morning, and if the moose is there, it was offered. A hunter who dreams nothing for a month goes out anyway, and is careful.

Some people have a helper who comes in their dreams again and again: a wolf, a loon, the north wind, a dead grandfather. A helper warns of thin ice and bad weather, shows where the game is, and teaches songs, and the person it helps sings its song before a hunt and feeds it a little fat thrown into the fire. Nobody names their helper aloud, because a helper named is a helper shared, and some never tell anyone they have one.

## The Shoulder Blade

When a band does not know which way to move, someone reads a shoulder blade. Anyone may do it, and the old do it best. The reader takes the flat shoulder blade of a caribou or a hare, cleans it, asks it the band's question aloud, and holds it over the coals until it cracks and blackens. Then they read it like a map of the band's ground. A long crack is a river or a trail, a black spot is a lake or a herd, a crack that turns back on itself is a way that leads nowhere, and a bone that burns through means the question was asked badly.

The trail-breaker listens and decides. A band that has read a shoulder blade still moves where its trail-breaker leads, and a trail-breaker who keeps going the other way from the bones and keeps finding meat is a trail-breaker the band follows anyway.

## The Bear

The bear is the one animal the Spruce People speak to before they kill it. A hunter who finds a bear asleep in its winter den stands at the mouth and talks to it, calls it grandfather, tells it that the band is hungry and asks it to come out, and the bear that wakes and comes out has agreed. After the kill the band feasts it in a lodge with the bear's own head in the place of honor, painted, with tobacco in front of it, and no dog gets a scrap of it. When the feast is done the skull is painted again and tied high in a spruce, facing the way the band will move, so that it can watch the forest for its people.

## The Shaking Lodge

When a band needs to know what no one can see, a lodge-shaker builds a shaking lodge (_tisoman_), a narrow tower of poles wrapped in hides, as tall as two people, and goes into it alone at dusk while the band sits outside in the dark. The lodge begins to sway. Voices come out of it, many of them, high and low, in the speech of animals and in the speech of people, and the band asks them its questions: where the caribou are, why a child is sick, whether a band three lakes away is alive.

The first voice in the lodge is always the Loon, who speaks every tongue and carries what the others say. Behind the Loon come the keepers of the animals, the lodge-shaker's helpers, the north wind, and sometimes the dead, who can be asked what they want. A lodge-shaker who has the Loon's favor can send words to another lodge, built on the same night by another lodge-shaker far off, and get an answer before morning. Some bands use it to arrange a meeting at the summer lakes.

A person becomes a lodge-shaker by dreaming it, usually more than once, usually after a hard winter. The band pays in tobacco, a pelt or a share of the next kill. Every band keeps the rule that the [Loon Lake Killing]{.name data-type="lore/history"} left: no voice in a shaking lodge can name a person _mohkisk_ on its own, and the whole lake must hear the case in summer.

## The Ice in the Heart

The starving spirit (_mohkisk_) is cold. It takes hold of a person in a famine winter as ice takes hold of a lake, from the edges in. A person it is taking stops eating what there is, and talks of nothing but meat, and looks at the children of the band in a way the band notices. Their heart is turning to ice. When it has turned all the way, they will eat human flesh, and nothing that the band can do afterward will bring them back.

Before then, something can. The band builds up the fire, melts the best fat it has, bear grease if there is any, and makes the person drink it hot, as much as they can hold, while the old people sing over them and hold them down if they fight. The fat melts the ice from the inside. A person who vomits up a lump of ice, or of something that looks like ice, has been brought back, and goes on as before, and nobody speaks of it. A person the fat does not warm is beyond the band's help, and in the old stories that is when the band kills them.

The bands do not agree how often the fat has worked. The old people of the north say they have seen it work. The lake keeper of the Loon Lake, who tells the story of the Killing there from beginning to end, says that the strangers on the ice were never offered it.

## The Dancing Dead

The dead walk north, past the treeline into the country of the caribou keeper, and live among the herds without hunger. On a clear winter night they dance, and the dancing is the northern lights. Children are told not to whistle at them, because the dead may come down to dance closer, and nobody laughs at the lights. A band that sees them dancing red takes it to mean that someone of the band has died far off, and waits for the news at the summer lake.

## The Question at Loon Lake

The family of [Barren-Ground People]{.name data-type="lore/culture"} that came starving to the [Apowosipa]{.name data-type="place/feature"}, the Loon Lake, last winter is still there. The lake keeper, [[being-osipisa|Osipisa]], Watches the Lake, fed them from her own stores, and half the bands that meet there have not forgiven her. Now the lodge-shaker of the Loon band, [Yahkinisa]{.name data-type="being/npc"}, Watches the North Wind, has proposed a middle way: the strangers should be given the hot fat, in front of the whole lake at the summer gathering, so that everyone can see whether there is ice in them.

Osipisa has refused. The fat is for one of the people whose heart is turning, she says, and giving it to the strangers is saying that they are _mohkisk_ without a hearing, which is the thing the rule forbids. Yahkinisa answers that the fat never harmed anyone, and that a lake that will not look is a lake that is afraid of what it might see. The strangers have been asked nothing, and they understand little of what is said about them.
