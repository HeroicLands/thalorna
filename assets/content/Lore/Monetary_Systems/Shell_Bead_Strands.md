On the beach at [Pitashwil]{.name data-type="place/settlement"} nobody counts beads. The seller of a cake of tar takes the buyer's strand, winds it once around her own closed hand, and watches the two ends meet over her knuckles. If they meet, the strand is full and the tar is his. If they fall short, she hands it back, and he adds loose beads from his pouch until they do.

The [[affiliation-shuqash|Shuqash]], the Shell-Bead People of the [[place-shuqhalaqrgn|Bead Coast]], keep the only true money in the north of [[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik]]: small disks of shell, drilled and strung. They call themselves after the bead (_shuq_), and every price on the coast is counted in strands (_hamil_). The beads are money, wealth and, in the astronomer-priests' teaching, pieces of the sky, because the stars that fell into the western sea became the small olive-colored snails the islanders drill.

**Scope.** Strands are the money of the coast towns and the island towns, and of the [[place-tulishlumuqrgn|Oak Valleys]] behind them, which sell the coast its acorns for beads. The [[affiliation-kelwanuk|Cedar People]] and the [[affiliation-teyumi|Turquoise People]] take beads in trade and as gifts and spend them in their own ways. Elsewhere in the north shell is a trade good, weighed and strung but not counted as money, and the north's great wealth is [[lore-nrthcopprcrncy|copper]]. South of the desert the money is [[lore-cacaojadecrncy|cacao and jade]], and the south has no name for the Shell-Bead People.

## The Bead and the Strand

A bead (_shuq_) is a disk of shell ground smooth and drilled through the middle, smaller than a fingernail. Most are made at [[place-shuqwil|Shuqwil]] on Bead Island, where the men dive for the snails in the kelp of the cove and the women and children break the shells, grind the disks on sandstone, drill them with stone points on pump drills and string them. A good driller makes about one strand a day, and a household with three drillers lives well. The strings are plant-fiber cord, and tar from the shore seeps fixes the knots.

A strand (_hamil_) is a cord of beads long enough to reach once around a grown hand, a little shorter than a forearm. It is the unit of every price, and the beads in it are not counted one by one: a strand is full or it is short. Loose beads make up the difference and pay for small things, a fish or a handful of seeds.

Each town's counter (_alpilik_) keeps a measuring cord of a full strand's length. At every midwinter, at the [Holding of the Sun]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"}, the counters bring their cords to [[place-kulaywil|Kulaywil]] to be checked against the [Measure of Kulaywil]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, the cord of exact length the astronomer-priests keep. A cord found short is cut and retied in front of the gathered chiefs, and every debt in every town is counted in those days.

The best strands come from Shuqwil. Its counter, [[being-ayulchim|Ayulchim]], cuts and restrings any strand that is short before it leaves her doorway, so mainland traders pay a little more for a Shuqwil strand, and a full-length strand anywhere on the coast is called "an Ayulchim strand".

A theft is paid back twice over. A killing is paid to the dead person's kin at twenty strands, and once it is paid the kin may not take revenge. Entry into the society of astronomer-priests costs a fortune in beads, which is why most of them are chiefs' children.

## The Chief's Count

A town's wealth sits in the chief's storehouse beside the chief's house: its beads, its dried fish and its acorns, and the counter knows what is in it to the bead. Every canoe that lands on the town's beach pays the chief a bead in ten of its cargo, every household brings the chief a share of its catch and its acorns, and every fine passes through the chief's hands. From that store the chief (_hilaq_) feeds the poor, gives the feasts, pays what the town owes other towns and ransoms the town's people from raiders.

Beads are meant to move. A rich man who sits on his strands is mocked as a strand asleep, and the man the town admires is the one who feasts, lends and pays the debts of a family that has fallen behind. A debt (_pashaq_) a man cannot pay sends him into his creditor's house as a bond-worker (_tutuq_) until the count is square. A debt denied is worse than a theft, because the debtor has broken the count itself.

## The Crossing Strand

Every child is given a first bead by its mother's mother, and begins a crossing strand (_hipalshuq_) with it. It is the fare for the canoe of the dead from [[place-hipalhalaq|Hipal Halaq]] to the Far Island. A person adds to it all their life and never spends, lends or pledges it, and no creditor may seize it, not even from a bond-worker. Stealing one is punished as a killing. A chief owes a crossing strand to anyone of the town who dies without one, and at the grave the strand is laid on the body and buried with it, after the counter has read out the dead person's count and the creditors have been paid from what the dead left.

At the [Feast of Strands]{.name data-type="lore/gathering"} a family that fears its dead had too short a strand pays the astronomer-priests to sing the dead person's name. The priests take a strand for every name sung, and throw a strand into the sea at the Crossing Shore for every name they were paid to send. A man going out past the kelp line in a canoe drops one bead into the water to pay the sea for the crossing.

## Beads beyond the Coast

The coast spends beads over the passes in the Oak Valleys, for acorns, seeds, deer meat and baskets, and the valley people take them home and spend them back.

The Cedar People's trading canoes come south each autumn for beads, and their war canoes come in summer for slaves. Every spring the chiefs of the northern coast towns send two canoes of beads up the coast to [[place-lamekis|Lamekis]], the [Northern Strand]{.name data-type="lore/custom"}, and the Bear House there gives them away at its feasts, a strand to each guest in the order of seats. The Cedar war canoes pass the coast towns by. Three times a year the canoe master [[being-sitiqnush|Sitiqnush]] takes a cargo of beads north into the Cedar People's waters and buys back captives, at whatever price is asked.

The Turquoise People send traders over the mountains each summer for beads and shells, and the few beads that cross the desert pass through their hands first.

East of the mountains shell is not money. The Dawn People drill white and purple beads on the [[place-tsiorhenrgn|Dawn Shore]], and the [[affiliation-osetakona|Maple People]]'s rememberers string them into memory belts that hold the Grove's agreements. A belt is a record and a gift. Shell from the east and south is weighed at [[place-hanalxilan2|Ha'nal Shi'lan]] with copper and grain, and nobody there measures a strand around a hand.

## Practical Notes

### Short Strands

The coast holds that good is a true count and evil the short strand, and the short strand is the commonest cheat: a strand strung a few beads short, or eked out with beads too thin, badly drilled or ground from the wrong shell. A good bead and a bad one feel different under the thumb, and the blind elders who sort beads by touch are the coast's surest testers. A strand found short in a bargain is restrung on the spot, and a man found short twice is named at the next feast by the chief's herald.

### Carrying Strands

A strand weighs a few ounces and coils into a palm. A canoe's price, a hundred strands, fits in one tight basket. Beads do not rot or rust, and a strand lost overboard is the only kind that is lost for good. When raiders' canoes are sighted from the headlands, the people of the towns take their beads and run for the hills.
