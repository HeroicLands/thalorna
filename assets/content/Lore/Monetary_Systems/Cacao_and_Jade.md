On a market day in the great plaza of [[place-tsamash|Tsa'mash]], a woman selling turkeys takes her price in cacao. She counts the beans into her palm by twenties, rattles each handful beside her ear, and drops one bean back into the buyer's hand without a word. It is light. A light bean is an empty husk with earth packed inside it, and the buyer must find another before the bargain stands.

[[place-kiichchik|Ki'ich'chik]] strikes no coin. Its money is what it grows, weaves and carves: **cacao beans** counted by the twenty, **cotton cloth** by the piece and **jade** by weight. All three are tribute as well as money. A household that pays its lord's tribute steward in cacao pays him in the same beans it buys its salt with, and the cloth a ward owes its Sun Lord is the cloth a merchant takes for a jar of honey.

**Scope.** Cacao and jade are the money of the heartland realms, [[affiliation-ixilankul|Ish'ilankul]], [[affiliation-kankul|K'ankul]], [[affiliation-neokbaate|Ne'ok Ba'ate]] and [[affiliation-xelem|Shelem]], and of the southern market towns, [[place-nepaxel|Ne' P'ashel]] above all. The southern realms run differently. Their kin groups are fed from the storehouses and pay their lords in labor, and a farmer of [[affiliation-ixbaltzaku|Ish'bal Tsa'ku]] can go a season without handling a bean; there, cacao, jade and metal move through the market towns and the lords' tribute, not through a farmer's hands. North of the desert the money is different again: the [[lore-shellbeadcrncy|shell-bead strands]] of the western coast, and [[lore-nrthcopprcrncy|copper]] by weight and by gift.

### Cacao

The bean is the small money of every heartland market. It grows in the southern highlands and in the rainforests of [[place-xelemrgn|Shelem]], whose beans are the largest and fetch the most. Market folk count it by twenties, the way the calendar counts days: twenty beans to a _ts'ob_, twenty twenties to a _pel'ush_ that fills a cloth bag, and twenty bags to a _q'umpel_ of eight thousand, which fills a sack. A porter carries three sacks.

Cacao is also a drink. _Ch'ishal_, the bitter bean drink, is ground from the same beans that pay for it, so a lord who pours it for his guests is pouring out money before them, and the cups at a Sun Lord's feast are counted in sacks. The bean does not keep. After a few wet seasons it molds, so a household spends its beans and saves its cloth.

### Cloth

The tribute cloth (_p'ilul_) is a length of plain white cotton of a set size, measured by the tribute steward against his own cord. Every ward member owes his Sun Lord tribute in maize, cloth and cacao, and the stewards (_Ch'imelbal_) keep the count of what each subject town owes in cloth, maize, cacao and feathers, and send it on at the fixed days. A plain piece passes for about a hundred beans, a finely woven one for up to three hundred. Cloth keeps where beans rot, and a family that does well lays it by in a chest.

Cloth dyed with cochineal and woven with a Ts'enmah's seals is not money. It is a luxury that only the nobles of tributary states can pay for.

### Jade

Jade (_ha'nakel_), the green stone, is the money of lords. It passes by weight, but its color and cutting count for as much: a deep, clear green outweighs a cloudy one many times over, and a carved bead or ear-spool of K'ankul work is worth more than the stone in it. A Sun Lord's treasury holds jade, cloth and feathers, and the gifts by which one city acknowledges another are counted in jade. A K'ankul scribe's knowledge is reckoned worth its weight in jade. Only the quetzal feather is worth more.

Gold and silver pass by weight as jade does. The smiths of [[place-nekiahkul|Ne' Ki'ahkul]] work them into ceremonial objects and jewelry, never into money, and the iron and steel of the southern forges are blades, not payment.

## The Market and the Levy

The great market plazas of [[place-kultaqan|Kul'taq'an]] and Tsa'mash meet on a rotating twenty-day cycle kept in step with the calendar, so that each market day falls on the same day-sign as the last. The lesser towns hold their markets on the days the great plazas do not.

The [[affiliation-itzanpnthn|Itsani priesthood]] levies a religious tribute on all trade, one part in twenty, and its assessors count it at the edge of the plaza before anything is carried home. In the south no great bargain is struck without a priest watching it. At Ne' P'ashel the priestly assessors stand over the canoes of cacao, feathers and medicines as they are unloaded, and the temples take their share before the merchants take theirs.

The merchants answer to their own lords. In Ish'ilankul the Lord of traders (_Ch'eshmah_) sends the guild's caravans south, judges its members in the guild's own court and brings the Ts'enmah word of what his traders saw abroad. Tribute and its tallies travel by the [[miscgear-kotselcord|k'otsel cords]], and a valley's whole count by a [[miscgear-kotselledger|K'otsel Ledger]].

## Practical Notes

### False Beans

The commonest cheat in a heartland market is the false bean: a husk emptied of its meat and packed with earth, wax or clay, then dried until it looks whole. A small or moldy bean slipped into a count is the lesser cheat. Market folk weigh beans in the hand, rattle them beside the ear and press a doubtful one with a thumbnail, and a seller who counts quickly is watched.

A counterfeiter caught in the market goes before a judge (_Ts'ekulbal_) in the commoners' court, or before the Lord of traders' court if he is of the guild. He pays twenty good beans for every false one, and one who cannot pay is sold for the debt, as any debtor is.

### Weighing Jade

Jade is weighed on a beam against the weight-stones the temples keep, smooth basalt stones that carry a priest's mark. The priestly assessors weigh any jade of consequence before the levy is taken from it, and a trader who brings his own stones to a bargain is asked whose they are. Green serpentine and stained stone pass as jade to a careless buyer. A lapidary knows true jade by its weight, its coldness and the way it takes a polish.

### Carrying Money

Cacao is heavy for what it buys. A sack of eight thousand beans weighs about 8 kilograms (18 pounds) and is worth some two hundred Ardin, and it spoils if it gets wet. Cloth is bulkier still. Jade is the only Ki'ich'chik money that carries a fortune in a closed hand, which is why a merchant bound south on the rivers carries cacao for the road and jade for the bargain.
