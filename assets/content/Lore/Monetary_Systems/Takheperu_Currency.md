**Scope:** The Ta'Kheperu currency is the standard currency of the **Northern Fertile Region of Xerathia** — the empire of [[affiliation-empirtkhpr|Ta'Kheperu]] itself, the [[affiliation-mtrrchybth|Matriarchy of Bethûa]], and the kingdom of [[affiliation-okharis|Okháris]]. Outside the Northern Fertile Region it is exchangeable as foreign currency, principally against the [[lore-vylrncrncy|Vylarian system]] through moneylenders at the trading ports of the Vylarian Sea.

The system is **weight-based rather than coined**. Where the Vylarian Aerarium Imperii strikes named coins (Aurion, Argentus, Octus) bearing the Augustar's image and the imperial Aquila, the Kheperi temples attest **standardized weight-pieces** of copper, silver, and gold — small ingots, rings, or stamped wedges of fixed mass, marked with the issuing temple's seal certifying their weight and metal purity. The pieces are not called coins. They are _attested metal of known weight_, and they are accepted at face value in commerce throughout the Northern Fertile Region without need for re-weighing.

The system is administered by the **[[affiliation-perhati|Pér-Háti]]** — the temple-treasury institution of the Kemetian priesthood — which maintains the reference weights against which any piece can be checked, holds the great granaries that anchor the empire's grain-based credit system, and issues the **temple chits** that serve as the Northern Fertile Region's equivalent of paper script. [[affiliation-mtrrchybth|Bethûa]] and [[affiliation-okharis|Okháris]] do not maintain native monetary systems; they use the Kheperi system through commercial institutions (the [[affiliation-bthntrdhss|matriarchal trade-houses]] of Bethûa, the [[affiliation-crwntrdskhrs|crown-chartered trades]] of Okháris) whose credit is ultimately backed by correspondent relationships with specific Ta'Kheperan temples.

## The Three Metals and Two Units

| Metal      | Unit         | Mass   | Conversion                                                        |
| ---------- | ------------ | ------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Gold**   | Deben        | ~91 g  | 1 gold deben = 10 gold kite                                       |
| **Gold**   | Kite (Qedét) | ~9.1 g | 1 gold kite = 1/10 gold deben                                     |
| **Silver** | Deben        | ~91 g  | 1 silver deben = 10 silver kite; 1 gold deben = 10 silver deben   |
| **Silver** | Kite (Qedét) | ~9.1 g | 1 silver kite = 1/10 silver deben                                 |
| **Copper** | Deben        | ~91 g  | 1 copper deben = 10 copper kite; 1 silver deben = 10 copper deben |
| **Copper** | Kite (Qedét) | ~9.1 g | 1 copper kite = 1/10 copper deben                                 |

The three metals interconvert at fixed temple-attested ratios — **1 gold deben = 10 silver deben = 100 copper deben** — which is to say that a piece of gold of any given weight is worth ten times a piece of silver of the same weight, which is worth ten times a piece of copper of the same weight. These ratios are reaffirmed annually by the Per-Aá's seal at the New Year's festival and are inviolable across the empire. Where rare market conditions cause the ratios to drift in informal trade, the temples re-attest by edict, and the ratios snap back to canonical.

The Kheperi people speak in _deben_ and _kite_ with the metal specified by context: "the field cost twelve gold deben" or "the loaf was three copper kite." The unit _qedét_ is the formal liturgical form of _kite_; everyday speech uses kite, formal documents and temple records use qedét. The distinction parallels the Vylarian distinction between Argentus (formal) and Argo (colloquial).

## The Standardized Pieces

The temples produce, attest, and circulate pieces of metal in standardized weights. The pieces are small ingots, rings, or wedge-stamped fragments — never round coins (the round-coin form is a Vylarian and Helionite convention that the Kheperi consider foreign). Each piece bears the issuing temple's seal, an indication of metal and weight, and (on the larger pieces) the regnal cypher of the Per-Aá in whose reign it was attested.

| Denomination       | Mass  | Argo-equivalent value | Typical use                                                                               |
| ------------------ | ----- | --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Copper Kite piece  | ~9 g  | 0.1 Argo (~1 Bit)     | Daily small purchases — bread, beer, market goods                                         |
| Copper Deben piece | ~91 g | 1 Argo                | Everyday medium purchases — meals, lodging, common tools                                  |
| Silver Kite piece  | ~9 g  | 0.2 Argo (~2 Bits)    | Wages, common commerce                                                                    |
| Silver Deben piece | ~91 g | 2 Argo                | Significant purchases — weapons, tack, fine cloth                                         |
| Gold Kite piece    | ~9 g  | 2 Argo                | Same value as silver deben but lighter; used for ceremonial gifts and high-status payment |
| Gold Deben piece   | ~91 g | 20 Argo               | Large purchases, ceremonial offerings, temple tributes                                    |

A trader's purse normally contains a mix sized to his transaction needs. Cheap purchases (bread, beer, market goods) are paid in copper kite or copper deben; medium purchases in silver kite or silver deben; large purchases call for gold or temple chits. The metal a trader chooses for a given payment is itself a social signal — paying in gold deben for a small purchase is conspicuous and ostentatious; paying in copper for a large purchase is insulting. The cultural expectation is that the metal match the transaction's scale and dignity.

For "making change," the temples and most market stalls maintain small balances against which pieces can be weighed in case of dispute, but the standardized pieces normally pass at face value without verification. A piece of unknown provenance — hack-silver from foreign trade, irregular pieces from minor uncertified mints, recovered pieces from old hoards — is weighed and assayed at receipt and accepted at intrinsic value, often at a small discount for the friction.

## Conversion with Vylarian Currency

The conversion ratio between the Vylarian and Kheperi systems is fixed by long convention:

| Vylarian | Ta'Kheperan                                              |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 Bit    | 0.625 silver kite (or ≈ 1 copper kite + small remainder) |
| 1 Argo   | 5 silver kite (= ½ silver deben)                         |
| 1 Aurion | 80 silver deben (= 8 gold deben)                         |

Working the other way:

| Ta'Kheperan    | Vylarian               |
| -------------- | ---------------------- |
| 1 copper kite  | 0.1 Argo (~1 Bit)      |
| 1 copper deben | 1 Argo                 |
| 1 silver kite  | 0.2 Argo (~2 Bits)     |
| 1 silver deben | 2 Argo                 |
| 1 gold kite    | 2 Argo                 |
| 1 gold deben   | 20 Argo (= 1/8 Aurion) |

A SoHL item priced at _X_ Argo costs **X × 0.5 silver deben** (or equivalent in any other metal). A 6 Argo dagger is 3 silver deben; a 240 Argo Mail Shirt is 120 silver deben (or 12 gold deben, or any equivalent combination). The conversion is mechanical: no SoHL item's value field needs to change for a Ta'Kheperan campaign.

The Vylarian Argo trades within the Northern Fertile Region at a small discount against its theoretical silver-content value — typically 3–5% — because of the friction of accepting foreign coin in a system that prefers temple-attested local pieces. Conversely, a Vylarian moneylender at a frontier port honors Ta'Kheperan pieces at intrinsic silver-content value minus a similar 3–5% spread. The spread is the basic income of the **changers** — the specialist moneylenders who operate at the conversion boundaries.

## Grain Credit and the Temple Granaries

In addition to the metal-piece system, Ta'Kheperu maintains a parallel **grain-credit economy** administered through the temple granaries. Every nome's principal temples hold great granaries — vast clay-walled silos of wheat, barley, and other staples — and serve as the empire's grain banks. Workers' wages, soldiers' rations, and tax payments often pass through the granaries as **khar of grain** (one khar ≈ 76 liters, roughly a quarter of a year's grain ration for an adult).

The granaries maintain ledger accounts for individuals, families, businesses, and noble houses; a depositor brings grain at harvest and receives credit in his account, drawing against it through the year for daily rations or for conversion to silver-kite payment when needed. The exchange rate of grain to silver fluctuates seasonally — grain is cheap at harvest, expensive in the late dry season before the next flood — but is publicly posted at every major granary and is generally honored across the empire on the day of transaction.

For the Northern Fertile Region as a whole, grain-credit accounts are _Ta'Kheperan_ — held at Ta'Kheperan temples and convertible only through Kheperi granaries. Bethûan and Okhárian merchants doing substantial business with Ta'Kheperu often maintain grain accounts at temples in cities they trade with regularly; this is a practical commercial arrangement, not a religious one, and the temple priesthood is comfortable serving foreign clients as long as the relationship is correct.

For game purposes, grain credit is unlikely to be a routine player concern — characters carrying physical coin and using temple chits will handle most transactions. Grain credit becomes relevant for substantial commercial play (a player merchant character) or for stories involving the granary system (famines, granary robberies, harvest-festival politics).

## Temple Chits

For transactions too large to carry as metal — multi-deben deals, cross-territory remittance, large credit extensions — the Northern Fertile Region uses **temple chits**, issued by the [[affiliation-perhati|Pér-Háti]] and honored at any Pér-Háti chapter across Ta'Kheperu (and, by correspondent arrangement, at any associated Bethûan trade-house or Okhárian crown-trade office).

A chit is a small clay-sealed clay tablet (for small chits) or a sealed papyrus letter (for larger ones), bearing the issuing temple's seal, the value (in silver or gold deben), the recipient's name or "to bearer," and the temple's registered serial mark. Functionally similar to a Vylarian traveler's note. Theologically different: the chit is an act of the temple, and the small fee the temple charges (typically 2–3% on issuance, comparable to the Aerarium's 2%) is framed as a votive offering rather than as a commercial commission.

The chit system extends across the Northern Fertile Region through correspondent arrangements between specific Ta'Kheperan temples and specific commercial institutions in Bethûa and Okháris. A Bethûan matriarchal trade-house holding a correspondent account with the great temple at Wasetkara can honor Pér-Háti chits drawn on that temple, and can issue its own local chits backed by its Wasetkara account. The arrangement is private and varies by relationship; the trade-houses with the strongest Ta'Kheperan correspondent networks become the principal banking institutions in Bethûan commercial life, despite holding no formal "banking" charter.

For travelers, the practical rule is: large sums move on temple chits; small sums move as physical metal pieces; the conversion happens at the nearest Pér-Háti chapter, correspondent trade-house, or correspondent crown-trade office.

## Practical Notes

### The Cultural Significance of Metal

The choice of metal in a Kheperi payment carries social meaning that has no equivalent in the Vylarian coin system. Copper is the metal of ordinary commerce, daily life, and small transactions — paying in copper is unremarkable. Silver is the metal of significant transactions and respectful dealing — paying in silver dignifies the recipient and acknowledges the transaction's importance. Gold is the metal of high commerce, ceremonial gift-giving, temple offering, and noble-to-noble exchange — paying in gold for a small purchase is conspicuous (and possibly insulting, suggesting that the recipient cannot make change), while paying in copper for a large purchase is contemptuous.

A Kheperi merchant negotiating a substantial deal will often discuss the _metal_ of the payment as well as the _amount_ — "twelve silver deben paid in gold kite" is a different transaction from "twelve silver deben paid in copper deben pieces," even though the metal-content value is identical. The first signals respect and continuing relationship; the second signals reluctance, low expectation, and possibly final settlement of a strained partnership.

### Hack-Metal and Unattested Pieces

Pieces of metal of unknown provenance — hack-silver, hack-gold, irregular pieces from foreign mints, recovered pieces from temple hoards — circulate alongside the temple-attested standard pieces but at a discount. The receiving merchant or moneylender weighs the unattested metal at his own balance, assays it for purity, and accepts it at intrinsic value minus a friction fee (typically 5–10%, depending on how foreign the metal looks). Some changers and frontier moneylenders specialize in handling hack-metal; the better ones can read regional smithing styles and identify the metal's likely origin within minutes.

### Counterfeit and Debasement

Counterfeiting an attested temple piece is a sacrilegious crime under Kheperi religious law and an ordinary crime under Bethûan and Okhárian civil law. Penalties are severe — the Kheperi convict counterfeiters of attested pieces are ritually executed at the New Year's festival. The temples maintain assay specialists who can detect debased metal in a piece by weight, sound, and (for the most experienced) by visual inspection of the cut edge. Large counterfeit operations are typically detected within months and prosecuted vigorously; small individual counterfeiters operate longer in marginal regions but rarely produce pieces convincing enough to pass repeated assay.

### Carrying Weight

Like the Vylarian system, large sums of physical metal are heavy. 100 silver deben weighs about 9 kg (20 lb); 1,000 silver deben weighs 91 kg, impractical for a single traveler. Wealth above the carryable threshold moves on temple chits or trade-house correspondent arrangements. A Kheperi merchant's purse in serious commerce typically contains: a handful of gold pieces (kite or deben) for emergency liquidity, perhaps 20–50 silver kite for ordinary transactions, a small bag of copper for daily small purchases, and a sealed temple chit for the bulk of his working capital.
