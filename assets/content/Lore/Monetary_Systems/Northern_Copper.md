At [[place-hanalxilan2|Ha'nal Shi'lan]] the Weigher sets a copper blade in one pan of his balance and maize in the other, a cupped handful at a time, while a Maple People canoeman and a One Fire trader watch the beam. When it hangs level, the Weigher says the count aloud. Neither side disputes it, because the town chose him for exactly that.

Copper is the north's wealth, but it is not the north's money. The only true money north of the desert is the [[lore-shellbeadcrncy|shell-bead strand]] of the western coast. Nobody counts copper into a hand, and nothing in the north is priced in it the way the Bead Coast prices in strands. Copper is what a chief gives away, what a killer's kin pay, what a speaker wears while he speaks and what a family lays in the grave. Where it is weighed and traded, it comes nearest to money.

**Scope.** Copper is wealth among every people of the north: the [[affiliation-osetakona|Maple People]], who dig it; the [[affiliation-tokchiaska|One Fire]], who buy it; the [[affiliation-kelwanuk|Cedar People]], who beat it into coppers and break them; and the [[lore-kawiskakclt|Spruce People]] and the barren-ground bands beyond them, who carry the far north's copper south. South of the desert copper is one metal among several, cast as well as hammered, and the money there is [[lore-cacaojadecrncy|cacao and jade]].

## Where It Comes From

Nobody in the north smelts ore. The copper comes out of the ground pure, and a smith beats it cold.

Most of it is dug on the shores of the [Copper Lake]{.name data-type="place/feature"} in the [[place-kahnionrgn|Western Lakes]], and on the long island out in the lake, by the Copper People of the Maple People's Grove. Each summer the men of [[place-kahnekwaroten|Hammerstone]] cross to the island and dig the old pits along its ridges. They light fires on the rock and throw cold water on it to crack it, pry the copper out with stones and wooden wedges, and leave tobacco at each pit before they dig. The Copper People hold that the copper is the shed scales of the [Great Sturgeon]{.name data-type="lore/deity"} under the lake.

At Hammerstone a smith (_tsikenhahen_) sets a lump on a flat anvil stone and beats it with a round hammerstone held in both hands, folding and beating it again until it is a sheet, a bar or the rough shape of a blade, then grinds and polishes it with sand and water. The fire only softens copper that has grown hard under the hammer. A good smith makes an awl in a morning, a knife in a day and a speaker's throat plate in a month.

A little copper comes from the barren grounds of the far north. The barren-ground bands bring it to the treeline and trade it to the Spruce People's forest bands for spruce gum, birch bark and wood for spear shafts, and from there it goes south from hand to hand. The trade at the treeline opens every year with a gift of food from the Spruce People to the barren-ground bands, before anything is traded.

A cast bell is proof of a southern smith, because no one in the north casts. The Turquoise People's cloud dancers wear them at the knee, and a few reach the One Fire's Suns, who prize them above anything made at home.

## Copper as Payment

Copper settles what the north will not settle with a club.

- **Among the Maple People**, a wrong between nations or between clans is sweetened (_siwate_): the side at fault sends furs, beads, copper and maple sugar until the clan mothers of the other side accept it. A killing within the Grove is paid with ten belts' worth of furs, beads and copper for a man and fifteen for a woman.
- **Among the One Fire**, the Mound Chief sets the payment owed to an injured clan in maize, copper or labor. A chief rewards a deed with a copper ornament or an engraved gorget, a household troubled by its dead pays a Keeper in maize or copper to speak at the temple fire, and wagers on the rolling-stone game run up to a copper plate.
- **On the Cedar coast**, a killing is answered with a payment from the killer's house to the dead person's, in blankets and coppers. A chief who takes a name or raises a pole gives away or destroys a fortune in blankets, canoes, grease and copper while his Keeper of the Count says the count aloud, and a chief who breaks a copper, cutting off a piece and giving it to a rival, obliges the rival to break one of equal worth. The Bear House of Lamekis sells its captives for blankets, grease and copper.

Copper also goes into the ground. At the Maple People's Feast of Bones each clan lays its dead in the great pit with copper, beads and furs, so that no longhouse holds much copper for long. The One Fire's Suns are buried with their embossed plates. Robbing a grave of its copper is robbing the dead, and it brings the town's warriors down on whoever did it.

## Weighing at Ha'nal Shi'lan

The trading town of Ha'nal Shi'lan stands between the Maple People and the One Fire, and its [[affiliation-hanalxilanmktcncl|market council]] keeps the peace of the bargain. Maple People canoes come down the rivers with copper, furs, maple sugar and dried fish, and One Fire traders come up from the south with shell, engraved copper plates and fine pottery. At every bargain the Weigher, a townsman both sides trust, weighs the copper, the shell and the grain, and his count is not disputed. The trading families store the goods, broker the bargains and take a share. Copper weighed at Ha'nal Shi'lan is the closest thing to money between the two nations, and traders of both nations reckon a bargain in copper by weight even when it is paid in robes.

## The Quarrel over Copper

The copper goes south because the One Fire pays for it, and the One Fire's warriors carry copper-headed clubs into the valleys of the River People. The River People's speakers have asked the Grove's great council three times to stop the trade, and three times the Copper People have refused to agree. A council of the Grove decides nothing until every nation agrees, so the matter is an unboiled kettle and the copper still goes south. Hammerstone's speaker, [[being-tsikenhakahtiron|Tsikenhakahtiron]], says that copper is the Copper People's maize.

Every northern chief also knows what copper is not. The south fights in steel, and a cold-hammered copper blade does not stand against it. That difference is the plainest reason the [[affiliation-redstncmpct|Red Stone Compact]] exists.

## Practical Notes

### Judging Copper

Lake copper is pure, soft and bright, and a smith knows good metal by how it moves under the hammer. A blade that has been folded badly splits along the fold, and a sheet beaten too thin tears at the edge. The value of worked copper is in the work: a plate a smith spent a month on is worth many times its weight in lumps, and a named copper of the Cedar coast is worth whatever its last giving made it.

### Carrying Copper

Copper is heavy, and the north moves it by canoe. A pound of lake copper is a fair-sized lump, a Cedar copper weighs about six pounds, and a canoe-load from Hammerstone is the wealth of a village. Over the portages it goes on the backs of the paddlers, a few pounds each.
